\figtext{%
\figpara{How to read the table.}{One row per set of people used over the eleven
iterations. \textbf{Train} is how many people the model studied and
\textbf{Test} how many it was graded on, always different people.
\textbf{Records/person} says how long a typical day is in that set. The last
column is the score of the one-line rule ``repeat the last antenna'' on that
set's test people, and it is the number every result in the corresponding
chapter is compared against.}

\figpara{Why the last column moves so much.}{It is a property of the people, not
of the rule. Nothing about ``repeat the last antenna'' changes between 69.2 and
43.1\,\%; what changes is who is being scored. A file taken as it comes is full
of people who barely move, and repetition wins easily on them. Once only people
who genuinely move around are kept, the same rule loses a quarter of its score.
This is the single reason why an accuracy from one chapter must never be
compared with an accuracy from another, and why every result in this report is a
distance to the rule on the matching line of this table.}

\figpara{Why there are six sets and not one.}{Each new idea needed data the
earlier files did not carry: keeping the times required regenerating the
sequences, testing whether more people help required more people, and testing
whether many more people help required stacking eleven files. Every
regeneration drew a new test group, and therefore moved the rule.}}

\vspace{10pt}
\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\footnotesize
\textbf{Number of records behind each row.} \sraw: about 31{,}000 records over
the 500 people. \smob: 34{,}408 records to train on and 8{,}725 to test on.
\slev: 80{,}000 and 20{,}000, which is $2.33\times$ more, all of it copies.
\stwok: 69{,}379 records at 900 people or 146{,}713 at 1{,}900, and 7{,}845 test
records giving 7{,}745 predictions. \seleven: 369{,}451 records at the
5{,}000-person size rising to 1{,}538{,}379 at 21{,}000, and 14{,}705 test
predictions.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Why \smob{} carries two values for the rule.} 45.5\,\% is measured on the
last fifth of each test person's day only; 43.1\,\% is measured on every position
of it, one at a time, after the evaluation was widened in the sixth iteration.
Scoring every position is strictly harder, which is why the rule scores less.
Every \smob{} result in this report is compared with the value that matches its
own protocol.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Training and test people are always different people}, inside every row,
so nothing the model is graded on was ever trained on. Each row also draws its
test people afresh, which is why absolute scores from two rows are not
comparable and only the distance to each row's own rule is.
\end{minipage}
