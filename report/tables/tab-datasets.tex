\figtext{%
\figpara{How to read the table.}{One row per set of people used over the eleven
iterations. \textbf{Train} is how many people the model studied and \textbf{Test}
how many it was graded on, always different people.
\textbf{Records/person} says how long a typical day is in that set. The last
column is the score of the one-line rule on that set's test people, and it is the
number every result in the corresponding chapter is compared against.}

\figpara{Why the last column moves so much.}{It is a property of the people, not
of the rule. Nothing about ``repeat the last antenna'' changes between 69.2 and
43.1\,\%; what changes is who is being scored. A file taken as it comes is full
of people who barely move, and repetition wins easily on them; once only people
who genuinely move are kept, the same rule loses a quarter of its score. This is
the single reason an accuracy from one chapter must never be compared with an
accuracy from another. Six sets exist rather than one because each new idea
needed data the earlier files did not carry, and every regeneration drew a new
test group. \smob{} carries two values because 45.5\,\% is measured on the last
fifth of each test person's day and 43.1\,\% on every position of it, one at a
time, after the evaluation was widened in the sixth iteration; scoring every
position is strictly harder.}}
