\figtext{%
\figpara{The question, and how it is tested.}{Between two training sizes the score
moves a little. Is that the model genuinely improving, or the luck of which
thousand people happened to be in the test set? A thousand people are drawn at
random from the thousand held-out ones, with replacement; both models are scored
on exactly that draw and the difference noted; that is repeated two thousand
times, and the middle 95\,\% of the differences is the range in the third column.
Because both models always see the same draw, the luck of the sample cancels.}

\figpara{What that licenses me to say.}{If a range contains zero, ``no difference
at all'' is a perfectly ordinary outcome and the two sizes cannot be told apart.
Every row contains zero, including the last, which spans the whole ladder from
5{,}000 to 21{,}000 people and whose best estimate is 0.04. So the claim is not
``the gain is small'' but \emph{no gain was measured}. The fitted scaling law
agrees: a slope of $+0.18\pt$ per ten-fold increase in users, on a line accounting
for only 12\,\% of the spread of the eight points.}}
