\figtext{%
\figpara{Who is in this table.}{These are \textbf{the seven worst-scoring people
of the whole test set} on \sfilt, and nobody else: every person there whose score
is at or below 12.5\,\%. They were pulled out precisely because a single bad
number hides several completely different problems, and only one of the three is
about the model.}

\figpara{The columns.}{\texttt{user\_id} is the anonymous number the operator
assigned to that person for that day. \textbf{Moments} is how many graded moments
they contribute, so how much material there is to judge anything on.
\textbf{Ceiling} is the best score reachable at all given the antennas that
appear in that person's own history: if an antenna never appeared before, no
model could have named it. \textbf{Rule} and \textbf{Model} are the one-line rule
and my model on the same moments.}

\figpara{Situation A, four people: there is nothing to measure.}{Between two and
twelve graded moments each. A person with three moments scores 0, 33, 67 or
100\,\%, and nothing in between, so their number says almost nothing about a
model.}

\figpara{Situation B, one person: a genuine miss.}{The ceiling is 89\,\%, so the
behaviour was learnable, and neither the rule nor the model followed it. This is
the only row of the table that is a straightforward modelling failure.}

\figpara{Situation C, two people: beaten by the trivial rule, and the row that
mattered.}{The ceiling is 100\,\%, plain repetition already scores 50 to 67\,\%,
and my model scores nothing at all. These two people bounce between near-twin
antennas of the \emph{same} mast, \cid{UKVCHE102} and \cid{UKVCHE104} for
instance. The model tries to choose between the two names and keeps choosing
wrong, so it is being marked down for failing a distinction that involves no
movement. This is the observation that led to measuring mobility on the mast
while keeping the full antenna name as what has to be predicted.}}
