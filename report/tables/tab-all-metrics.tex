\figtext{%
\figpara{Why six rows and not one.}{A flat headline can hide movement underneath.
\textbf{Top-1} is the strict headline; \textbf{top-3} and \textbf{top-5} ask
whether the right antenna is anywhere in the first three or five. \textbf{Cold
start} gives the model only 30\,\% of each person's day. \textbf{The middle
person} ignores the busiest people entirely, and \textbf{the worst tenth} guards
against a model that lifts its average by abandoning the people it already served
badly.}

\figpara{The verdict is the last column.}{Across four training sizes covering a
four-fold increase in people, no row moves by more than 1.3 correct answers per
hundred. One row is worth isolating: on a cold start the model beats its rule by
4.6 against 4.3 at 80\,\% of the day, so its edge is \emph{largest} on people
about whom little is known, which is the useful case, and that edge too is
identical at 5{,}000 and at 21{,}000 people. The 1{,}900 column, marked
$^{\dagger}$, is measured on \stwok{} against a 48.2\,\% rule: a reference point,
not a fifth size, and the \textbf{Spread} column covers the four \seleven{} sizes
only.}}
