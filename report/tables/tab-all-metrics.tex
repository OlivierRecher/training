\figtext{%
\figpara{Why six rows and not one.}{A flat headline can hide movement underneath.
Perhaps more people help the model rank better without changing its first guess,
or help the people it already served well while abandoning the rest. These six
rows are that objection, tested.}

\figpara{What each row measures.}{\textbf{Top-1} is the strict headline: the
right antenna in first place. \textbf{Top-3} and \textbf{top-5} ask whether it is
anywhere in the first three or five. \textbf{Cold start} gives the model only
30\,\% of each person's day instead of 80\,\%, which is the operationally
interesting case. \textbf{The middle person} is the score of the person sitting
exactly halfway down the ranked list, which ignores the busiest people entirely.
\textbf{The worst tenth} is a guard against a model that lifts the average by
abandoning the people it already served badly.}

\figpara{The verdict is in the last column.}{Across four training sizes covering
a four-fold increase in people, no row moves by more than 1.3 correct answers per
hundred, and most move by less than half of that. Depth of ranking is flat. The
hard tenth is flat. Cold start is flat. Nothing hides behind the average.}

\figpara{The one row worth isolating.}{On a cold start the model beats its rule
by 4.6 against 4.3 at 80\,\% of the day, so its edge is \emph{largest} on people
about whom little is known. That is the useful case. But that edge, too, is
identical at 5{,}000 and at 21{,}000 people. All four \seleven{} columns are
scored on the same 1{,}000 held-out people.}}

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\footnotesize
$^{\dagger}$ The 1{,}900 column is measured on \stwok{} rather than on \seleven,
which is a ten times smaller test set with a 48.2\,\% rule instead of 50.2\,\%.
It is a reference point and not a fifth size, the \textbf{Spread} column covers
the four \seleven{} sizes only, and the two blank cells are simply measurements
that were never computed on it.
\end{minipage}
