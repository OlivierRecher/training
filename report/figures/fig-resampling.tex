\caption{Stretching every day to 200 records: a high score, a worse result}
\label{fig:resampling}
\figtext{%
\figpara{What the technique does.}{Every person's day is stretched to exactly 200
records by repeating records that are already there, spread evenly along the day.
The aim is legitimate: without it, the longest quarter of people carries 44\,\% of
the training material, so a fine-tune spends most of its budget on the heaviest
users.}

\figpara{Panel (a): a spectacular score, and a result that changed sign.}{Each
run is shown against \emph{its own} one-line rule, and only the comparison inside
a pair means anything. The levelled model reaches 74.9\,\%, twenty-eight above
the natural-length run and by a wide margin the highest absolute score in the
whole project. But the rule on those same stretched days reaches 76.5\,\%, so the
distance flips from 3.7 ahead to 1.6 behind. The score went up and the result got
worse.}

\figpara{Panel (b): the reason is arithmetic, not modelling.}{Of the 4{,}000
graded moments, 100 people times the last 40 of their 200 records, \textbf{2{,}262
ask for a record that is a copy of the one before}. Any rule that repeats is right
on all of them by construction, so both the model and the rule are handed those
answers for free, which is why both rose by about thirty. On the 1{,}738 moments
that are real decisions the rule scores 45.9\,\% against the model's 42.2\,\%.}

\figpara{Why it could not have worked here.}{A copied step never changes location,
by construction. So levelling compresses the spread between people in how often
they move by a factor of 2.3 and drags everybody towards the stay-at-home profile.
That is fatal for this particular pipeline, because telling the four kinds of user
apart is the mechanism that produced the project's only real gain, and it needs
that spread to work. The technique does deliver what it promises, the
longest-to-shortest ratio falling from 200:19 to 1:1 and per-person weight becoming
perfectly equal, but on this data it removes more information than it
redistributes.}

\figpara{One honest note on the drawing.}{The model bar in panel (b) is derived
from the reported 74.9\,\% rather than re-measured. It assumes the model never
misses a copy, and it rises to 44.8\,\% if it misses 2\,\% of them. Both ends
stay below the rule.}}
