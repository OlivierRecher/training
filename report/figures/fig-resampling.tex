\caption{Stretching every day to 200 records: a high score, a worse result}
\label{fig:resampling}
\figtext{%
\figpara{Panel (a): a spectacular score, and a result that changed sign.}{Every
person's day is stretched to exactly 200 records by repeating records already
there, so that the longest quarter of people stops carrying 44\,\% of the
training material. Each run is shown against \emph{its own} one-line rule, and
only the comparison inside a pair means anything. The levelled model reaches
74.9\,\%, by a wide margin the highest absolute score of the project, but the
rule on those same stretched days reaches 76.5\,\%. The distance flips from 3.7
ahead to 1.6 behind: the score went up and the result got worse.}

\figpara{Panel (b): the reason is arithmetic, not modelling.}{Of the 4{,}000
graded moments, \textbf{2{,}262 ask for a record that is a copy of the one
before}. Any rule that repeats is right on all of them by construction, which is
why both bars rose by about thirty. On the 1{,}738 moments that are real
decisions the rule scores 45.9\,\% against the model's 42.2\,\%. The model bar
here is derived from the reported 74.9\,\% rather than re-measured, assuming it
never misses a copy; it rises to 44.8\,\% if it misses 2\,\% of them, and stays
below the rule at either end.}

\figpara{Why it could not have worked here.}{A copied step never changes
location, so levelling compresses the spread between people in how often they
move by a factor of 2.3 and drags everybody towards the stay-at-home profile.
Telling the four kinds of user apart is the mechanism that produced the
project's only real gain, and it needs that spread. The technique delivers
exactly what it promises, per-person weight becoming perfectly equal, but on
this data it removes more information than it redistributes.}}
