\caption{What a pair of back-to-back records actually is}
\label{fig:transition-composition}
\figtext{%
\figpara{What is being counted.}{Take every pair of back-to-back records in a
file, and ask what separates the two records of the pair. Each bar above is
100\,\% of one such file's pairs, cut three ways. Dark blue: the two records
carry the very same antenna name, so nothing at all happened. Teal: the antenna
name changed but the mast did not, so the phone hopped to another antenna of
the same installation while the person stayed where they were. Orange: the mast
changed too, so the person genuinely went somewhere.}

\figpara{The top bar, an ordinary day.}{Out of a hundred pairs, 43.6 are pure
repetition and only 44.0 are a real movement. The remaining 12.4 are the middle
band, and they are the trap: they look like movement in the raw data and are
not.}

\figpara{The bottom bar, once only mobile people are kept.}{Real movement rises
from 44 to 51 out of a hundred, which is the whole point of that filtering. The
middle band, however, stays at 12, which tells us something useful: that band is
a property of the radio network, not of the people. Filtering the population
cannot remove it.}

\figpara{What follows from it.}{Counting the middle band as mobility would
inflate every movement figure in this report by about a quarter, and would blur
the four kinds of user of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, whose whole purpose is to
separate people who move from people who do not. So mobility is always measured
on the mast. The prediction target deliberately stays the full antenna name, and
the price of that, namely that the model is marked wrong for a change that is
not a movement, is accepted openly rather than hidden (Chapter~\ref{ch:user}).}}

\end{figure}
