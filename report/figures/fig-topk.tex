\caption{What top-1, top-3 and top-5 mean, and how a score is obtained}
\label{fig:topk}
\figtext{%
\figpara{The model does not give an answer, it gives an order.}{Asked what comes
next, it does not name one antenna. It assigns a degree of confidence to all 369
antennas of the area and sorts them, most likely first. Panel (a) shows the top
of that sorted list for one moment: it is 52\,\% sure of \cid{UKVBER101},
23\,\% sure of \cid{UKVTGM104}, and so on.}

\figpara{The three depths are three ways of reading the same list.}{\textbf{Top-1}
asks whether the right answer is in first place. \textbf{Top-3} asks whether it
is anywhere in the first three. \textbf{Top-5}, anywhere in the first five. In
the example the right answer sits third, so the same prediction is a failure at
top-1 and a success at top-3 and top-5. Nothing about the model changes between
the three numbers, only how strict the question is.}

\figpara{Why report three of them rather than one.}{Because they answer different
practical questions, and because reporting several depths is the convention of
this literature rather than a choice of mine \cite{luca2021survey,qin2025mobllm}.
An operator that can prepare one antenna in advance cares about top-1. One that
can prepare three cares about top-3, and for it a model that reliably puts the
right antenna somewhere in its first three is useful even if it rarely puts it
first. Top-1 is the strict headline used throughout this report; the other two
say whether a model that misses is missing narrowly or wildly.}

\figpara{Panel (b): where a percentage comes from.}{The score is a plain count
and nothing more. Every graded moment of every test person is answered, marked
right or wrong at the chosen depth, and the score is the number of right answers
divided by the number of moments. Ten moments, seven right, 70\,\%. The one-line
rule answers the very same moments and gets its own count, and every result in
this report is the difference between the two counts.}}
