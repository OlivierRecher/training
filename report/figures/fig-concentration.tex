\caption{What actually predicts how well a person is scored}
\label{fig:concentration}
\figtext{%
\figpara{What each bar is.}{One row per candidate property of a person, named on
the left in plain words. The length of the bar is the Pearson correlation of
Equation~\eqref{eq:pearson} between that property and that person's own score. A
bar of length zero means the property tells you nothing about the score. A bar
reaching $\pm1$ means it tells you everything. The side it points to gives the
direction: to the left means ``more of this, worse predicted''.}

\figpara{Two sets of people, two colours.}{Dark blue bars are measured on the
1{,}173 scored people of a raw day file, teal bars on the 100 held-out people of
a different set. A row with only one bar is a property that was computed on one
of the two only.}

\figpara{The ranking is not close.}{How often somebody changes antenna reaches
$-0.971$ on the first set and is confirmed at $-0.773$ on the second. How spread
out their time is ($-0.811$) and how much of it sits on one main antenna
($+0.696$) are the same information seen from other angles. Everything else,
including how much history the person has, stays below $0.24$ in absolute value,
which eliminates the intuitive explanations in a single pass.}

\figpara{And a warning about the top bar.}{The $-0.971$ is close to arithmetic,
for the reason given in the text: how often somebody changes antenna is the
mirror image of how often the one-line rule is right on them, so any model
imitating that rule is guaranteed a large negative value before anything is
measured. It says as much about the run as about the people. The distribution of
Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}, not this coefficient, is the finding of the
chapter.}}

\end{figure}
