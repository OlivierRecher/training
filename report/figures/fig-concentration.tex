\caption{Concentration of the evaluation signal and per-user correlations}
\label{fig:concentration}
\figtext{%
(a)~The 1{,}173 evaluated users, sorted by how many predictions each
contributes and cut into five equal groups, the axis labels giving the range
of each; grey-blue is the persistence baseline and dark blue the model, both
scored on the same positions. The evaluation signal is extremely concentrated:
the median user contributes 11 predictions, while the most active tenth, 117
people, carries 58.6\,\% of the 54{,}173 predictions, so any average on this
corpus is essentially the score of those 117 people. Accuracy nevertheless
barely moves across the groups, and the raw correlation with volume is weak
($r = +0.15$), so this is not a volume effect: heavy users simply happen to
switch cell less often ($r = -0.15$). (b)~One bar per candidate feature, its
length being the Pearson correlation between that feature and the user's own
accuracy; zero means the feature says nothing, $\pm1$ that it says everything.
Dark blue bars are measured on the 1{,}173-user set and teal bars on the 100
held-out users of a different dataset; features with a single bar were
computed on one dataset only. The ranking is not close. The cell-switch rate
reaches $r = -0.971$ and is confirmed at $-0.773$ on the second corpus, while
normalised entropy ($-0.811$) and dominant-cell frequency ($+0.696$) are the
same information seen from other angles and everything else stays below
$0.24$. Section~\ref{sec:retro-user} argues that the $-0.971$ is close to a
tautology and that the distribution, not the correlation, is the real finding.
Group sizes in panel~(a), left to right: 324, 234, 234, 234 and 117 users,
carrying 353, 948, 2{,}601, 7{,}553 and 42{,}718 predictions.}
