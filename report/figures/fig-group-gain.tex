\figtext{%
\figpara{The result is the ordering, and it is exact.}{One pair of bars per kind
of user, plus the plain average of the four on the right. Grey-blue is the
one-line rule, dark blue my model, both on exactly the same moments of the same
100 held-out people, over every position of their day. The gain ranks in the
precise reverse order of the rule, with no exception among the four. The
stay-at-home user, whom repetition already predicts 56.0\,\% of the time, gains
0.8 and has almost nothing left to win; the morning-moving user, whom repetition
predicts less than one time in five, gains 6.4. In between, the steady user
gains 2.3 and the evening-moving user 5.4. Over four points a correlation
coefficient would add nothing to that statement, so none is quoted.}

\figpara{Why this is the report's central picture, and what tempers it.}{Nothing
separates this run from the one that scored 1.8 \emph{behind} the rule except
which numbers were allowed to answer (Figure~\ref{fig:adapter-routing}). Read
against Chapter~\ref{ch:user} it is that chapter's finding from the other
direction: difficulty lives in the person, and a model earns its cost precisely
where the trivial rule fails. Two qualifications belong with the headline. The
3.7 is the plain average over four kinds counted once each; weighted by how many
people each kind holds it is 3.3, and that is the figure an operator would
experience, because 28\,\% of the population sits in the kind that needed no
model at all. And the mechanism depends on the kind of user being identifiable,
which Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty}(b) shows collapsing below roughly 60\,\%
of a day of history.}}
