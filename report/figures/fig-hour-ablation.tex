\caption{Does the model actually read the hour?}
\label{fig:hour-ablation}
\figtext{%
\figpara{Panels (a) and (b): breaking the hour, and only the hour.}{Each event
enters the sequence as an hour symbol followed by an antenna name, and mistakes
in two chosen stretches of the day were made to count more, twice as much at 04h
and 05h and four times as much at 18h and 19h. Panel (b) then re-grades the same
trained model on the same people three more times, never touching the list of
antennas: the hours are shuffled, so they are real hours attached to the wrong
events; or all replaced by one value, which deletes the information; or all moved
forward by twelve, so the ordering survives but every label is wrong.}

\figpara{Panel (c): the argument is the gap between two bars.}{Deleting the hour
costs 0.7 correct answers per hundred. Merely shuffling it costs 3.0, more than
four times as much. If the hour symbol were just occupying a slot, destroying its
\emph{ordering} could not cost more than deleting it altogether, so the model has
genuinely learned which hour goes with which antenna.}

\figpara{Panel (d): the informative failure.}{Moving every hour forward by twelve
changes the overall score by only $+0.5$, which looks like indifference and is
not: inside the two upweighted stretches the score falls from 42.7 to 39.7 while
outside them it rises from 45.3 to 47.1. The two halves of the day exchanged
their difficulty along with their labels. \smob, 80\,\% of each day given as
history, 1{,}747 graded moments.}}
