\caption{The group as a token, and the group as a routing decision}
\label{fig:adapter-routing}
\figtext{%
The change that turned the project's main idea from a failure into its best
result, and it is plumbing rather than modelling. (a)~In the first attempt the
behavioural group was written into the input as one extra symbol, \cid{<G0>}
to \cid{<G3>}, at the head of a sequence of up to five hundred tokens.
Attention is free to look at it, and it did not: feeding the correct group, a
deliberately wrong group, or no group at all gave 41.3, 41.2 and 41.1\,\%,
three numbers inside two tenths of a point of one another. Ignoring the token
cost the training loss almost nothing, so the optimiser ignored it. (b)~In the
second attempt the group is not information but a switch. Four LoRA adapters
are trained on the same frozen base, one per group, and the group detected
from a user's past decides which one runs, so there is nothing left to dilute.
On the same users, the same protocol and the same architecture, the gap to the
persistence baseline moves from $-1.8\pt$ to $+3.7\pt$ (Figure~\ref{fig:group-gain}). Four adapters are also four times the trainable capacity, and the
control separating the two effects was not run (Section~\ref{sec:retro-groups}).}
