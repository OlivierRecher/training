\figtext{%
\figpara{What is on each axis.}{Eight training runs differing only in how many
people they were trained on. The horizontal axis is that number on a logarithmic
scale, so equal distances are equal multiples. The vertical axis is \emph{not} a
score: it is the distance between the model and the one-line rule on that run's
own test people, the only quantity that survives a change of test set. The dashed
line at zero means ``indistinguishable from repeating the last antenna''.}

\figpara{The middle band holds everything, and two things constrain how far it
reads.}{At 400 people the model gains 0.6, which is to say nothing. Between 400
and 1{,}900 the gain climbs 0.6, 2.8, 3.5, 4.2, essentially the entire benefit
this project ever obtained from data volume. The ten-fold increase that follows
buys 0.1, and the best size, 15{,}000, is followed by the worst, 21{,}000: the
curve does not slow down, it stops. Two caveats. The points do not come from the
same test people, which is why the axis is a distance and the small step across
that boundary is not a measurement. And every size received the same budget of
training time, so at 21{,}000 people only 36\,\% of the material was ever read:
the defensible claim is that 1{,}900 people are enough \emph{at this budget}.}}
