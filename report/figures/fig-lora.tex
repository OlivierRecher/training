\caption{What LoRA and QLoRA do}
\label{fig:lora}
\figtext{%
A language model is a stack of large weight matrices, and adapting one can be
done at three very different costs. (a)~The obvious way is to update every
number in them, which means holding the weights, a gradient for each of them
and two optimiser statistics for each of them in GPU memory at once, several
times the size of the model. (b)~LoRA \cite{hu2022lora} leaves the big matrix
untouched and adds two thin matrices beside it, whose product has the same
shape; only those are trained. ``Low-rank'' means exactly that, a large change
approximated by the product of two narrow matrices, and here it amounts to
about 2\,\% of the parameters. (c)~QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora}, the regime
used throughout this report, additionally stores the frozen matrix at 4 bits
per number rather than 16, which quarters what the frozen part occupies; the
adapters themselves are still trained at full precision. The consequence is
the one that mattered in practice: a training run fits in half an hour on a
free cloud GPU, so eleven weeks buy thirty experiments rather than two.}
