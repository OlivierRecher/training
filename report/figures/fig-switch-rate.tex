\caption{The score follows how often the person changes antenna}
\label{fig:switch-rate}
\figtext{%
\figpara{How the groups were made.}{In each panel the people are sorted by how
often they change antenna, from the calmest to the most restless, and the sorted
list is then cut into equal groups: five on the left, four on the right. So
restlessness increases from left to right in both panels. The two panels label
their horizontal axis differently: on the left the label gives the range of
switch rates the group actually covers, on the right it simply names the quarter.
The vertical axis is the number of correct answers per hundred graded moments
for that group.}

\figpara{The two bars in each pair.}{Grey-blue is the one-line rule, dark blue is
my model, and both are scored on exactly the same moments of the same people. The
number written above each pair is the distance between them.}

\figpara{Panel (a): a spread of 65, produced by nobody's decision.}{The fifth of
people who never change antenna are predicted at 100\,\% by both, because for
them the task is to notice that nothing is happening. The most restless fifth
plateaus at 34.4\,\%. That is 65 correct answers per hundred separating the two
ends of the same population, on one single trained model, with no setting
involved anywhere. Whatever else is true, this is far larger than anything the
modelling in this report ever moved.}

\figpara{Panel (a), second reading: where the model earns anything.}{In the three
calmest groups the two bars are the same height. The model only pulls ahead in
the two hardest groups, by 3.7 and then by 7.8. But those two groups hold 18.5\,\%
of all the graded moments between them, and the most restless one alone holds
5.6\,\%, so winning eight answers per hundred there moves the overall average by
less than half an answer per hundred. Real contribution, invisible average.}

\figpara{Panel (b): the same ordering, and less comfortable.}{On a second,
independent set of people the model \emph{loses} to the rule in the three calmer
quarters, by 7.1, 5.3 and 3.4, and only draws level in the most restless one, by
0.6. The ordering is identical and the mechanism is the same; the overall result
is negative. This is exactly how a model can be genuinely useful on hard people
and still be worse than one line of code on average. The rule is recomputed for
each person, on the same moments the model is scored on.}}
