\figtext{%
\figpara{Reading the cell-switch quintile panels of Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}a and Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}b.}{People are sorted by how often they change
antenna, from those who move least to those who move most, and the sorted list is
cut into equal groups, five on the left in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}a and four on the right in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}b; movement increases
left to right in both. Grey-blue is the one-line rule, dark blue fine-tuned model, both
scored on exactly the same moments of the same people, and the number above each
pair is the distance between them.}

\figpara{Performance spread in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}a and performance degradation in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}b.}{The fifth
of people who never change antenna are predicted at 100\,\% by both in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}a; the fifth that
moves most plateaus at 34.4\,\%. That is 65 correct answers per hundred between the
two ends of one population, on one trained model. The two bars are the same height
in the three groups that move least in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}a: the model only pulls ahead in the two hardest,
by 3.7 and 7.8, and those hold 18.5\,\% of the graded moments. On a second,
independent set of people in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}b, the model \emph{loses} to the rule in the three quarters
that move less, by 7.1, 5.3 and 3.4, and only draws level in the one that moves
most. Same ordering, same mechanism, negative result overall.}}
