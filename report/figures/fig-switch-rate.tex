\caption{The score follows how often the person changes antenna}
\label{fig:switch-rate}
\figtext{%
\figpara{How to read either panel.}{People are sorted by how often they change
antenna, calmest to most restless, and the sorted list is cut into equal groups,
five on the left and four on the right; restlessness increases left to right in
both. The left panel labels each group by the range of switch rates it covers,
the right one simply names the quarter. Grey-blue is the one-line rule, dark blue
my model, both scored on exactly the same moments of the same people, and the
number above each pair is the distance between them.}

\figpara{Panel (a): a spread of 65, and where the model earns anything.}{The
fifth of people who never change antenna are predicted at 100\,\% by both,
because for them the task is to notice that nothing is happening; the most
restless fifth plateaus at 34.4\,\%. That is 65 correct answers per hundred
between the two ends of one population, on one trained model, with no setting
involved. The two bars are the same height in the three calmest groups: the model
only pulls ahead in the two hardest, by 3.7 and 7.8. Those two hold 18.5\,\% of
the graded moments and the most restless one alone 5.6\,\%, so eight answers per
hundred there move the overall average by less than half an answer.}

\figpara{Panel (b): the same ordering, and less comfortable.}{On a second,
independent set of people the model \emph{loses} to the rule in the three calmer
quarters, by 7.1, 5.3 and 3.4, and only draws level in the most restless one, by
0.6. Same ordering, same mechanism, negative result overall. That is how a model
can be genuinely useful on hard people and still be worse than one line of code
on average.}}
