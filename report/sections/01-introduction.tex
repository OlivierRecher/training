\section{Where the work took place}

This report covers a second-year engineering internship carried out from
29 June to 28 August 2026 at the \host, under the supervision of \supervisor.
The host laboratory holds a corpus of anonymised mobile-network signalling
records\footnote{\textbf{Signalling record}: a row written by the operator each time a
handset and a base station exchange a message. It is produced by the network,
not by the user, so an untouched phone still generates them: a record is not a
movement.} donated by a Czech operator, and hosts a small group of interns
analysing it from different angles.

\section{The corpus, and the team around it}

The material is described in detail in Chapter~\ref{ch:data}; in one sentence, it
is fifteen daily exports covering 12 to 26 March 2014 for the Karlovy Vary study
area, holding between 80{,}217 and 99{,}504 users per day, where each row records
one person's whole day as a list of (cell, timestamp) pairs. The area is served
by 369 distinct radio \textbf{cells}\footnote{\textbf{Cell}: the area covered by one antenna pointing in one direction.
\textbf{Base station} (or \emph{site}, \emph{mast}): the installation
carrying several such antennas, typically three or four. A phone can therefore
change cell without the person moving.} mounted on 113 physical base stations.

Four colleagues were working on that same corpus when I arrived, with different
instruments, and the division of labour is worth stating because it determines
what is mine and what is not (Figure~\ref{fig:team-context}). Nino was pursuing
his own descriptive analysis of the corpus, on questions of his own: the
classification of home and activity cells, and the characterisation of users who
are mobile and present in the recording area across the day. He had built
tooling for that analysis, and he very kindly used it to produce the per-day
extracts and the user selections I asked for, which was a considerable help and
without which most of the experiments reported here would not have been
practical. Tiphain and Benjamin were pursuing the
prediction side of it by \emph{prompting}\footnote{\textbf{Prompting}: getting an answer from a model by writing an
instruction for it, leaving its internal numbers untouched. \textbf{Fine-tuning}, taken here, instead lets the data change them. Figure~\ref{fig:prompting} is one such instruction.} a frozen language model, that is, by writing
better instructions rather than changing any weights. Reda was working on the
same corpus alongside them.

\section{The question I was given}

My branch was the other one: \emph{can a large
language model,\footnote{\textbf{LLM}, \emph{large language model}: a neural
network trained to predict the next symbol of a sequence. Nothing in that
definition is specific to language, which is why one can be pointed at a
trajectory.} fine-tuned on
these traces, predict a user's next \cid{cell\_id}\footnote{\cid{cell\_id}: the
identifier of one radio cell, e.g.\ \cid{BSOCHO1}. It is a structured string
rather than an opaque token, and Figure~\ref{fig:cellid-anatomy} takes it
apart.}?} Fine-tuning means the
model's weights\footnote{\textbf{Weights}: the hundreds of millions of numbers inside the model that
turn an input into an output. ``Frozen'' means left exactly as its original
authors trained them.} are actually modified by the data, as opposed to prompting, where they
are frozen and only the instructions change. The two approaches answer
different questions and neither supersedes the other.

Why a language model at all, for a problem with no language in it? Because the
shapes match. A trajectory is an ordered sequence of symbols drawn from a small
closed vocabulary,\footnote{\textbf{Vocabulary}: the fixed set of symbols a
language model can read and produce. For a general model that is tens of
thousands of word fragments; here 369 cell identifiers are \emph{added} to it,
so that each cell becomes one symbol of its own.} 369 cell identifiers, and
next-symbol prediction over a sequence is precisely what these models are built
to do. Adapting one to this task is mostly a matter of teaching it that a
\cid{cell\_id} is a symbol rather than a string of characters, which
Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} describes.

What makes the question genuinely hard is not the model. It is the competitor.
The rule \emph{``predict that the next cell is the last one seen''}, one line of
code, no parameters, no training, is right 69.2\,\% of the time on a raw day of
this corpus (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}). That rule is called the
\textbf{persistence baseline} throughout this report, and beating it is the only
result that means anything. For five of the eleven iterations, nothing I
trained managed to.

Figure~\ref{fig:worked-example} is the whole task on two positions of one day,
and it is worth looking at before anything else, because it shows where the
competitor cannot be beaten and where it can. When a user stays where they are,
repeating the last cell is correct and both predictors score the point for
nothing. When a user leaves, the rule necessarily fails, and the answer has to
come from somewhere else. Every point reported in this document is won on
positions of that second kind.

\section{The spine of this report}

Two ideas hold the whole document together, and stating them here means the
reader can check every later claim against them.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Every result is a gap to the persistence baseline, measured on
        the same data.} The corpus changed four times over the internship, each
        time because a new approach needed data the previous files did not
        carry, and the difficulty of the test users changed with it. An absolute
        accuracy therefore describes the population it was measured on more than
        it describes the model; only the distance to the matching baseline
        survives.
  \item \textbf{The score is set by the user; the model moves the residual.}
        Per-user accuracy on this task varies from 0\,\% to 100\,\%, and what
        explains it is how repetitive that person's mobility already is. The
        model's genuine contribution is concentrated in the volatile minority,
        which is also the minority that owns the smallest share of the
        evaluation volume. This is the finding that reorganised the second half
        of the internship, and it is why results here are reported per segment
        rather than as a single number.
\end{enumerate}

Read against those two ideas, the outcome of the nine weeks is a modest but
solid one. A fine-tuned model clears the persistence baseline by \textbf{+4.3
points}\footnote{\textbf{Point} means \emph{percentage point} throughout: an
accuracy of 54.5\,\% against a baseline of 50.2\,\% is $+4.3$ points, not
$+4.3$ percent. Every headline number in this report is a difference of two
accuracies.} of top-1
accuracy\footnote{\textbf{Top-1 accuracy}: the share of predictions whose
highest-ranked candidate is the correct \cid{cell\_id}. \textbf{Top-3} and
\textbf{top-5} ask whether it is among the three or five highest-ranked: the
same predictions read at three depths.} on the largest corpus, and $+4.2$ points
on a second, independent held-out test set.\footnote{\textbf{Held-out test set}:
users set aside before training and never shown to the model, on which the
reported score is measured. Here train and test users are always disjoint
people, so the model is graded only on strangers.} The data needed for that is
bounded: the edge grows from $+0.6$ to $+4.2$ points between 400 and 1{,}900
training users, and a further ten-fold increase buys $+0.1\pt$ and no measurable
gain. And the difficulty is not where I expected it to be, 22.4 points separate
the behavioural profiles of the population against four tenths of a point across
the entire scaling ladder.

\section{How to read this report}

The chapters are organised by \emph{question}, not by week.
Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} sets out what is already
known about how predictable a person is, what has been used to predict it, and
what that leaves open at the point this work starts. Chapter~\ref{ch:data}
establishes what the data is and what it permits. Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} settles
the instrument, which fine-tuning regime, which model, which representation.
Chapter~\ref{ch:first} sets out three measurement principles that cost more, in
points, than most of the modelling that followed. Chapter~\ref{ch:user}
establishes that the user, not the model, sets the score.
Chapters~\ref{ch:time} and~\ref{ch:groups} are one continuous argument: adding
time of day to the input works as a mechanism but fails on the hours I guessed,
and grouping users by \emph{when} they move is what turns that mechanism into
the project's only substantial gain. Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} asks how many users
that requires, and finds the answer sooner than expected.
Chapters~\ref{ch:critique} and~\ref{ch:conclusion} consolidate.

Each of Chapters~\ref{ch:data} to~\ref{ch:scale} closes with a short
\emph{Retrospective} giving my own view of that step with hindsight. The
chronology, which the thematic organisation deliberately breaks, is preserved in
Appendix~\ref{app:timeline}; Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the configuration of
every run and the provenance of every headline number, and
Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} a short glossary of the terms that recur from
chapter to chapter.
