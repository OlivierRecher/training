\section{Where I worked}

This report covers a second-year engineering internship carried out from
29 June to 28 August 2026 at the \host, under the supervision of \supervisor.
The laboratory holds a large set of anonymous records donated by a Czech mobile
operator, and hosts a small group of interns who study it from different angles.

The records contain no names, no phone numbers and no message of any kind. Each
line says only this: an anonymous number standing for one person, and the list
of antennas their phone was attached to during one day, with the time of each.
That is the entire material of this internship.

\section{The goal}

A mobile phone is always attached to one antenna, the one that currently gives
it the best signal. As the person walks, drives or takes a train, the phone lets
go of one antenna and attaches to the next. The operator writes a line down each
time that happens, and also at regular intervals when nothing happens at all.

The question I was given is short: \emph{from the beginning of somebody's day,
can I predict which antenna their phone will be attached to next?} An operator
wants to know this because it decides where capacity is needed, and because a
connection that is prepared before it is required is a connection that does not
drop.

Two words are worth fixing right away, because they are not synonyms and the
whole report depends on the difference.\footnote{\textbf{Base station}, also
called a \emph{site} or a \emph{mast}: one physical installation. \textbf{Cell}:
the area covered by one antenna of that installation, pointing in one
direction.} A \textbf{base station} is a physical mast standing somewhere in the
landscape. It carries several antennas pointing in different directions, and
each of those is called a \textbf{cell}. The study area of this report has
\textbf{369 cells} mounted on \textbf{113 base stations}, so a mast carries three
or four cells on average. What I predict is a cell, not a mast, which is a
slightly harder question than predicting a place, and Chapter~\ref{ch:data}
explains why.

The instrument is a \textbf{language model}.\footnote{\textbf{Language model}: a
program trained to guess the next symbol of a sequence. Nothing in that
description mentions language, which is why one can be pointed at a day of
movement instead.} These are the programs behind the writing assistants everyone
has now used: given the beginning of a sentence, they produce the word most
likely to come next. Nothing in how they work is specific to words. They take an
ordered list of symbols drawn from a fixed set and continue it. A day of
movement is an ordered list of cells drawn from a fixed set of 369, so the two
problems have exactly the same shape, and a model built for one can be pointed
at the other.

I do not use such a model as it comes. I \textbf{fine-tune} it, which means the
numbers inside it are modified by my data until it stops being a general-purpose
writer of English and becomes a predictor of movement in one Czech region.
Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} describes how, in detail and with pictures; the sentence
above is enough to read the rest of this introduction.

The other branch of the same problem, which two colleagues took, leaves those
numbers untouched and instead writes better instructions for a general model.
That is called \textbf{prompting}, and the two approaches answer different
questions rather than competing with each other. Mine is the fine-tuning one.

\section{The rule I have to beat}

What makes the question hard is not the model. It is the competitor.

Consider the rule \emph{``the person is still on the same cell as a moment
ago''}. It has no model, no training and no settings; it is one line of code. On
an ordinary day of these records it is right \textbf{69.2 times out of a
hundred}, simply because people spend most of their time not going anywhere.
This rule is called the \textbf{persistence baseline} throughout the report, and
beating it is the only result that means anything here. For five of the eleven
iterations of this internship, nothing I trained managed to.

Figure~\ref{fig:worked-example} shows why on two moments of a single day, and it
also shows where the competitor can and cannot be beaten.

\input{figures/fig-worked-example}

When the person stays put, repeating the last cell is correct, and both the rule
and my model collect the point for free. When the person leaves, the rule is
guaranteed to be wrong, and the answer has to come from somewhere else. Every
number reported in this document was won on moments of that second kind.

\section{What I found, in three sentences}

Read against that competitor, the outcome of nine weeks is modest and solid.

A fine-tuned model gets \textbf{4.3 more answers right out of every hundred}
than the one-line rule: 54.5\,\% against 50.2\,\% on a thousand people the model
had never seen, and 4.2 more out of a hundred on a second, separate group of
people.\footnote{\textbf{Held-out people}: users set aside before training and
never shown to the model, on which the reported score is measured. Training and
test users are always different people here.} What produced that gain was
neither a bigger model nor more data, but \emph{sorting people by the hours at
which they actually move} and giving each kind of person its own set of trained
numbers.

The amount of data needed for it is small, and the report measures where it
stops paying: the edge grows from 0.6 to 4.2 more correct answers per hundred as
the training population goes from 400 to 1{,}900 people, and multiplying that
population by ten again buys 0.1, which is nothing.

And the difficulty is not where I expected it. \textbf{22.4} correct answers per
hundred separate the calmest kind of user from the most restless one, against
\textbf{0.4} across the entire range of training sizes. Who the person is
matters more than how much data was used, by a factor of fifty.

\subsection*{How a gain is written in this report}

Every headline figure above is a \emph{difference between two percentages},
never a percentage of a percentage. When I write that the model gains 4.3\,\%
over the rule, I mean it answers correctly 54.5 times out of a hundred where
the rule answers correctly 50.2 times, so 4.3 more correct answers out of a
hundred. The two scores are always measured on the same people and on the same
moments of their day, so the difference between them is the only quantity that
is fair to compare from one chapter to the next.

That last point is worth stating on its own, because it is a rule the report
never departs from. The set of people I trained and tested on changed four times
over the internship, each time because a new idea needed data the earlier files
did not carry, and the difficulty of the test group changed with it. An absolute
accuracy therefore describes the people it was measured on more than it
describes the model. Only the distance to the one-line rule, measured on those
same people, survives that.

\section{How to read this report}

The chapters are organised by \emph{question}, not by week.
Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} sets out what is already known about how predictable a
person is, and what that leaves open. Chapter~\ref{ch:data} says what the data
is and what it permits. Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} settles the instrument: which
model, which training method, and how a day is written down for it.
Chapter~\ref{ch:first} sets out three rules about \emph{measuring} that were
worth more, in correct answers, than most of the modelling that followed.
Chapter~\ref{ch:user} establishes that the person, not the model, sets the
score. Chapters~\ref{ch:time} and~\ref{ch:groups} are one continuous argument:
giving the model the time of day works as a mechanism but fails on the hours I
guessed, and sorting people into kinds of user is what turns that mechanism into
the project's only substantial gain. Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} asks how many people
that requires, and finds the answer sooner than expected.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} consolidates, and says what I did not have time to
do.

Each of Chapters~\ref{ch:data} to~\ref{ch:scale} closes with a short
\emph{Looking back} section giving my own view of that step with hindsight. The
chronology, which the thematic organisation deliberately breaks, is preserved in
Appendix~\ref{app:timeline}; Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the settings of every
run and the origin of every headline number, and Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} is
a short glossary of the terms that recur.
