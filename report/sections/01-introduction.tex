\section{Where I worked}

This report covers a second-year engineering internship carried out from
29 June to 28 August 2026 at the \host, under the supervision of \supervisor.
The laboratory holds a large set of anonymous records donated by a Czech mobile
operator, and hosts a small group of interns who study it from different angles.

Those records contain no names, no phone numbers and no message of any kind.
One line says only this: an anonymous number standing for one person, and the
list of antennas their phone was attached to during one day, with the time of
each. That is the entire material of this internship.

\section{The goal}

A mobile phone is always attached to one antenna, the one that currently gives
it the best signal. As the person walks, drives or takes a train, the phone lets
go of one antenna and attaches to the next. The operator writes a line down each
time that happens, and also at regular intervals when nothing happens at all.

The question I was given is short: \emph{from the beginning of somebody's day,
can I predict which antenna their phone will be attached to next?} An operator
wants to know this because it decides where capacity is needed, and because a
connection prepared before it is required is a connection that does not drop.

Two words are not synonyms, and the whole report depends on the
difference.\footnote{\textbf{Base station}, also called a \emph{site} or a
\emph{mast}: one physical installation. \textbf{Cell}: the area covered by one
antenna of that installation, pointing in one direction.} A \textbf{base
station} is a physical mast standing somewhere in the landscape. It carries
several antennas pointing in different directions, and each of those is called a
\textbf{cell}. The study area has \textbf{369 cells} on \textbf{113 base
stations}, so a mast carries three or four cells on average. What I predict is a
cell, not a mast, which is harder than predicting a place.

\section{What I built}

The instrument is a \textbf{language model}.\footnote{\textbf{Language model}: a
program trained to guess the next symbol of a sequence. Nothing in that
description mentions language, which is why one can be pointed at a day of
movement instead.} These are the programs behind the writing assistants everyone
has now used: given the beginning of a sentence, they produce the word most
likely to come next. Nothing in how they work is specific to words. They
continue an ordered list of symbols drawn from a fixed set, and a day of
movement is an ordered list of cells drawn from a fixed set of 369.

I do not use such a model as it comes. I \textbf{fine-tune} it: the numbers
inside it are modified by my data until it stops being a general-purpose writer
of English and becomes a predictor of movement in one Czech region.
Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} shows how. The other branch of the same problem, taken by
two colleagues, leaves those numbers untouched and writes better instructions
for a general model instead. That is called \textbf{prompting}, and it answers a
different question rather than competing with mine.

\section{The rule I have to beat}

What makes the question hard is not the model. It is the competitor.

Consider the rule \emph{``the person is still on the same cell as a moment
ago''}. No model, no training, no settings; one line of code. On an ordinary day
of these records it is right \textbf{69.2 times out of a hundred}, simply because
people spend most of their time not going anywhere. This rule is called the
\textbf{persistence baseline} throughout the report, and beating it is the only
result that means anything here. For five of the eleven iterations of this
internship, nothing I trained managed to.

\input{figures/fig-worked-example}

When the person stays put, repeating the last cell is correct and both the rule
and my model collect the point for free. When the person leaves, the rule is
guaranteed to be wrong, and the answer has to come from somewhere else. Every
number in this document was won on moments of that second kind.

\section{What I found, in three sentences}

A fine-tuned model gets \textbf{4.3 more answers right out of every hundred}
than the one-line rule: 54.5\,\% against 50.2\,\% on a thousand people the model
had never seen, and 4.2 more out of a hundred on a second, separate
group.\footnote{\textbf{Held-out people}: people set aside before training and
never shown to the model. Training and test people are always different people
here.} What produced that gain was neither a bigger model nor more data, but
\emph{sorting people by the hours at which they actually move} and giving each
kind of person its own set of trained numbers.

The data needed for it is little: the edge grows from 0.6 to 4.2 more correct
answers per hundred as the training population goes from 400 to 1{,}900 people,
and multiplying that population by ten again buys 0.1, which is nothing.

And the difficulty is not where I expected it. \textbf{22.4} correct answers per
hundred separate the calmest kind of user from the most restless one, against
\textbf{0.4} across the entire range of training sizes. Who the person is matters
more than how much data was used, by a factor of fifty.

\subsection*{How a gain is written in this report}

Every figure above is a \emph{difference between two percentages}. The model
gaining 4.3\,\% over the rule means it answers correctly 54.5 times out of a
hundred where the rule answers correctly 50.2 times, so 4.3 more correct answers
out of a hundred, always on the same people and the same moments.

Only that distance is comparable from one chapter to the next. The set of people
I trained and tested on changed four times over the internship, each time
because a new idea needed data the earlier files did not carry, and the
difficulty of the test group changed with it. An absolute accuracy therefore
describes the people it was measured on more than it describes the model.
