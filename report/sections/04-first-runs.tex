\chapter{Measuring before modelling}
\label{ch:first}

\section{The question}

A fine-tuned model produces a number. Whether that number means anything
depends entirely on three things that have nothing to do with the model: which
checkpoint is being read, how the day is cut, and how much room the benchmark
leaves in the first place. Each of the three cost me a wrong conclusion before
it was understood, and each is worth more, in points, than most of the modelling
ideas that came later. This chapter sets out the three principles and uses the
early runs as the evidence for them.

\section{The measurement, fixed once}

Every result in this report uses the same three-part measurement, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}. Each user's day is cut once into a \emph{context}
prefix and a \emph{target} suffix; the model is trained on the prefix and must
produce the suffix, which it has never seen. At scoring time it predicts
position $i$ from positions $0 \ldots i-1$ of the \emph{real} history, so it
can neither see the future nor drift on its own earlier mistakes, which
panel~(b) of Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} draws, and the persistence baseline
answers the same questions at the same positions. The ranked output is then read at
three depths: top-1, top-3 and top-5.

\section{Principle 1: the last epoch is never the model}

A language model fine-tuned on a few hundred short sequences will memorise
them.\footnote{\textbf{Overfitting}: the point past which a model stops learning the
regularity of the data and starts memorising the examples. The tell is in
Figure~\ref{fig:validation-curves}: the training loss keeps falling while the
score on unseen users turns and declines.}
That is not a hypothesis; it is what the loss curve does. The clearest evidence
in this project is a single run, deliberately allowed to continue to a hard
ceiling of forty-nine epochs on \Draw: its training
loss falls from 9.7 to 0.11, and its validation accuracy climbs for nine epochs,
peaks, and then declines for the remaining forty. Panel~(a) of
Figure~\ref{fig:validation-curves} shows both halves of that sentence at once.

\input{figures/fig-validation-curves}

The important part is not that overfitting happened, it was expected, but
how much it costs to ignore it. The checkpoint at the end of that run scored
48.7\,\% on the held-out users. The checkpoint at the validation peak scored
64.2\,\%. Reading the end of training instead of its best point therefore throws
away $15.5\pt$, and spends forty epochs of GPU time doing so. Acting on
that, keeping the best checkpoint, stopping on a plateau, and adding mild
anti-overfitting settings (adapter dropout 0.1, learning rate
$1.5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01),\footnote{Three standard knobs.
\textbf{Dropout}: a tenth of the adapter's internal values are zeroed at random
at each step, so the model cannot lean on any single one. \textbf{Learning
rate}: how big a step to take when adjusting a weight. \textbf{Weight decay}: a
mild pull of every trainable number back towards zero.} moved test top-1 from 48.7\,\%
to
\textbf{64.4\,\%}, which is $+15.7\pt$ for the checkpoint rule and the three
settings together. It also cut GPU time from seventy-two to twenty-nine
minutes, and lifted top-3 from 76.2\,\% to 82.7\,\%.

Panels~(b), (c) and~(d) of Figure~\ref{fig:validation-curves} confirm that the
shape is a property of the task rather than of one run: on three further
datasets, with baselines ranging from 43.5\,\% to 79.0\,\%, validation peaks
between epoch four and epoch six and then decays. The peak is always early,
which is why every run in this report is early-stopped and evaluated at its best
checkpoint. Two runs are then always compared at their respective peaks, which
is the only comparison that is not an accident of where each one happened to
stop.

\section{Principle 2: the cut is part of the ruler, not of the model}

The share of the day given as context, the \emph{context
fraction},\footnote{\textbf{Context fraction}: the share of a user's day handed to the model
before it must predict the rest. For a user with 100 events, 80\,\% context
means reading events 1--80 and being graded on 81--100. Distinct from the
\emph{context window} of Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}, which is an architectural
limit.} looks like a
hyperparameter\footnote{\textbf{Hyperparameter}: a setting chosen by the
experimenter before training, as opposed to a weight, which the training
procedure discovers. Learning rate and number of epochs are hyperparameters; the
whole point of this section is that the context fraction is \emph{not} one.} of
the experiment. It is not: it is part of the
measurement. Moving the cut changes which cell counts as ``the last one seen'',
so it changes the persistence baseline too. A model evaluated at 90\,\% context
is not a better model than the same model evaluated at 30\,\%; it is the same
model measured against an easier ruler.

Table~\ref{tab:context-sweep} is the evidence, and it contains the mistake this
principle exists to prevent. On \Dfilt{} the model reaches
80.1\,\% at the reference 80/20 split, against a baseline of 79.0\,\%: a gap of
$+1.1\pt$, and the first time in the project that a trained model cleared the
one-line rule. Read on its own that looks like a result. Read across the three
rows it is far weaker: at 70\,\% context the model trails by $0.3\pt$, and at
90\,\% context, where its own validation accuracy is the \emph{highest} of the
three, it trails by $2.4\pt$. One split out of three passes.

\input{tables/tab-context-sweep}

The lower block of the same table settles it. On \Dmob{}
a single trained model was evaluated at seven context fractions from 30\,\% to
90\,\%: its accuracy rises monotonically from 41.5\,\% to 47.6\,\%, and the
baseline rises with it, in lockstep, at every single value. No split beats the
baseline; the closest is $-0.3\pt$ and the worst, again at 90\,\% context, is
$-2.1\pt$. More history helps the model and helps ``repeat the last cell''
by the same amount, so a rising accuracy curve across context fractions carries
no information about the model at all.

One clarification belongs here, because the two blocks of
Table~\ref{tab:context-sweep} are not the same experiment. The \sfilt{} rows are
three separate trainings, each with its own cut. The \smob{} rows are one
training at 80/20, evaluated seven times, so what they measure is
\emph{robustness} to being given more or less history than the model was trained
on, not which cut trains the best model. Both questions are legitimate; they are
not interchangeable, and the second is the one this report can answer.

\section{Principle 3: a benchmark has to leave room}

The third principle is the one that took longest to accept. On a day-file taken
as it comes, ``repeat the last cell'' is right 69.2\,\% of the time. There is
almost nothing above that for a model to win, and most of what is left is not
mobility at all, it is users with three records from a single cell. A model
scoring 64.4\,\% on such a corpus has not failed at prediction; it has been
measured on a benchmark with no room in it.

The same trained architecture, on three corpora with different populations,
faces baselines of 69.2\,\%, 79.0\,\% and 45.5\,\%. Nothing about the rule
changed between those three numbers; the users did. That is why the corpus
decision of Chapter~\ref{ch:data} was worth more than any hyperparameter: it is
what created a benchmark on which the question could be measured at all.

\section{What the three principles settled}

Taken together they closed the ``tune it harder'' avenue. Once runs were read at
their peak, once the cut was recognised as part of the ruler, and once the
corpus had been chosen so that the yardstick was beatable in principle, the
model still sat within a few points of a one-line rule, usually below it, at
every context fraction and on every corpus tried.

The example that decided what to do about it is small enough to quote in full.
On one run, user 775719, whose context is the same cell repeated ten times, is
predicted correctly with 100\,\% confidence. User 3268099, whose context ends on
one cell and whose next event is a different one entirely, is predicted as ``the
same cell again'' with 68\,\% confidence. The model was right for free on the
first and wrong for free on the second, and in neither case did training have
much to do with the outcome. A corollary experiment made the same point
statistically: on a 100-user version of the corpus, per-user accuracy ranged from
10\,\% to 100\,\% and the model never passed 42.1\,\%. One hundred users is not a
population; it is a handful of anecdotes with an average printed underneath.

\section{Retrospective}
\label{sec:retro-first}

Two things stand out with hindsight.

The first is the $+1.1\pt$. I presented it as a result, and it was not one. Of
the three cuts tried, one passes and the two others lose ground, $-0.3$ and
$-2.4\pt$, and every figure in that table comes from a single training. One
favourable cell in a table of three is too weak to carry a conclusion, and the
following iteration duly made it disappear: the same architecture on a cleaner
corpus beat nothing at all. What I would ask of that number now is the one thing
I did not ask of it, repeat it. A second training, or the same cut on a second
corpus, would have cost half an hour and would have said immediately whether
there was anything there.

The second is that the three principles of this chapter arrived one at a time,
and each of them arrived \emph{after} a conclusion that turned out to be wrong.
They are not deep ideas; they are hygiene. A short measurement checklist written
before the first training, read the validation peak and not the last epoch,
recompute the baseline every time the cut moves, check that the benchmark leaves
room before spending a week inside it, would have caught all three at once,
and the modelling work would have started two iterations earlier. That checklist
is, in effect, what this chapter has become.
