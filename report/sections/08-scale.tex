\chapter{How many users are enough}
\label{ch:scale}

\section{The question}

Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} ended on an uncomfortable arithmetic. Four hundred
training users split across four behavioural profiles leaves the smallest
profile with a few dozen people, and a profile estimated from a few dozen people
is barely a profile. If the mechanism that produced the project's only real gain
depends on profiles being well populated, then the obvious next lever is
population size. Hence the question of this chapter: \emph{how many users does a
model need before the population itself is described, and what happens after
that point?}

The question is unusually well posed on this corpus. Since the unit is one day
per user (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}), the only scaling axis available is more
\emph{people}, never more history per person, so this chapter measures what
knowing how the population moves is worth, and nothing else.

\section{First, a wrong way to ask it}

An early attempt at the same question got a misleading answer, and it is worth
recording because the mistake is easy to repeat. On the raw day-file, moving from
400 to 5{,}000 training users lifted the model's top-1 from 64.4\,\% to 76.0\,\%,
eleven and a half points, for eight times the training time. Read alone that
looks like a scaling law.

It is not, because the persistence baseline of the larger sample rose from
69.2\,\% to 81.6\,\% at the same time. The gap to the baseline did not close; it
\emph{widened}, from $-4.8$ to $-5.6\pt$. What had actually happened is that a
larger random sample of an unfiltered day-file contains a larger absolute number
of near-immobile users, so both the model and the trivial rule got easier
questions. Scaling a dataset within a regime whose baseline scales with it buys
nothing at all, and this is the clearest illustration in the report of why every
number here is a gap.

\section{The ladder, done properly}

The proper version fixes everything except the number of users. Same
architecture, same hyperparameters, same context fraction, hour tokens and
behavioural groups active throughout, and, crucially, each tier read
against the persistence baseline of its own test users. Eight training-set sizes
were measured, from 400 training users up to 21{,}000.

The largest tiers required a corpus built for the purpose: eleven day-files of
2{,}000 users each, concatenated into 22{,}000 user-days, of which 1{,}000 users
are held out and never trained on. That concatenation is exactly what it looks
like, more people, each observed for one day. The tiers are nested, so that a
larger tier is strictly \emph{more} data and never \emph{other} data: the
21{,}000-user set is the 5{,}000-user set plus sixteen thousand more. Without
that, a difference between two tiers could have come from \emph{which} users
were drawn rather than from how many. Over those tiers the number of
events grows from 369{,}451 to 1{,}538{,}379 and the number of available training
windows\footnote{\textbf{Training window}: one contiguous slice of a user's day, at most the
context length, used as a single training example. A long user yields several
and a short one just one, which is why the window count grows more slowly than
the user count.} from 5{,}379 to 22{,}522, for a total of two hours fifty-one minutes of GPU time,
forty-two minutes per tier.

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-ladder} is the result, and it has three distinct
regions.

The curve does not slow down. It stops.

\section{Is the flatness real, or is it noise?}

``The best tier is followed by the worst'' is the signature of noise, but a
signature is not a test. The test used is a paired bootstrap over the 1{,}000
held-out users. A thousand users are drawn at random from those thousand, with
replacement so that some appear twice and others not at all; both tiers are
scored on exactly that draw and the difference between them is noted; and the
whole thing is repeated two thousand times. The middle 95\,\% of those two
thousand differences is the interval reported, and because the two tiers always
see the same draw, the luck of the sample cancels and only the difference
between the models is left. An interval containing zero means the two cannot be
told apart. Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap} gives them.

\input{tables/tab-bootstrap}

Every interval contains zero, each individual step, and the interval spanning
the entire ladder from 5{,}000 to 21{,}000 users, whose point estimate is
$+0.04\pt$. Fitting a scaling law\footnote{\textbf{Scaling law}: in the
pre-training literature \cite{kaplan2020scaling}, the observation that error
falls in a straight line when data and error are both plotted logarithmically.}
to the same points asks how many points of edge a ten-fold increase in users
buys. The answer is a slope of $+0.18\pt$ per ten-fold increase, which at that
rate would need on the order of 1.5 billion users to buy one further point, and
the fit accounts for only 12\,\% of the spread of the eight measured points, so
the ``law'' is fitting noise rather than a trend. The correct statement is
therefore not ``the gain is small'' but ``no gain was measured''.

Nor does anything hide behind the aggregate. Table~\ref{tab:all-metrics} takes
the same four tiers through six different metrics, top-1, top-3, top-5, the
cold-start regime with only 30\,\% of the history, the median user, and the
bottom decile\footnote{\textbf{Bottom decile}: the tenth of users on whom the
model scores worst. Reporting it separately is a guard against a model that lifts
the average while abandoning the users it already served badly.} of users, and
the spread across the whole ladder is between $0.2$ and $1.3\pt$ on every
line. Depth of ranking is flat. The hard decile is
flat. Cold start is flat.

One line of that table is worth isolating, because it is the only place where

\section{What does move the score}

One thing does, and it is a property of the user rather than of the dataset:
which behavioural profile they belong to. Evaluated with the
21{,}000-user model on the same held-out users, the four profiles of
Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} score 62.8\,\% (G0, the homebody, 22\,\% of the users of
\seleven), 53.6\,\% (G1, the steady user, 37\,\%), 43.0\,\% (G2, the evening
mover, 22\,\%) and 40.4\,\% (G3, the morning mover, 18\,\%): a spread of
\textbf{22.4 points}. The shares are not those measured on \smob{} in
Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, since these are twenty-one thousand different people,
but the four shapes themselves were quantitatively identical at every tier,
which is itself a scaling result: 5{,}000 users already describe this population
completely, so there is nothing left for the next sixteen thousand to add.

Put side by side, 22.4 points against 0.4: who the user is outweighs how much
data was used to train, by a factor of more than fifty.

\section{Why it climbs and then stops}

Two mechanisms account for the two ends of the curve, and both are checkable.

\paragraph{Below the threshold, copying is simply the cheapest way to lower the
loss.} On the 7{,}745 predictions of the 100-user test set, 48\,\% ask for the
same cell again and 52\,\% are an actual decision. Half the loss therefore falls
without modelling anything at all. Before any of the 369 cell identifiers means
a place, the model has to learn them as symbols; the median user offers around
twenty-one candidate cells over sixty-four events. Meanwhile 400 training users
split across four profiles and four bands of the day leaves roughly
twenty-five examples per cell of that grid, and twenty-five examples cannot tell
a habit from a coincidence. A $+0.6\pt$ edge is the signature of a model that
has learned the format and the trivial habit and nothing about behaviour, which
is exactly what the ablations of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} independently
concluded.

\paragraph{The jump happens where the smallest profile becomes estimable.} One
mechanism fits the timing of the transition, and it is testable. On the corpus
the profiles were cut on, G2, the evening mover, is the least populated of the
four at 14\,\% of users. At the 400-user tier that is \textbf{56 people}; at 900
training users, \textbf{126}; at 1{,}900, \textbf{266}. It crosses roughly a
hundred members exactly between the two tiers where the edge jumps from $+0.6$
to $+2.8\pt$. On the much larger \seleven{} that same profile holds 22\,\% of
users, which would move the crossing earlier rather than later, so the argument
does not turn on which of the two shares is taken. A
group token can only carry information if the group has enough inhabitants for
its rhythm to be estimated at all, below that, the model is right to ignore
it, which is precisely what the failed ablation showed it doing. Cross-training
did not become a better idea at 900 users than at 400; the groups simply became
large enough to be worth reading.

\paragraph{And above the threshold, every new user is a behavioural duplicate.}
With 113 physical sites, four profiles and a test vocabulary already fully
covered at 5{,}000 users, the sixteen thousand additional people bring no
behaviour the model has not already seen several hundred times.

\section{Two limits of the experiment, stated plainly}

The flat half of the curve was measured at \emph{constant compute}, and that is
a deliberate choice with a consequence. Every tier received the same budget of
optimiser steps, enough to read 8{,}000 training examples, however many were
available. At 5{,}000 users there are 5{,}379 windows, so the run gets through all
of them one and a half times. At 21{,}000 users there are 22{,}522, so 8{,}000
are read and \textbf{14{,}522 are never read even once}: 36\,\% coverage. The
larger dataset was not added, it was spread thinner. Separately, only about
2\,\% of the model was ever allowed to change, and that fraction was never
increased with the data.

So the defensible conclusion is \emph{``with this much training time and this
much trainable capacity, 1{,}900 users are enough''}, not ``more users can never
help''. Both statements would draw the same flat line, and one experiment would
tell them apart: give the largest dataset the training time its size deserves.
That run was not performed, and the claim is limited accordingly.

\section{What the numbers said}

The chapter settles two things. Data volume is a real lever over one narrow
stretch, from roughly 400 to 1{,}900 training users, and is worth nothing above
it at constant compute. And what difficulty there is lives in the user: 22.4
points across behavioural profiles, against 0.4 points across the whole training
ladder.

A third result belongs beside them, and the section that follows is given to it:
one way of using the same users better, levelling the number of records each of
them contributes, does everything it promises and still cannot be used here.

\section{A parenthesis: levelling records instead of adding users}
\label{sec:levelling}

One alternative to adding users was tested in the same iteration, and it is
instructive enough to deserve its own section, both for what it delivers and
for why it does not work here.

The idea, adapted from a colleague's work, is that a few very long users dominate
training: with natural sequence lengths, the top quarter of users carries
44.3\,\% of the training positions,\footnote{\textbf{Training position}: one scored \cid{cell\_id} in the training data.
A user with 200 events contributes about 200 of them and a user with 20
contributes 20, so long users dominate the training simply by being long.} so a fine-tune spends most of its budget
learning the habits of the heaviest users. The remedy is to stretch every
sequence to exactly 200 records by repeating each existing record a few times,
spread evenly along the sequence rather than piled up at one end.

\paragraph{What it delivers is exactly what it promises.} Measured on the 400
training users: the ratio between longest and shortest user goes from 200:19 to
1:1; the Gini coefficient\footnote{\textbf{Gini coefficient}: a measure of
inequality borrowed from economics, from 0 when every user contributes the same
number of training positions to 1 when a single user contributes all of them.}
of per-user weight goes from a moderately unequal 0.294 to \textbf{0}; the
top decile's share of training positions falls from 21.0\,\% to 10.0\,\% and the
top quartile's from 44.3\,\% to 25.0\,\%; and the number of training positions
rises from 34{,}408 to 80{,}000. Every user now weighs exactly 0.25\,\% of the
training. The diagnosis behind the technique is right, and the technique does
what it was designed to do.

\paragraph{What it does to the score is a warning about baselines.}
Figure~\ref{fig:resampling} shows the outcome. The model on levelled sequences
reaches 74.9\,\%, which is twenty-eight points above the natural-length run and
by a wide margin the highest absolute accuracy in the whole project. On the same
levelled sequences, the persistence baseline reaches 76.5\,\%. The gap changed
sign: from $+3.7\pt$ to $-1.6\pt$.

\input{figures/fig-resampling}

The arithmetic is simple once seen. The reference protocol scores the last 20\,\%
of each sequence, forty predictions per user, 4{,}000 over the hundred test
users, and \textbf{2{,}262 of those 4{,}000 positions ask for a record that is
a copy of the one before}. Any rule that repeats is right on all of them by
construction. Both the model and the baseline are handed those 2{,}262 free
answers, which is why both rose by about thirty points. On the 1{,}738 positions
that are real decisions, the baseline scores 45.9\,\% and the model between
42.2\,\% and 44.8\,\% depending on how many copies one assumes it misses, below
the baseline at either end, where the natural-length run had been $+3.7\pt$
above it.

The redistribution the technique intends is visible in the results, and it is
paid for exactly as one would fear: split by sequence length, the shortest
quartile of users comes out at parity ($-1.1\pt$, on sequences that are 82\,\%
copies) while the longest quartile loses $3.6\pt$ (22\,\% copies). Weight was
successfully transferred from the users who had the most to say to the users who
had the least.

\paragraph{Why it is not conclusive, and why it could not be.} The deeper reason
is that copying destroys the very signal the behavioural profiles are cut on. A
copied step never changes site, by construction. So on the training users the
average site-change rate falls from 0.508 to 0.189; the standard
deviation\footnote{\textbf{Standard deviation}: the typical distance of a value from its
population's average, in the same units. It matters here because clustering
can only separate users who differ: at $0.079$ instead of $0.183$, the four
profiles overlap.} \emph{between} users, which is what the four profiles are
carved out of, falls from 0.183 to 0.079, a factor of $2.3$; and the most mobile user in the
corpus, who changed site at every step, drops from 1.00 to 0.56. The whole range
compresses from $0.17$--$1.00$ to $0.08$--$0.56$: every user drifts towards the
homebody profile.

That is fatal for this particular pipeline, because group detection is the
mechanism that produced the project's only real gain, and group detection needs
between-user spread to work. Levelling the records makes the users look alike,
which makes the profiles harder to tell apart, which disables cross-training.
The technique is not wrong, it fixes a real imbalance, and the imbalance it
fixes is genuine, but on this corpus and with this method it removes more
information than it redistributes.

\section{Retrospective}
\label{sec:retro-scale}

Finding the operating point, the number of training users beyond which
nothing further is bought, is the result of this chapter I am most satisfied
with, because it is a statement about the problem rather than about my pipeline.
Knowing that the curve stops at around two thousand users turns ``get more
data'' from an assumption into a settled question, and it is cheap knowledge:
the eight tiers together cost under three hours of GPU time.

What I would change is \emph{when} it was measured, not how. Most of the
internship was spent improving the model on four hundred training users, a
tier that this very curve later placed inside the imitation regime, the one
place where a fine-tuned model cannot be told apart from the rule it is meant to
beat. Establishing the ladder in the first weeks would have said so
immediately, and the work on the temporal signal and on the behavioural groups
could then have been carried out at a tier where those mechanisms have room to
show an effect at all.

It did, at least, arrive in time to inform the final findings rather than after
them, and there is a reason the order was not simply wrong. The explanation
for the threshold, that the smallest behavioural profile crosses roughly a
hundred members exactly where the edge jumps, only exists because the profiles
had already been built and measured. Run first, the scaling study would have
produced the curve without the mechanism that explains it. Run last, it produced
both.
