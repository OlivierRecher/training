\chapter{Scaling Analysis: Impact of Cohort Size on Predictability}
\label{ch:scale}

\section{Scaling Analysis Objectives}

Stratifying 400 training users across four behavioral user groups leaves smaller clusters (such as $G2$, comprising 14\% of the cohort) with approximately 56 training trajectories. Estimating robust subgroup transition probabilities from limited samples presents empirical bounds.

This observation leads to the central research question evaluated in this chapter: how does increasing user cohort size impact next-cell prediction accuracy, and at what scale does performance saturate?

\paragraph{Data Provenance.} Evaluation spans three dataset cohorts from \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:datasets}}: 400 users (\Dmob{}, 100 held-out users, baseline 43.1\%), 900 to 1,900 users (\Dtwok{}, 100 held-out users, baseline 48.2\%), and 5,000 to 21,000 users (\Deleven{}, \textbf{1,000 held-out users}, baseline 50.2\%). Because baseline scores vary across evaluation cohorts, performance is evaluated strictly using accuracy deltas relative to each cohort's one-line baseline (``repeat the last cell'') rather than absolute accuracy percentages, ensuring robust comparisons across varying test user pools.

\section{Experimental Setup for Cohort Size Sweep}

The cohort scaling evaluation sweeps training population size across eight nested steps (400, 900, 1,400, 1,900, 5,000, 10,000, 15,000, and 21,000 users) while holding model architecture, LoRA adapter configuration, hour tokens, and per-group routing constant. Nested sampling ensures that larger cohorts strictly expand smaller cohorts without introducing population composition shifts. Across training sizes, training log counts increase from 369,451 to 1,538,379 events.

\input{figures/fig-scaling-ladder}

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-ladder}} plots prediction accuracy delta relative to baseline against training cohort size on a logarithmic scale, so that equal distances are equal multiples. The vertical axis is not a score but the distance to the baseline on each run's own test users, and the dashed line at zero marks a model indistinguishable from repeating the last cell. The empirical curve exhibits three distinct regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Imitation regime (400 users):} At 400 training users, the model achieves a marginal $+0.6\pt$ delta over baseline, failing to capture group-level transition dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Generalization regime (400 to 1,900 users):} Accuracy delta climbs rapidly through $+2.8\pt$ (900 users) and $+3.5\pt$ (1,400 users) to $+4.2\pt$ (1,900 users), capturing essentially the entire benefit derived from dataset volume expansion.
    \item \textbf{Saturation regime (1,900 to 21,000 users):} A ten-fold expansion in user scale from 1,900 to 21,000 users yields negligible additional gains (+4.3\pt{} at 5,000, +4.4\pt{} at 10,000, +4.7\pt{} at 15,000, and +4.3\pt{} at 21,000 users), and the best size (15,000) is followed by the worst (21,000): performance growth halts rather than merely slowing down.
\end{enumerate}

Two important experimental caveats govern this scaling trend. First, because test user pools vary across cohort sizes, the small step across the boundary between two test pools is not a measurement. Second, all scaling steps were evaluated under a constant compute budget (Section~\ref{sec:scale-limits}), so the defensible claim is that 1,900 users suffice \emph{at this budget}.

\subsection{Statistical Validation of Performance Saturation}

To establish whether performance plateauing above 2,000 users reflects true saturation rather than random variance, paired bootstrap resampling \textbf{\cite{efron1993bootstrap}} was conducted across 2,000 resamples of the 1,000 held-out users. In each bootstrap iteration, 1,000 users were sampled with replacement and both candidate models were scored on the identical user draw, canceling evaluation sample variance.

\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}} presents the resulting 95\% confidence intervals across cohort scale transitions. For all transitions above 5,000 users, the 95\% confidence interval spans zero (e.g., net delta between 5,000 and 21,000 users is $+0.04\pt$, 95\% CI $[-0.43, +0.51]$): the claim is not that the gain is small, but that no gain was measured. Fitting a log-linear scaling law across all eight data points yields a minimal slope of $+0.18\pt$ per decade of users, accounting for only 12\% of variance ($R^2 = 0.12$).

\input{tables/tab-bootstrap}

\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:all-metrics}} confirms that performance saturation holds uniformly across six complementary evaluation metrics. Across four training sizes (5,000 to 21,000 users), top-1 accuracy (54.5\% to 54.9\%, spread $0.4\pt$), top-3 accuracy (71.4\% to 72.2\%, spread $0.2\pt$), top-5 accuracy (78.6\% to 79.3\%, spread $0.4\pt$), cold-start accuracy (51.4\% to 52.6\%, spread $0.5\pt$), median user accuracy (49.1\% to 50.3\%, spread $1.2\pt$), and worst-decile accuracy (13.1\% to 14.4\%, spread $1.3\pt$) show no systematic improvement; the 1,900-user reference column, marked $^{\dagger}$, is measured on \Dtwok{} against a 48.2\% baseline and is excluded from the spread. Crucially, under cold-start conditions (evaluating only the first 30\% of a user's daily history), the model achieves its highest edge over baseline (+4.6\pt{} vs. +4.3\pt{} at 80\% context history), demonstrating that model capabilities are most effective when user history is sparse, and this advantage remains identical at 5,000 and 21,000 users.

\input{tables/tab-all-metrics}

Furthermore, \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:context-scale}} evaluates performance across context history fractions (30\% to 90\% of daily observations), comparing 900 and 1,900 user models graded on the same 100 held-out users. The 1,900-user model leads the baseline by $+4.3\pt$ at 30\% context and $+4.2\pt$ at 80\%, and across all context fractions expanding training cohort size from 900 to 1,900 users yields a consistent gain between $+0.9\pt$ and $+1.7\pt$, establishing that dataset scale improvements operate independently of context history length.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis: User Typology vs. Data Scale}

Evaluating the 21,000-user model across behavioral cohorts reveals substantial variance: top-1 accuracy reaches 62.8\% for stationary users ($G0$, 22\% of cohort), 53.6\% for regular mobile users ($G1$, 37\%), 43.0\% for evening-mobile users ($G2$, 22\%), and 40.4\% for morning-mobile users ($G3$, 18\%). This establishes a performance spread of \textbf{22.4\,\% across user types}, compared to a marginal \textbf{0.4\,\% spread across dataset scale} (from 1,900 to 21,000 users in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-ladder}}).

User mobility typology exerts a $50\times$ stronger impact on prediction accuracy than scaling training cohort size beyond 2,000 users.

\section{Mechanistic Analysis of Saturation Thresholds}

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:why-it-stops}} details two physical mechanisms underlying performance scaling and saturation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Subgroup Sample Threshold (\panelref{fig:why-it-stops}{a}):} Tracking user counts in the smallest behavioral cohort ($G2$ evening-mobile users, 14\% population share) reveals 56 members at 400 users (+0.6\pt{} gain), 126 members at 900 users (+2.8\pt{} gain), 196 members at 1,400 users (+3.5\pt{} gain), and 266 members at 1,900 users (+4.2\pt{} gain). Crossing approximately 100 members in the smallest subgroup provides sufficient sample volume to robustly estimate subgroup-specific transition matrices, explaining the performance jump between 400 and 900 users.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Vocabulary Coverage Saturation (\panelref{fig:why-it-stops}{b}):} Each square is one of the 100 cells the test users actually use; the grid shows that 99 cells are already observed at 400 users, and 100\% coverage is reached by 5,000 users. Because cell vocabulary coverage saturates and the underlying behavioral transition patterns (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:group-profiles}}) remain invariant, expanding cohorts beyond 5,000 users introduces redundant trajectories across previously seen cell graphs rather than novel mobility structures.
\end{enumerate}

\input{figures/fig-why-it-stops}

These two mechanisms fit the curve without proving it: the alternative reading, that the compute budget rather than the population ran out, is set out in Section~\ref{sec:scale-limits}.

\subsection{Methodological Constraints and Scope Boundaries}
\label{sec:scale-limits}

Scaling experiments maintained constant compute step budgets (8,000 gradient update steps per run). At 5,000 users (5,379 training sequences), epoch coverage reaches 1.5 passes. At 21,000 users (22,522 sequences), 8,000 steps correspond to 36\% single-pass epoch coverage. Consequently, reported saturation bounds hold under fixed compute step constraints and fixed parameter adapter capacity.

\section{Evaluation of Trajectory Length Equalization}

An alternative preprocessing approach was evaluated: equalizing user trajectory lengths by oversampling short trajectories to a uniform 200 records per user, addressing data imbalances where the top 25\% longest trajectories comprise 44\% of training events.

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:resampling}} evaluates trajectory equalization, each run shown against its own baseline: trajectory stretching yields an apparent top-1 accuracy of 74.9\%, representing the highest absolute score recorded. However, evaluating the baseline rule on the same equalized trajectories yields 76.5\%, placing the fine-tuned model $-1.6\pt$ behind baseline.

\input{figures/fig-resampling}

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:resampling-split}} exposes the underlying breakdown: oversampling short trajectories inserts 2,262 pure copy moments (out of 4,000 total test points) where target cell IDs directly replicate preceding tokens. On these copy moments, both baseline and model achieve 100\% accuracy, artificially inflating baseline scores by $+33.4\pt$. On the remaining 1,738 true decision moments where movement occurs, baseline accuracy is 45.9\% while model accuracy drops to 42.2\% (a net $-3.7\pt$ degradation; the model bar is derived from the reported 74.9\% assuming it never misses a copy, and stays below the baseline at either end of that assumption). Moreover, zero-movement copy step repetition compresses inter-user accuracy variance by a factor of 2.3, destroying the behavioral variance critical for subgroup stratification. Equalizing trajectory lengths distorts empirical transition distributions and degrades true decision quality.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:retro-scale}

Cohort scaling analysis demonstrates that next-cell prediction accuracy saturates at approximately 2,000 users under parameter-efficient fine-tuning (+4.2\,\% over baseline in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-ladder}}). Expanding training cohorts from 2,000 to 21,000 users yields negligible accuracy gains (+0.04\,\%, $p > 0.05$ in \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}}). Furthermore, individual user mobility typology accounts for a 22.4\,\% accuracy spread, demonstrating that individual user characterization exerts a far stronger influence on predictability than raw data scale.

Two mechanisms account for the shape of the curve: the gain rises while the smallest kind of user, $G2$, grows past about 100 members (56 at 400 users, 126 at 900), and it stops once the cells used by the test users are all known, 99 of 100 of them already at 400 users. The saturation was measured under a fixed budget of 8,000 steps, so at 21,000 users the model sees only 36\% of its data once; it is a statement about this budget, not about every budget. Finally, stretching every day to 200 records produced the highest absolute score of the project (74.9\%) and a worse result, 1.6\,\% behind the baseline, a reminder that only the distance to the baseline counts.
