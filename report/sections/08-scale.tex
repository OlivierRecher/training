\chapter{The impact of the number of users}
\label{ch:scale}

\section{The goal}

Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} ended on an uncomfortable piece of arithmetic. Four
hundred training people split across four kinds of user leaves the smallest kind
with a few dozen members, and a profile estimated from a few dozen people is
barely a profile at all.

If the mechanism that produced the project's only real gain depends on those
kinds being well populated, then the obvious next lever is the number of users.
Hence the question of this chapter: \emph{how many people does a model need
before the population itself is described, and what happens after that point?}

The question is unusually well posed on this data. Since the unit is one day per
person (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}), the only thing that can be increased is the
number of \emph{people}, never the amount of history per person. So this chapter
measures what knowing how a population moves is worth, and nothing else.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} Three sets of people of
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. The 400-person point is \Dmob, graded on 100 held-out
people against a 43.1\,\% rule. The 900 to 1{,}900-person points are \Dtwok,
graded on 100 held-out people against a 48.2\,\% rule. And the 5{,}000 to
21{,}000-person points are \Deleven, graded on \textbf{1{,}000 held-out people}
against a 50.2\,\% rule. In every case the test people are people the model has
never trained on, and because those three rules differ, this chapter never
compares two scores directly. It compares distances.

\section{Eight training sizes, everything else fixed}

The proper version of this experiment fixes everything except the number of
people. Same architecture, same settings, same cut of the day, hour symbols and
the four kinds of user active throughout, and, crucially, \emph{each size read
against the one-line rule measured on its own test people}. Eight sizes were
measured, from 400 training people up to 21{,}000.

The largest sizes required data built for the purpose: eleven day files of 2{,}000
people each, stacked into 22{,}000 person-days, of which 1{,}000 people are held
out and never trained on. The sizes are nested, so that a larger one is strictly
\emph{more} data and never \emph{other} data: the 21{,}000-person set is the
5{,}000-person set plus sixteen thousand more. Without that, a difference between
two sizes could have come from \emph{which} people were drawn rather than from
how many.

Over those sizes the number of records grows from 369{,}451 to 1{,}538{,}379 and
the number of available training slices\footnote{\textbf{Training slice}: one
continuous stretch of a person's day, used as a single training example. A long
day yields several and a short one just one, which is why the slice count grows
more slowly than the person count.} from 5{,}379 to 22{,}522, for a total of two
hours fifty-one minutes of computing time, forty-two minutes per size.

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-ladder} is the result, and it has three distinct regions.

The curve does not slow down. It stops.

\section{Is the flat part real, or is it noise?}

``The best size is followed by the worst'' is the signature of noise, but a
signature is not a test.

The test used is a \emph{paired resampling} of the 1{,}000 held-out people. A
thousand people are drawn at random from those thousand, with replacement so that
some appear twice and others not at all. Both sizes are then scored on exactly
that draw, and the difference between them is noted. The whole thing is repeated
two thousand times, and the middle 95\,\% of those two thousand differences is
the interval reported. Because the two sizes always see the same draw, the luck
of the sample cancels out and only the difference between the two models is left.
An interval containing zero means the two cannot be told apart.

Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap} gives them.

\input{tables/tab-bootstrap}

Every interval contains zero: each individual step, and the interval spanning the
entire range from 5{,}000 to 21{,}000 people, whose best estimate is 0.04.

Fitting a trend line to the same points\footnote{\textbf{Scaling law}: in the
literature on training large models \cite{kaplan2020scaling}, the observation
that error falls in a straight line when data and error are both plotted on
logarithmic scales.} asks how much a ten-fold increase in people buys. The answer
is 0.18 correct answers per hundred per ten-fold increase, which at that rate
would need on the order of 1.5 billion people to buy one further correct answer
per hundred, and the line accounts for only 12\,\% of the spread of the eight
measured points. So the ``law'' is fitting noise rather than a trend, and the
correct statement is not ``the gain is small'' but \textbf{``no gain was
measured''}.

Nor does anything hide behind the overall average. Table~\ref{tab:all-metrics}
takes the same sizes through six different measurements: top-1, top-3, top-5, the
cold-start case with only 30\,\% of the history, the middle person, and the worst
tenth of people. The spread across the whole range is between 0.2 and 1.3 on
every single line. Depth of ranking is flat. The hard tenth is flat. Cold start
is flat.

\input{tables/tab-all-metrics}

One line of that table is worth isolating, because it is the only place where
more data might have been expected to pay and where the model is genuinely more
useful. With only 30\,\% of a person's day as history, the model beats its rule by
4.6 against 4.3 at 80\,\%. Its edge is largest on people about whom little is
known, which is the operationally interesting case and also the one where the
identification of the kind of user is least reliable. But that edge, too, is
identical at 5{,}000 and at 21{,}000 people.

\section{What does move the score}

One thing does, and it is a property of the person rather than of the dataset:
which kind of user they are.

Evaluated with the 21{,}000-person model on the same held-out people, the four
kinds score 62.8\,\% for the stay-at-home user, who is 22\,\% of the people in
\seleven; 53.6\,\% for the steady user, 37\,\%; 43.0\,\% for the evening-moving
user, 22\,\%; and 40.4\,\% for the morning-moving user, 18\,\%. That is a spread
of \textbf{22.4 correct answers per hundred}.

The shares are not those measured on \smob{} in Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, since
these are twenty-one thousand different people, but the four shapes themselves
were essentially identical at every size. That is itself a result about scale:
5{,}000 people already describe this population completely, so there is nothing
left for the next sixteen thousand to add.

Put side by side: \textbf{22.4 against 0.4}. Who the person is outweighs how much
data was used to train, by a factor of more than fifty.

\section{Why it climbs, then stops}

Two mechanisms account for the two ends of the curve, and both are checkable.

\paragraph{Below the threshold, copying is simply the cheapest way to score well.}
On the 7{,}745 graded moments of the 100-person test set, 48 out of every hundred
ask for the same antenna again and 52 are an actual decision. Half the task
therefore falls without modelling anything at all. Before any of the 369 antenna
names means a place, the model has to learn them as symbols, and the middle
person offers around twenty-one candidate antennas over sixty-four events.
Meanwhile 400 training people split across four kinds of user and four stretches
of the day leaves roughly twenty-five examples per cell of that grid, and
twenty-five examples cannot tell a habit from a coincidence. A gain of 0.6 is the
signature of a model that has learned the format and the trivial habit and
nothing about behaviour, which is exactly what the checks of
Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} independently concluded.

\paragraph{The jump happens where the smallest kind of user becomes estimable.}
One mechanism fits the timing of the transition, and it is testable. On the data
the kinds were cut on, the evening-moving user is the least populated of the four
at 14\,\% of people. At 400 training people that is \textbf{56 people}; at 900,
\textbf{126}; at 1{,}900, \textbf{266}. It crosses roughly a hundred members
exactly between the two sizes where the gain jumps from 0.6 to 2.8. On the much
larger \seleven{} that same kind holds 22\,\% of people, which would move the
crossing earlier rather than later, so the argument does not turn on which of the
two shares is taken. A label can only carry information if the group it names has
enough inhabitants for its rhythm to be estimated at all; below that, the model is
right to ignore it, which is precisely what the failed run of
Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} showed it doing. Cross-training did not become a better
idea at 900 people than at 400. The kinds simply became large enough to be worth
reading.

\paragraph{And above the threshold, every new person is a behavioural duplicate.}
With 113 masts, four kinds of user, and every antenna in the test set already
seen during training at 5{,}000 people, the sixteen thousand additional people
bring no behaviour the model has not already seen several hundred times.

\section{Two limits of this experiment}

The flat half of the curve was measured at \emph{constant computing time}, and
that is a deliberate choice with a consequence.

Every size received the same budget: enough steps to read 8{,}000 training
slices, however many were available. At 5{,}000 people there are 5{,}379 slices,
so the run gets through all of them one and a half times. At 21{,}000 people
there are 22{,}522, so 8{,}000 are read and \textbf{14{,}522 are never read even
once}, which is 36\,\% coverage. The larger dataset was not added; it was spread
thinner. Separately, only about 2 numbers in every 100 were ever allowed to
change, and that fraction was never increased with the data either.

So the defensible conclusion is \emph{``with this much training time and this much
trainable capacity, 1{,}900 people are enough''}, and not ``more people can never
help''. Both statements would draw the same flat line, and one experiment would
tell them apart: give the largest dataset the training time its size deserves.
That run was not performed, and the claim is limited accordingly.

\section{What I conclude: the population is cheap, the person is not}

This chapter settles two things, and they point in the same direction.

\textbf{Data volume is a real lever over one narrow stretch and worthless above
it.} From roughly 400 to 1{,}900 training people the gain climbs from 0.6 to 4.2,
and above 1{,}900 it does not move at all: 0.1 across a ten-fold increase, with
zero inside every confidence interval and six different measurements agreeing.
For anyone who would pick this work up, that is an actionable number rather than
an abstract one. Two thousand people is the operating point; collecting ten times
more is not where the next improvement lies.

\textbf{And what difficulty there is lives in the person.} 22.4 correct answers
per hundred separate the four kinds of user, against 0.4 across the whole range
of training sizes. The ratio between those two numbers is more than fifty, and it
is the most robust finding in this report.

Read together, the two say where the remaining work is. It is not in collecting
more people, because that is exhausted at two thousand. It is in conditioning
better on who each person is, which is what produced every gain this project
measured, and specifically in doing so for somebody about whom little is yet
known. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} returns to that.

A third result belongs beside them, and the section that follows is given to it:
one way of using the same people better, levelling the number of records each of
them contributes, does everything it promises and still cannot be used here.

\section{A side experiment: levelling records instead of adding users}
\label{sec:levelling}

One alternative to adding people was tested in the same iteration, and it is
instructive enough to deserve its own section, both for what it delivers and for
why it does not work here.

The idea, adapted from a colleague's work, is that a few very long days dominate
training: with natural day lengths, the longest quarter of people carries 44.3\,\%
of the training positions,\footnote{\textbf{Training position}: one graded antenna
name in the training data. Somebody with 200 events contributes about 200 of them
and somebody with 20 contributes 20, so long days dominate simply by being long.}
so a fine-tune spends most of its budget learning the habits of the heaviest
users. The remedy is to stretch every day to exactly 200 records by repeating
each existing record a few times, spread evenly along the day rather than piled
up at one end.

\paragraph{What it delivers is exactly what it promises.} Measured on the 400
training people: the ratio between the longest and the shortest day goes from
200:19 to 1:1; the inequality of per-person weight\footnote{\textbf{Gini
coefficient}: a measure of inequality borrowed from economics, running from 0
when everybody contributes the same number of training positions to 1 when a
single person contributes all of them.} goes from a moderately unequal 0.294 to
\textbf{0}; the busiest tenth's share of training positions falls from 21.0\,\%
to 10.0\,\% and the busiest quarter's from 44.3\,\% to 25.0\,\%; and the number
of training positions rises from 34{,}408 to 80{,}000. Every person now weighs
exactly 0.25\,\% of the training. The diagnosis behind the technique is right,
and the technique does what it was designed to do.

\paragraph{What it does to the score is a warning about rulers.}
Figure~\ref{fig:resampling} shows the outcome. The model on levelled days reaches
74.9\,\%, which is twenty-eight above the natural-length run and by a wide margin
the highest absolute score in the whole project. On the same levelled days, the
one-line rule reaches 76.5\,\%. The distance changed sign: from 3.7 ahead to 1.6
behind.

\input{figures/fig-resampling}

The arithmetic is simple once seen. The reference protocol scores the last fifth
of each day, forty moments per person, 4{,}000 over the hundred test people, and
\textbf{2{,}262 of those 4{,}000 moments ask for a record that is a copy of the
one before}. Any rule that repeats is right on all of them by construction. Both
the model and the rule are handed those 2{,}262 free answers, which is why both
rose by about thirty. On the 1{,}738 moments that are real decisions, the rule
scores 45.9\,\% and the model between 42.2 and 44.8\,\% depending on how many
copies one assumes it misses, below the rule at either end, where the
natural-length run had been 3.7 above it.

The redistribution the technique intends is visible in the results, and it is paid
for exactly as one would fear: split by day length, the shortest quarter of people
comes out level, 1.1 behind on days that are 82\,\% copies, while the longest
quarter loses 3.6, on days that are 22\,\% copies. Weight was successfully
transferred from the people who had the most to say to the people who had the
least.

\paragraph{Why it is not conclusive, and why it could not be.} The deeper reason
is that copying destroys the very signal the four kinds of user are cut on. A
copied step never changes location, by construction. So on the training people
the average rate of location change falls from 0.508 to 0.189; the spread
\emph{between} people,\footnote{\textbf{Standard deviation}: the typical distance
of a value from its population's average, in the same units. It matters here
because an automatic sorting can only separate people who differ.} which is
exactly what the four kinds are carved out of, falls from 0.183 to 0.079, a
factor of 2.3; and the most mobile person in the data, who changed location at
every single step, drops from 1.00 to 0.56. The whole range compresses from
0.17--1.00 to 0.08--0.56, and every person drifts towards the stay-at-home
profile.

That is fatal for this particular pipeline, because identifying the kind of user
is the mechanism that produced the project's only real gain, and it needs
between-person spread to work at all. Levelling the records makes people look
alike, which makes the kinds harder to tell apart, which disables the
cross-training. The technique is not wrong and the imbalance it fixes is genuine,
but on this data and with this method it removes more information than it
redistributes.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-scale}

Finding the operating point, the number of training people beyond which nothing
further is bought, is the result of this chapter I am most satisfied with, because
it is a statement about the problem rather than about my pipeline. Knowing that
the curve stops at around two thousand people turns ``get more data'' from an
assumption into a settled question, and it is cheap knowledge: the eight sizes
together cost under three hours of computing time.

What I would change is \emph{when} it was measured, not how. Most of the
internship was spent improving the model on four hundred training people, a size
that this very curve later placed inside the imitation region, the one place where
a fine-tuned model cannot be told apart from the rule it is meant to beat.
Establishing the curve in the first weeks would have said so immediately, and the
work on the time of day and on the kinds of user could then have been carried out
at a size where those mechanisms have room to show an effect at all.

It did, at least, arrive in time to inform the final findings rather than after
them, and there is a reason the order was not simply wrong. The explanation for
the threshold, that the smallest kind of user crosses roughly a hundred members
exactly where the gain jumps, only exists because the kinds had already been built
and measured. Run first, the scaling study would have produced the curve without
the mechanism that explains it. Run last, it produced both.
