\chapter{The impact of the number of users}
\label{ch:scale}

\section{The goal}

Four hundred training people split across four kinds of user leaves the smallest
kind with a few dozen members, and a profile estimated from a few dozen people is
barely a profile at all. If the mechanism that produced the project's only real
gain depends on those kinds being well populated, then the obvious next lever is
the number of users. Hence the question: \emph{how many people does a model need
before the population itself is described, and what happens after that point?} The
question is unusually well posed on this data, because the unit is one day per
person, so the only thing that can be increased is the number of \emph{people},
never the amount of history per person.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} Three sets of people of
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. The 400-person point is \Dmob, graded on 100 held-out
people against a 43.1\,\% rule. The 900 to 1{,}900-person points are \Dtwok,
graded on 100 held-out people against a 48.2\,\% rule. And the 5{,}000 to
21{,}000-person points are \Deleven, graded on \textbf{1{,}000 held-out people}
against a 50.2\,\% rule. In every case the test people are people the model has
never trained on, and because those three rules differ, this chapter never compares
two scores directly. It compares distances.

\section{Eight training sizes, everything else fixed}

The proper version of this experiment fixes everything except the number of people.
Same architecture, same settings, same cut of the day, hour symbols and the four
kinds of user active throughout, and each size read against the one-line rule
measured on its own test people.

The largest sizes required data built for the purpose: eleven day files of 2{,}000
people each, stacked into 22{,}000 person-days, of which 1{,}000 are held out. The
sizes are nested, so a larger one is strictly \emph{more} data and never
\emph{other} data; without that, a difference between two sizes could have come
from \emph{which} people were drawn rather than from how many. Over those sizes the
number of records grows from 369{,}451 to 1{,}538{,}379 and the number of available
training slices\footnote{\textbf{Training slice}: one continuous stretch of a
person's day, used as a single training example.} from 5{,}379 to 22{,}522, for a
total of two hours fifty-one minutes of computing time.

\subsection{What does move the score}

One thing does, and it is a property of the person rather than of the dataset:
which kind of user they are. Evaluated with the 21{,}000-person model on the same
held-out people, the four kinds score 62.8\,\% for the stay-at-home user, who is
22\,\% of the people in \seleven; 53.6\,\% for the steady user, 37\,\%; 43.0\,\%
for the evening-moving user, 22\,\%; and 40.4\,\% for the morning-moving user,
18\,\%. That is a spread of \textbf{22.4 correct answers per hundred}. The shares
are not those measured on \smob{} in Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, since these are
twenty-one thousand different people, but the four shapes themselves were
essentially identical at every size, which is itself a result about scale.

Put side by side: \textbf{22.4 against 0.4}. Who the person is outweighs how much
data was used to train, by a factor of more than fifty.

Below the threshold, copying is simply the cheapest way to score well. On the
7{,}745 graded moments of the 100-person test set, 48 out of every hundred ask for
the same antenna again, so half the task falls without modelling anything.
Meanwhile 400 training people split across four kinds of user and four stretches of
the day leaves roughly twenty-five examples per cell of that grid, and twenty-five
examples cannot tell a habit from a coincidence. A gain of 0.6 is the signature of
a model that has learned the format and the trivial habit and nothing about
behaviour, which is exactly what the checks of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}
independently concluded.

\subsection{Two limits of this experiment}
\label{sec:scale-limits}

The flat half of the curve was measured at \emph{constant computing time}, and that
is a deliberate choice with a consequence. Every size received the same budget:
enough steps to read 8{,}000 training slices, however many were available. At
5{,}000 people there are 5{,}379, so the run gets through all of them one and a
half times. At 21{,}000 there are 22{,}522, so 8{,}000 are read and \textbf{14{,}522
are never read even once}, which is 36\,\% coverage. The larger dataset was not
added; it was spread thinner. Separately, only about 2 numbers in every 100 were
ever allowed to change, and that fraction was never increased with the data either.

So the defensible conclusion is \emph{``with this much training time and this much
trainable capacity, 1{,}900 people are enough''}, and not ``more people can never
help''. Both statements would draw the same flat line, and one experiment would tell
them apart: give the largest dataset the training time its size deserves. That run
was not performed, and the claim is limited accordingly.

\section{What I conclude: the population is cheap, the person is not}

\textbf{Data volume is a real lever over one narrow stretch and worthless above
it.} From roughly 400 to 1{,}900 training people the gain climbs from 0.6 to 4.2,
and above 1{,}900 it does not move at all: 0.1 across a ten-fold increase, with zero
inside every confidence interval and six different measurements agreeing. For
anyone who would pick this work up, two thousand people is the operating point.

\textbf{And what difficulty there is lives in the person.} 22.4 correct answers per
hundred separate the four kinds of user, against 0.4 across the whole range of
training sizes. The ratio between those two numbers is more than fifty, and it is
the most robust finding in this report.

Read together, the two say where the remaining work is. It is not in collecting
more people, because that is exhausted at two thousand. It is in conditioning
better on who each person is, and specifically in doing so for somebody about whom
little is yet known.

\section{A side experiment: levelling records instead of adding users}
\label{sec:levelling}

One alternative to adding people was tested in the same iteration. The idea,
adapted from a colleague's work, is that a few very long days dominate training:
with natural day lengths, the longest quarter of people carries 44.3\,\% of the
training positions,\footnote{\textbf{Training position}: one graded antenna name in
the training data. Somebody with 200 events contributes about 200 of them and
somebody with 20 contributes 20, so long days dominate by being long.} so a
fine-tune spends most of its budget learning the habits of the heaviest users. The
remedy is to stretch every day to exactly 200 records by repeating each existing
record a few times, spread evenly along the day.

It delivers exactly what it promises. On the 400 training people the ratio between
the longest and the shortest day goes from 200:19 to 1:1 and the inequality of
per-person weight\footnote{\textbf{Gini coefficient}: a measure of inequality
borrowed from economics, running from 0 when everybody contributes the same number
of training positions to 1 when a single person contributes all of them.} from
0.294 to \textbf{0}.

\input{figures/fig-resampling}

The redistribution is paid for exactly as one would fear: the shortest quarter of
people comes out level, 1.1 behind on days that are 82\,\% copies, while the
longest quarter loses 3.6 on days that are 22\,\% copies. Weight was transferred
from the people who had the most to say to the people who had the least. The
technique is not wrong and the imbalance it fixes is genuine, but on this data it
removes more information than it redistributes.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-scale}

Finding the operating point is the result I am most satisfied with, because it is a
statement about the problem rather than about my pipeline, and the eight sizes
together cost under three hours of computing time. What I would change is
\emph{when} it was measured: most of the internship was spent improving the model
on four hundred training people, a size this very curve later placed inside the
imitation region. There is one reason the order was not simply wrong, which is that
the explanation for the threshold only exists because the kinds had already been
built and measured.
