\chapter{Impact of Individual User Characteristics on Predictability}
\label{ch:user}

\section{Objectives and Predictability Determinants}

Identical fine-tuned models exhibit extreme performance variance across individual subscribers, yielding near 100\% prediction accuracy for certain individuals while scoring near zero for others. This variance indicates that aggregate population accuracy is incomplete without individual trajectory breakdown. The objective of this chapter is to resolve the following question: \emph{which intrinsic trajectory properties determine individual predictability?} If predictability depends primarily on hyperparameter tuning, model capacity should be increased. If predictability depends on individual subscriber mobility traits, aggregate performance is fundamentally constrained prior to training. The empirical results presented in this chapter demonstrate that individual subscriber characteristics dictate overall predictability.

\paragraph{Data Provenance.} Analysis uses two dataset cohorts from \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:datasets}}: raw daily logs evaluated on 1,173 subscribers, and active mobility logs (\Dmob{}) evaluating 400 training subscribers and \textbf{100 held-out subscribers}.

\section{Correlation Analysis of Individual User Features}

To identify trajectory features governing predictability, five subscriber-level candidate metrics computable directly from individual traces were evaluated: cell transition frequency, primary cell concentration, distinct cell count, transition temporal entropy, and total log event volume.

Each candidate metric was correlated with individual model prediction accuracy using the \textbf{Pearson correlation coefficient} ($r$), formulated according to Pearson \textbf{\cite{pearson1895correlation}}:

\begin{equation}
  r \;=\;
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})\,(y_i - \bar{y})}
       {\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\;
        \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
  \label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}

where $n$ represents subscriber count, $x_i$ denotes candidate feature value for subscriber $i$, $y_i$ represents top-1 prediction accuracy for subscriber $i$, and $\bar{x}, \bar{y}$ denote sample means.

\bigskip

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:concentration}} ranks the candidate metrics by their Pearson coefficient $r$, measured on 1,173 subscribers of \Draw{} (dark blue) and on 100 held-out subscribers of \Dmob{} (teal); a bar pointing left means that more of the property goes with lower accuracy. The primary determinant of predictability is cell transition frequency, formalized as the \textbf{cell-switch rate}: the proportion of consecutive event records in a subscriber trajectory that register a change in cell identifier, near 0\% for stationary subscribers and approaching 100\% for highly mobile ones. It correlates inversely with prediction accuracy, at $r = -0.971$ on \Draw{} and $r = -0.773$ on \Dmob{}: \emph{higher cell transition frequency goes with lower prediction accuracy}. The first value is close to arithmetic, since a subscriber's cell-switch rate is the mirror image of persistence baseline accuracy on that subscriber, so any model imitating the baseline earns a strong negative coefficient before anything is learned. Primary cell concentration ($r = +0.696$) and transition temporal entropy ($r = -0.811$) reflect the same underlying repetitiveness from other angles. The remaining features stay below $|r| = 0.24$: unique cell count ($r = -0.232$), prediction instance count ($r = -0.101$) and total trajectory length ($r = +0.054$) exert negligible influence on individual predictability.

To verify that individual difficulty reflects intrinsic subscriber behavior rather than an artifact of the evaluation prefix length, cross-context stability was evaluated: individual accuracy at a 30\% context fraction and at an 80\% context fraction correlate at $r = 0.77$ (\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:context-collapse}} in Chapter~\ref{ch:time}), confirming that predictability is an intrinsic property of the subscriber trajectory.

\subsection{Performance Breakdown across Restlessness Segments}

On \Draw{} (\textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{a}}), the lowest quintile, subscribers who never change cell ID, is predicted at 100\% by both the model and the baseline, while the highest quintile plateaus at 34.4\%: 65 correct answers per hundred separate the two ends of a single population under one trained model. Across the three lowest quintiles the two bars are virtually identical. The model pulls ahead only in the two highest quintiles, by +3.7\,\% (56.5\% vs.\ 52.8\%) and +7.8\,\% (34.4\% vs.\ 26.6\%), and these hold only 18.5\% of the evaluated prediction instances, the highest quintile alone 5.6\%. A +7.8\,\% improvement on 5.6\% of the evaluation volume barely shifts aggregate accuracy. On the independent held-out cohort of \textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{b}}, the ordering is the same but the result is negative: the model trails the baseline in the three lower mobility quartiles ($-7.1$\,\%, $-5.3$\,\% and $-3.4$\,\%) and draws level ($+0.6$\,\%) only in the highest. This establishes a key reporting convention: \emph{aggregate population accuracy averages distinct mobility cohorts; reporting segment-specific accuracy relative to baseline benchmarks is required to capture true model contributions.}

\section{Impact of User Activity Volume on Aggregate Metrics}

Evaluating prediction volume distribution reveals significant inequality across subscribers. Partitioning 1,173 subscribers into quintiles based on event volume demonstrates that the highest-volume decile (117 subscribers) contributes \textbf{58.6\%} of the 54,173 total evaluated prediction instances. Consequently, unweighted aggregate accuracy metrics are heavily dominated by this sub-population.

However, event volume does not correlate strongly with prediction accuracy ($r = +0.154$). Higher event volume corresponds to slightly lower cell-switch rates ($r = -0.150$), explaining the minor upward accuracy trend in high-volume cohorts.

\section{Analysis of Cell-Level vs. Mast-Level Prediction Errors}

Analyzing error cases at individual subscriber resolution reveals that a portion of prediction errors stems from cell-level vs. base-station-level definitions.

\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:worst-users}} lists the seven lowest-scoring subscribers of the whole test set on \Dfilt{}, every one at or below 12.5\% accuracy. \texttt{user\_id} is the anonymous number the operator assigned to the subscriber for that day, \textbf{Moments} the number of evaluated moments they contribute, and \textbf{Ceiling} the best score reachable given the cells present in their own history. These worst cases fall into three distinct failure modes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Low record volume} (rows 1--4): subscribers contributing between 2 and 12 evaluation moments, where small samples produce high variance (3 moments can only yield 0\%, 33\%, 67\% or 100\%).
  \item \textbf{Genuine trajectory change} (row 5): a learnable behavioral shift that both baseline and model miss (ceiling 89\%, baseline 67\%, model 11.1\%).
  \item \textbf{Intra-site sector hand-offs} (rows 6--7): subscribers oscillating between adjacent sector antennas of the same physical mast (e.g.\ \cid{UKVCHE102} and \cid{UKVCHE104}). The ceiling is 100\% and the baseline reaches 50\%--67\% by repeating sector IDs, while the model, attempting to forecast the sector switches, scores 0\% despite zero physical displacement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:retro-user}

Three empirical findings summarize this chapter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Individual trajectory predictability is dictated primarily by cell-switch frequency (repetitiveness).
  \item Fine-tuned language model accuracy gains concentrate within high mobility cohorts representing a minor fraction of total evaluation events.
  \item Intra-site sector transitions account for a systematic fraction of cell-level prediction errors.
\end{enumerate}

Because scaling general capacity does not alter low-mobility baseline bounds, improving high-mobility transition forecasting requires temporal conditioning signals (hour of day) to anticipate transitions.

This chapter demonstrated that trajectory predictability is primarily governed by subscriber mobility characteristics rather than model parameter scale. Cell-switch rate represents the single strongest predictor of accuracy ($r = -0.773$), whereas trajectory length and unique cell counts exhibit minimal correlation ($|r| < 0.24$).

Model accuracy gains over persistence baselines concentrate within high mobility cohorts (+7.8\,\% in the highest cell-switch quintile of \textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{a}}). Because high mobility subscribers generate a minority of total event volume, aggregate population accuracy shifts marginally. These findings establish the necessity of reporting cohort-stratified accuracy deltas throughout mobility prediction research.
