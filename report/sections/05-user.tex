\chapter{The person sets the score, not the model}
\label{ch:user}

\section{The goal}

The same trained model, on the same run, predicts one person perfectly and
another not at all. That observation makes an average score almost uninformative,
and it replaces the project's question with a sharper one: \emph{what property of
a person decides whether that person is predictable?}

If the answer is a property of the model, there is a model to improve. If it is a
property of the person, then the average score is largely fixed before any
training begins, and the only honest way to report progress is to say \emph{where
in the population} it happened. This chapter establishes that it is the second.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} Two sets of people, both
described in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. The first is a day file taken as it comes,
on which 1{,}173 people were scored. The second is \Dmob, where 400 people were
used for training and \textbf{100 different people}, never seen during training,
were used for grading. Wherever a figure below says ``100 held-out people'', it
means those hundred strangers. Every score is obtained exactly as
Chapter~\ref{ch:first} describes: each graded moment is answered, marked right or
wrong, and the percentage is the count of right answers over the count of
moments.

\section{Which property of a person decides it}

The candidates are all things that can be computed from a person's own day
without any model: how often they change antenna, how much of their time is spent
on one dominant antenna, how many different antennas they visit, how irregular
their changes are, and how many records they have.

Each candidate was compared against that person's own score using the
\textbf{Pearson correlation}, written $r$. It is a single number saying how
tightly two quantities move together across a population:

\begin{equation}
  r \;=\;
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})\,(y_i - \bar{y})}
       {\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\;
        \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
  \label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}

Here $n$ is the number of people; $x_i$ is the candidate property measured on
person $i$, for instance how often they change antenna; $y_i$ is that same
person's score; and $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the averages of those two
quantities over everybody. The numerator is large when people who are above
average on one quantity are also above average on the other, and the denominator
simply rescales the result so that it always lands between $-1$ and $+1$.

Reading it is straightforward. \textbf{$r = +1$} means the two quantities rise
together in perfect lockstep. \textbf{$r = -1$} means one falls exactly as the
other rises. \textbf{$r = 0$} means they have nothing to do with each other.
Figure~\ref{fig:concentration} gives $r$ for every candidate.

The ranking is not close, and the winner needs a definition before it can be
used.

\paragraph{What a cell-switch rate is.} Take one person's day and walk through
their records in order, comparing each one with the one just before it. The
\textbf{cell-switch rate} is simply the share of those comparisons in which the
antenna is not the same. Out of every hundred back-to-back records, how many land
on a different antenna? A person who spends the whole day at home and produces
sixty records from the same antenna has a switch rate near 0\,\%. A person
constantly on the move has one near 100\,\%. It is a measure of restlessness, and
it is computed from the person's own day, with no model involved.

That quantity reaches $r = -0.971$ on the 1{,}173 people of the raw day, and
$-0.773$ on the 100 held-out people of \Dmob, a different set of people with a
different rule. The sign is negative, which means exactly what one would expect:
\emph{the more often somebody changes antenna, the worse they are predicted}. The
two other strong rows, how spread out a person's time is over their
antennas\footnote{\textbf{Normalised entropy}: how spread out a person's time is
over the antennas they visit, on a scale where 0 means they never left one and 1
that they split their time evenly between all of them.} ($-0.811$) and how much
of it sits on a single dominant antenna ($+0.696$), are the same information seen
from other angles.

Two things have to be said about that $-0.971$ before it is used for anything,
because taken alone it is the weakest number in this chapter.

The first is that it is very nearly arithmetic. The cell-switch rate is, by
construction, the mirror image of how often ``repeat the last antenna'' is
correct on the same moments: a person who changes antenna once in ten steps hands
the one-line rule nine easy answers out of ten. Any predictor that behaves like
that rule is therefore guaranteed a strong negative $r$ against the switch rate
before anything at all is measured.

The second is that this particular model was behaving like it. Both measurements
were taken on runs scoring at or below their own rule, so what the coefficient
really quantifies is how closely the run was imitating the thing it was supposed
to beat. That reading is more useful than the one I gave it at the time: a model
that genuinely helped the restless minority would show a \emph{weaker}
coefficient, because it would break the lockstep between somebody's switch rate
and their score. Nobody re-measured it once the change of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}
finally cleared the rule; it remains the cheapest unfinished check in the
project.

What the figure does establish, and what no argument about tautology can take
away, is the negative half of its ranking. The number of distinct antennas
visited, the number of predictions contributed and the raw length of the day all
stay below $0.24$ in absolute value. The intuitive explanations of why one person
is harder than another, that they simply have less history or a wider repertoire
of places, are eliminated in a single pass. Repetitiveness is the only candidate
left standing.

\subsection*{The same finding, without any correlation}

Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate} makes the point again without relying on $r$ at all,
simply by sorting people by their switch rate and cutting the sorted list into
equal groups.\footnote{\textbf{Quintile}: one fifth of the population, obtained by
sorting people on some quantity and cutting the sorted list into five equal
parts. \textbf{Quartile} is the same with four.}

\input{figures/fig-switch-rate}

The fifth of people who \emph{never} change antenna are predicted at 100\,\%, by
the model and by the one-line rule alike, because for them the task is to notice
that nothing is happening. At the other end, the most restless fifth plateaus at
34.4\,\%. That is a spread of \textbf{65 correct answers per hundred}, produced
entirely by who the people are, on one single trained model, with no setting
involved anywhere.

One objection has to be closed before any of that can be called a property of the
person rather than of the ruler. Perhaps these people are only hard because of
where their day happened to be cut. It is closed by a measurement made two
chapters later, in Table~\ref{tab:context-collapse}: the same person's score with
30\,\% of their day in context and with 80\,\% of it correlates at $r = 0.77$.
Somebody who is hard when the model has seen a third of their day is still hard
when it has seen four fifths. Difficulty travels with the person, not with the
amount of history handed over, and that is what makes it a trait rather than an
artefact.

\section{Where the model does help, and why it does not show}

The same figure carries the second half of the finding, and it is the more useful
half.

In the three easiest groups the model and the rule are indistinguishable: 100.0
against 100.0, 87.6 against 88.4, 71.6 against 71.7. It is only in the two hardest
groups that the trained model pulls ahead, by 3.7 and then by 7.8 correct answers
per hundred. The model's contribution is real, it is consistent, and it lives
exactly where repetition stops working.

It also lives exactly where the volume is not. Those two groups hold 18.5\,\% of
all the graded moments, and the most restless group alone, the one where the
model gains 7.8, holds 5.6\,\%. Winning eight answers per hundred on one
twentieth of the volume moves the overall average by less than half an answer per
hundred.

The consequence is a reporting rule rather than a modelling one, and it is
applied from this chapter onward: \emph{an average score on this task is a
weighted mixture of segments with wildly different difficulty, and quoting it
alone hides the only part of the result that is about the model.}

Panel (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate} makes the same point on the 100 held-out
people of \Dmob, and makes it less comfortably. There the model \emph{loses} to
the rule in the three easier quarters, by 7.1, 5.3 and 3.4, and only draws level
in the most restless one, by 0.6. Same ordering, same mechanism, and a negative
result overall. That is precisely how a model can be genuinely useful on hard
people and still be worse than one line of code on average.

\section{A tenth of the people decide the average}

If the score varies that much between people, then how much each person weighs in
the average matters. The weights turn out to be extremely uneven.

Splitting the same 1{,}173 people into five equal groups by how many predictions
each of them contributes, the middle person contributes eleven predictions while
the busiest tenth, 117 people, carries \textbf{58.6\,\%} of the 54{,}173 graded
moments. Any average on this data is, to a first approximation, the score of
those 117 people.

The tempting conclusion, that having more records makes somebody more
predictable, is not supported. The score is almost flat across the five volume
groups, and the raw correlation between volume and score is only $r = +0.154$.
What actually happens is that people with many records tend to change antenna
\emph{less} often, $r = -0.15$ between the two, so the mild upward tilt at the
busy end is the switch-rate finding wearing a different label.

This matters practically. It means that levelling the number of records per
person cannot be expected to redistribute difficulty, which is exactly the
prediction that Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} eventually tests head on.

\section{What the score counts, and what it deliberately does not}

Looking at people one by one also exposes something an average cannot: part of
the remaining error is not a modelling failure at all, but a deliberate property
of the measurement.

Table~\ref{tab:worst-users} lists \textbf{the seven worst-scoring people} on
\Dfilt, every one of them at or below 12.5\,\%, and behind that single bad number
sit three completely different situations.

\input{tables/tab-worst-users}

The one that mattered for the rest of the internship is the last pair. Those two
people oscillate between near-twin antennas of the \emph{same} mast,
\cid{UKVCHE102} and \cid{UKVCHE104} for instance, so plain repetition scores 50
to 67\,\% on them while the model, which tries to choose between the two names,
scores nothing at all. It is being marked wrong for failing a distinction that
involves no movement whatsoever.

This is where a decision had to be made, and it is worth stating explicitly
because it looks like an oversight and is not one. \emph{Movement}, for every
behavioural quantity in this report, is measured on the mast: that is what makes
the four kinds of user of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} meaningful. The \emph{prediction
target}, however, remains the full antenna name. Two reasons. It is the question
that was actually asked, since an operator wants to know which antenna will serve
the person, not merely which mast. And coarsening the target halfway through the
internship would have made every number measured before that point incomparable
with every number measured after it, which on a project whose entire discipline
is ``compare only against the matching rule'' is a price not worth paying.

The consequence is accepted rather than hidden: a small, known share of the
reported error is the model being marked wrong for a difference that is not a
movement.

\section{What I conclude, and where it leads}

Three statements survive this chapter, and they constrain everything after it.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The score of a person is set by how repetitive their movement
        already is, and by nothing else I measured.} It is a trait rather than an
        artefact of the protocol: the same person's score with a third of their
        day in context and with four fifths of it correlates at $0.77$, so their
        difficulty is fixed before any model sees them.
  \item \textbf{The model's real contribution is concentrated in the restless
        minority}, and that minority owns a small share of the graded volume, so
        the average can barely move even when the contribution is real. Eight
        correct answers per hundred on 5.6\,\% of the volume is worth less than
        half an answer per hundred overall.
  \item \textbf{A known share of the remaining error is not a modelling failure},
        but the model being marked wrong for a distinction between two names of
        the same physical place, a cost accepted deliberately so that results stay
        comparable across the whole internship.
\end{enumerate}

Where that leads is the important part, and it decided the second half of the
internship. Taken together, these three statements say that \emph{the lever is
not capacity}. Making the model bigger, or training it for longer, would improve
predictions in the segment that repetition already solves for free, which is the
segment that owns most of the volume and none of the difficulty. What was needed
instead was a signal that helps specifically \emph{at the moment a person is
about to move}. The most obvious candidate for such a signal is the time of day,
and that is what Chapter~\ref{ch:time} tries.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-user}

What I would change here is what I put first. The chapter as I presented it at
the time led with $r = -0.971$, and a coefficient that is almost arithmetic
cannot carry a chapter, however striking it looks.

The measurement that genuinely changed what I did next is the
\emph{distribution}. One fifth of the people are predicted perfectly by doing
nothing at all; one fifth plateau at 34.4\,\%; and the average is a weighted
mixture of the two, with the weights concentrated in 117 people out of 1{,}173.
That is why every chapter after this one reports numbers per segment rather than
a single score. The correlation was the headline; the histogram was the finding.

The other thing I would do differently costs one evaluation pass and I never ran
it: re-measuring that coefficient after the model finally beat the rule, which
would have turned the least informative number in the chapter into a test of the
project's main claim.
