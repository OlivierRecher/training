\chapter{Impact of Individual User Characteristics on Predictability}
\label{ch:user}

\section{Objectives and Predictability Determinants}

Identical fine-tuned models exhibit extreme performance variance across individual users, yielding near 100\% prediction accuracy for certain individuals while scoring near zero for others. This variance indicates that aggregate population accuracy is incomplete without individual trajectory breakdown. The objective of this chapter is to resolve the following question: \emph{which intrinsic trajectory properties determine individual predictability?} If predictability depends primarily on hyperparameter tuning, model capacity should be increased. If predictability depends on individual user mobility traits, aggregate performance is fundamentally constrained prior to training. The empirical results presented in this chapter demonstrate that individual user characteristics dictate overall predictability.

\paragraph{Data Provenance.} Analysis uses two dataset cohorts from \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:datasets}}: raw daily logs evaluated on 1,173 users, and active mobility logs (\Dmob{}) evaluating 400 training users and \textbf{100 held-out users}.

\section{Correlation Analysis of Individual User Features}

To identify trajectory features governing predictability, five user-level candidate metrics computable directly from individual traces were evaluated: cell transition frequency, primary cell concentration, distinct cell count, transition temporal entropy, and total log event volume.

Each candidate metric was correlated with individual model prediction accuracy using the \textbf{Pearson correlation coefficient} ($r$), formulated according to Pearson \textbf{\cite{pearson1895correlation}}:

\begin{equation}
  r \;=\;
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})\,(y_i - \bar{y})}
       {\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\;
        \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
  \label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}

where $n$ represents user count, $x_i$ denotes candidate feature value for user $i$, $y_i$ represents top-1 prediction accuracy for user $i$, and $\bar{x}, \bar{y}$ denote sample means.

\bigskip

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:concentration}} ranks the candidate metrics by their Pearson coefficient $r$, measured on 1,173 users of \Draw{} (dark blue) and on 100 held-out users of \Dmob{} (teal); a bar pointing left means that more of the property goes with lower accuracy. The primary determinant of predictability is cell transition frequency, formalized as the \textbf{cell-switch rate}: the proportion of consecutive event records in a user trajectory that register a change in cell identifier, near 0\% for stationary users and approaching 100\% for highly mobile ones. It correlates inversely with prediction accuracy, at $r = -0.971$ on \Draw{} and $r = -0.773$ on \Dmob{}: \emph{higher cell transition frequency goes with lower prediction accuracy}. The first value is close to arithmetic, since a user's cell-switch rate is the mirror image of persistence baseline accuracy on that user, so any model imitating the baseline earns a strong negative coefficient before anything is learned. Primary cell concentration ($r = +0.696$) and transition temporal entropy ($r = -0.811$) reflect the same underlying repetitiveness from other angles. The remaining features stay below $|r| = 0.24$: unique cell count ($r = -0.232$), prediction instance count ($r = -0.101$) and total trajectory length ($r = +0.054$) exert negligible influence on individual predictability.

\input{figures/fig-concentration}

In plain words, \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:concentration}} says one thing. Of all the properties measured, the one that best announces whether a user will be well predicted is how often that user changes cell: someone who stays on the same cell most of the day is easy to predict, someone who keeps switching is hard. The two other long bars, the time spent on the main cell and how spread out the time is, describe the same habit from another angle. The short bars say the opposite: how many records a user has, or how many different cells they visit, tells almost nothing about their score. Having more data on a user does not make that user easier to predict; moving less does.

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate}} tests this dependency without relying on a scalar coefficient: users are sorted by cell-switch rate and cut into equal cohorts, quintiles of 500 users on \Draw{} in \textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{a}} and quartiles of 100 held-out users on \Dmob{} in \textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{b}}, with mobility increasing from left to right. In each pair of bars, grey-blue is the persistence baseline and dark blue the fine-tuned model, both scored on exactly the same moments, and the number above the pair is the difference between them.

To verify that individual difficulty reflects intrinsic user behavior rather than an artifact of the evaluation prefix length, cross-context stability was evaluated: individual accuracy at a 30\% context fraction and at an 80\% context fraction correlate at $r = 0.77$ (\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:context-collapse}} in Chapter~\ref{ch:time}), confirming that predictability is an intrinsic property of the user trajectory.

\subsection{Performance Breakdown across Restlessness Segments}

On \Draw{} (\textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{a}}), users who never change cell are predicted perfectly, by the model and by the persistence baseline alike, while the most restless fifth gets only about one correct answer in three. In the three calmest fifths, the model and the baseline are level. The model pulls ahead only in the two most restless fifths, by +3.7\,\% and +7.8\,\%, and these hold fewer than one evaluated moment in five, so the gain barely moves the overall score. On the independent held-out set of \textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{b}}, the ordering is the same but the model stays behind the baseline, except in the most restless quarter where the two are level. The lesson is that an overall score mixes very different users, and the contribution of a model only becomes visible once the score is broken down by how much users move.

\section{Impact of User Activity Volume on Aggregate Metrics}

Evaluating prediction volume distribution reveals significant inequality across users. Partitioning 1,173 users into quintiles based on event volume demonstrates that the highest-volume decile (117 users) contributes \textbf{58.6\%} of the 54,173 total evaluated prediction instances. Consequently, unweighted aggregate accuracy metrics are heavily dominated by this sub-population.

However, event volume does not correlate strongly with prediction accuracy ($r = +0.154$). Higher event volume corresponds to slightly lower cell-switch rates ($r = -0.150$), explaining the minor upward accuracy trend in high-volume cohorts.

\section{Analysis of Cell-Level vs. Base-Station-Level Prediction Errors}

Analyzing error cases at individual user resolution reveals that a portion of prediction errors stems from cell-level vs. base-station-level definitions.

\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:worst-users}} lists the seven lowest-scoring users of the whole test set on \Dfilt{}, every one at or below 12.5\% accuracy. \texttt{user\_id} is the anonymous number the operator assigned to the user for that day, \textbf{Moments} the number of evaluated moments they contribute, \textbf{Ceiling} the best score reachable given the cells present in their own history, \textbf{Persistence baseline} the score of the baseline on that user, and \textbf{Result} the score of the model. These worst cases fall into three distinct failure modes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Low record volume} (rows 1--4): users contributing between 2 and 12 evaluation moments, where small samples produce high variance (3 moments can only yield 0\%, 33\%, 67\% or 100\%).
  \item \textbf{Genuine trajectory change} (row 5): a learnable behavioral shift that both baseline and model miss (ceiling 89\%, baseline 67\%, model 11.1\%).
  \item \textbf{Intra-site sector hand-offs} (rows 6--7): users oscillating between adjacent sector cells of the same base station (e.g.\ \cid{UKVCHE102} and \cid{UKVCHE104}). The ceiling is 100\% and the baseline reaches 50\%--67\% by repeating sector IDs, while the model, attempting to forecast the sector switches, scores 0\% despite zero physical displacement.
\end{enumerate}

\input{tables/tab-worst-users}

Read plainly, \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:worst-users}} shows that the worst scores of the test set are mostly not failures of the model. In the first four rows the user has so few moments that the score means little: with three moments, a single mistake costs a third of the score. In the fifth row the user really changed habits, and neither the baseline nor the model saw it coming; it is the only genuine prediction problem of the seven. In the last two rows the user did not move at all: the phone switched between two cells of the same base station, the baseline was right half the time or more by repeating the last name, and the model, trying to anticipate a switch, was always wrong.

Maintaining granular \cid{cell\_id} target prediction fulfills the cell-level forecasting mandate, even though a fraction of prediction errors represents intra-site sector handovers rather than geographical mobility.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:retro-user}

Three empirical findings summarize this chapter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Individual trajectory predictability is dictated primarily by cell-switch frequency (repetitiveness).
  \item Fine-tuned language model accuracy gains concentrate within high mobility cohorts representing a minor fraction of total evaluation events.
  \item Intra-site sector transitions account for a systematic fraction of cell-level prediction errors.
\end{enumerate}

Because scaling general capacity does not alter low-mobility baseline bounds, improving high-mobility transition forecasting requires temporal conditioning signals (hour of day) to anticipate transitions.

This chapter demonstrated that trajectory predictability is primarily governed by user mobility characteristics rather than model parameter scale. Cell-switch rate represents the single strongest predictor of accuracy ($r = -0.773$), whereas trajectory length and unique cell counts exhibit minimal correlation ($|r| < 0.24$).

Model accuracy gains over persistence baselines concentrate within high mobility cohorts (+7.8\,\% in the highest cell-switch quintile of \textbf{\panelref{fig:switch-rate}{a}}). Because high mobility users generate a minority of total event volume, aggregate population accuracy shifts marginally. These findings establish the necessity of reporting cohort-stratified accuracy deltas throughout mobility prediction research.
