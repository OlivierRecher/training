\chapter{The user sets the score, the model moves the residual}
\label{ch:user}

\section{The question}

The same trained model, on the same run, predicts one person perfectly and
another not at all. That observation makes the average accuracy almost
uninformative and replaces the project's question with a sharper one:
\emph{what property of a user decides whether that user is predictable?} If the
answer is a property of the model, there is a model to improve. If it is a
property of the person, then the aggregate score is largely fixed before any
training begins, and the only honest way to report progress is to say where in
the population it happened. This chapter establishes that it is the second.

\section{One quantity explains almost everything}

The candidate features are all derivable from a user's own sequence: how often
they change cell, how concentrated their visits are on one dominant cell, how
many distinct cells they visit, how irregular the switches are, how many records
they have. Correlating\footnote{\textbf{Pearson correlation} $r$: how tightly two quantities move together
across a population, between $-1$ and $+1$: \[ r \;=\; \frac{\sum_i
(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})} {\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2}\;\sqrt{\sum_i
(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}, \] with $x_i$ a feature of user $i$, $y_i$ that user's
accuracy and $\bar{x},\bar{y}$ the averages. $+1$ is perfect lockstep, $-1$
perfect opposition, $0$ no relation at all. Three users with switch rates
$0.1, 0.5, 0.9$ and accuracies $90, 50, 10\,\%$ give $r = -1$ exactly; real
data is never that clean, and $-0.971$ means it is close.} each of them against that user's own accuracy gives
Figure~\ref{fig:concentration}(b), and the ranking is not close. The
\textbf{cell-switch rate}, the share of consecutive steps at which the cell
changes, reaches $r = -0.971$ on the 1{,}173 evaluated users of \Drawbig{}
and is confirmed at $r = -0.773$ on the 100 held-out users of \Dmob, a
different corpus with a different baseline. Normalised entropy\footnote{\textbf{Normalised entropy}: how spread out a user's time is over the cells
they visit, rescaled to sit between 0 and 1. With $p_c$ the share of that
user's events in cell $c$ and $C$ the number of distinct cells they visited,
\[ H \;=\; \frac{-\sum_c p_c \log_2 p_c}{\log_2 C}. \] A user split evenly
between two cells has $H = 1$, maximally spread; one spending 90\,\% of their
time in one of the two has $H = 0.47$, concentrated and therefore easy to
predict; $H = 0$ means they never left one cell.} of the visited cells
($-0.811$) and concentration on a dominant cell ($+0.696$) are the same
information seen from other angles. Everything else, the number of distinct
cells, the number of predictions, the raw sequence length, is below $0.24$ in
absolute value.

Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate} shows what that correlation looks like when the
users are simply sorted by it. Splitting them into five equal groups\footnote{\textbf{Quintile}: one fifth of the population, obtained by sorting users
on some quantity and cutting the sorted list into five equal parts;
\textbf{quartile} and \textbf{decile} are the same with four and ten. ``The
most volatile fifth'' is a quintile, not everyone above a fixed threshold.} by switch rate, the fifth of users who \emph{never} change cell are
predicted at 100\,\%, by the model and by the baseline alike, because for them the task is
to notice that nothing is happening. At the other end, the most volatile fifth
plateaus at 34.4\,\%. That is a 65-point spread produced entirely by who the
users are, on a single trained model, with no hyperparameter involved.

\section{Where the model does add something, and why it does not show}

The same figure carries the second half of the finding, and it is the more
useful half. In the three easiest groups the model and the baseline are
indistinguishable: $100.0$ against $100.0$, $87.6$ against $88.4$, $71.6$
against $71.7$. It is only in the two hardest groups that the trained model
pulls ahead, by $+3.7\pt$ and then by $+7.8\pt$. The model's contribution is
real, it is consistent, and it lives exactly where repetition stops working.

It also lives exactly where the evaluation volume is not. Those two groups hold
18.5\,\% of the scored predictions, and the most volatile group alone, the one
where the model gains $7.8\pt$, holds 5.6\,\%. A gain of eight points on one
twentieth of the volume moves the aggregate by less than half a point. The
consequence is a reporting rule rather than a modelling one, and it is applied
from this chapter onward: an aggregate accuracy on this task is a weighted
average of segments with wildly different difficulty, and quoting it alone hides
the only part of the result that is about the model.

Panel~(b) of Figure~\ref{fig:switch-rate} makes the same point on a second,
independent corpus, and makes it less comfortably: on the 100 held-out users of
\Dmob{} the model \emph{loses} to the baseline in the three easier quartiles
($-7.1$, $-5.3$, $-3.4\pt$) and only draws level in the most volatile one
($+0.6\pt$). Same ordering, same mechanism, and a net negative aggregate,
which is precisely how a model can be genuinely useful on hard users and still
be worse than a one-line rule overall.

\section{The average is written by a tenth of the users}

If accuracy varies that much between users, the weights matter. They are
extremely uneven. Splitting the same 1{,}173 users into five equal groups by how
many predictions each contributes, Figure~\ref{fig:concentration}(a), the
median user contributes eleven predictions while the most active tenth, 117
people, carries \textbf{58.6\,\%} of the 54{,}173 scored predictions. Any
aggregate on this corpus is, to a first approximation, the score of those 117
people.

The tempting conclusion, that more records make a user more predictable,
is not supported. Accuracy is almost flat across the five volume groups, and the
raw correlation between volume and accuracy is $r = +0.154$. What actually
happens is that high-volume users tend to switch cell \emph{less} often
($r = -0.15$ between volume and switch rate), so the mild upward tilt at the
right of the panel is the switch-rate finding wearing a different label. This
matters practically: it means that levelling the number of records per user
cannot be expected to redistribute difficulty, which is the prediction that
Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} eventually tests directly.

\section{What the metric counts, and what it deliberately does not}

A per-user breakdown also exposes something the aggregate cannot: part of the
residual error is not a modelling failure but a deliberate property of the
measurement. Table~\ref{tab:worst-users} lists every user scoring at or below
12.5\,\% on \Dfilt, and behind that single bad number sit three different
situations.

\input{tables/tab-worst-users}

The one that mattered for the rest of the internship is the last pair. Those
users oscillate between near-twin cells of the \emph{same} antenna,
\cid{UKVCHE102} and \cid{UKVCHE104}, for instance, so plain repetition scores
50--67\,\% on them while the model, which tries to choose between the two names,
scores nothing. It is being penalised for failing a distinction that carries no
mobility whatsoever.

This is where a decision had to be made, and it is worth stating explicitly
because it looks like an oversight and is not one. Movement, for every
behavioural quantity in this report, is measured on the site root: that is what
makes the profiles of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} meaningful. The
\emph{prediction target}, however, remains the raw \cid{cell\_id}. Two reasons.
It is the question that was actually asked, an operator wants to know which
cell will serve the user, not merely which mast. And coarsening the target to
the site root partway through the internship would have made every number
measured before that point incomparable with every number measured after it,
which on a project whose entire discipline is ``compare only against the
matching baseline'' is a price not worth paying for a few points. The
consequence is accepted rather than hidden: a small, known share of the reported
error is the model being marked wrong for a difference that is not a movement.

\section{What the numbers said}

Three statements survive this chapter, and they constrain everything after it.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Per-user accuracy is explained almost entirely by how repetitive the
        user's mobility already is: $r = -0.971$ on one corpus, $-0.773$ on
        another. The difficulty of a user is fixed before any model sees them.
  \item The model's genuine contribution is concentrated in the volatile
        minority, and that minority owns a small share of the evaluation volume,
        so the aggregate can barely move even when the contribution is real.
  \item A known share of the residual error is the model being marked wrong for
        a distinction between two names of the same physical place, a cost
        accepted deliberately, so that results stay comparable across the whole
        internship.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together they say that the lever is not capacity. Making the model bigger
or training it longer would improve predictions in the segment that is already
solved by repetition. What was needed instead was a signal that helps
specifically \emph{when a user is about to move}.

\section{What I did next, and why}

The per-user diagnosis pointed at one. Scoring the same trained model with
progressively more context, a group of users does not merely fail to improve,
it collapses, from thirty or forty per cent down to zero, precisely when the
scored positions are the last ones of the day. A model that predicts a user
correctly in the morning and not at all in the evening is not short of capacity;
it is missing the fact that the evening is different from the morning. The
sequence given to the model contained no notion of time whatsoever.
Chapter~\ref{ch:time} adds it.

\section{Retrospective}
\label{sec:retro-user}

With hindsight I over-read the correlation. A coefficient of $r = -0.971$
between a user's cell-switch rate and that user's accuracy is close to a
tautology: the switch rate is, almost by construction, the complement of how
often ``repeat the last cell'' happens to be correct, so a strong negative
number was guaranteed before anything was measured. Presenting it as the finding
of the chapter gave it more weight than it can carry, and I would not lead with
it again.

What the measurement genuinely established is the \emph{distribution}, not the
correlation. One fifth of the users are predicted perfectly by doing nothing at
all; one fifth plateau at 34.4\,\%; and the aggregate is a weighted average of
the two, with the weights concentrated in 117 people out of 1{,}173. That is the
result that changed what I did next, it is why every chapter after this one
reports per-segment numbers rather than a single accuracy, and why the search
moved towards a signal that would help specifically at the positions where a
user is about to move. The correlation was the headline; the histogram was the
finding.
