\chapter{The person sets the score, not the model}
\label{ch:user}

\section{The goal}

The same trained model, on the same run, predicts one person perfectly and
another not at all. That makes an average score almost uninformative, and it
replaces the project's question with a sharper one: \emph{what property of a
person decides whether that person is predictable?} If the answer is a property
of the model, there is a model to improve. If it is a property of the person, the
average score is largely fixed before any training begins. This chapter
establishes that it is the second.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} Two sets of people, both
in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. The first is a day file taken as it comes, on which
1{,}173 people were scored. The second is \Dmob, where 400 people were used for
training and \textbf{100 different people}, never seen during training, for
grading. Wherever a figure says ``100 held-out people'', it means those hundred
strangers.

\section{Which property of a person decides it}

The candidates are all things computable from a person's own day without any
model: how often they change antenna, how much of their time is spent on one
dominant antenna, how many different antennas they visit, how irregular their
changes are, and how many records they have.

Each candidate was compared against that person's own score using the
\textbf{Pearson correlation}, written $r$. It is a single number saying how
tightly two quantities move together across a population:

\begin{equation}
  r \;=\;
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})\,(y_i - \bar{y})}
       {\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\;
        \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
  \label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}

Here $n$ is the number of people; $x_i$ is the candidate property measured on
person $i$, for instance how often they change antenna; $y_i$ is that same
person's score; and $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the averages of those two over
everybody. The numerator is large when people above average on one quantity are
also above average on the other, and the denominator rescales the result to land
between $-1$ and $+1$. So $r = +1$ means the two rise together in lockstep,
$r = -1$ that one falls exactly as the other rises, and $r = 0$ that they have
nothing to do with each other.

The winner needs a definition before it can be used. Take one person's day and
walk through their records in order, comparing each with the one just before. The
\textbf{cell-switch rate} is the share of those comparisons in which the antenna
is not the same. Somebody who spends the whole day at home has a rate near 0\,\%,
somebody constantly on the move near 100\,\%. It is a measure of restlessness,
computed from the person's own day with no model involved, and it reaches
$r = -0.971$ on the 1{,}173 people of the raw day and $-0.773$ on the 100 held-out
people of \Dmob: \emph{the more often somebody changes antenna, the worse they are
predicted}.

That $-0.971$ is the weakest number in this chapter, because it is very nearly
arithmetic: the switch rate is by construction the mirror image of how often
``repeat the last antenna'' is correct on the same moments, and this particular
model was behaving like that rule. What the coefficient really quantifies is how
closely the run was imitating the thing it was supposed to beat, and nobody
re-measured it once Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} finally cleared the rule.

What the figure does establish, and what no argument about tautology can take away,
is the negative half of its ranking. The number of distinct antennas visited, the
number of predictions contributed and the raw length of the day all stay below
$0.24$ in absolute value, so the intuitive explanations, that a hard person simply
has less history or a wider repertoire of places, are eliminated in a single pass.
Repetitiveness is the only candidate left standing.

One objection has to be closed before any of this can be called a property of the
person rather than of the ruler: perhaps these people are only hard because of
where their day happened to be cut. Table~\ref{tab:context-collapse}, in the next
chapter, closes it. The same person's score with 30\,\% of their day in context
and with 80\,\% of it correlates at $r = 0.77$, so difficulty travels with the
person, not with the amount of history handed over.

\subsection{Where the model does help, and why it does not show}

The same figure carries the second half of the finding, and it is the more useful
half. In the three easiest groups the model and the rule are indistinguishable. It
is only in the two hardest that the trained model pulls ahead, by 3.7 and then by
7.8 correct answers per hundred. The model's contribution is real, it is
consistent, and it lives exactly where repetition stops working.

It also lives exactly where the volume is not. Those two groups hold 18.5\,\% of
all graded moments, and the most restless group alone, where the model gains 7.8,
holds 5.6\,\%. Winning eight answers per hundred on one twentieth of the volume
moves the overall average by less than half an answer per hundred. The consequence
is a reporting rule rather than a modelling one, applied from this chapter onward:
\emph{an average score on this task is a weighted mixture of segments with wildly
different difficulty, and quoting it alone hides the only part of the result that
is about the model.}

\section{A tenth of the people decide the average}

If the score varies that much between people, then how much each person weighs in
the average matters, and the weights turn out to be extremely uneven. Splitting
the same 1{,}173 people into five equal groups by how many predictions each
contributes, the middle person contributes eleven predictions while the busiest
tenth, 117 people, carries \textbf{58.6\,\%} of the 54{,}173 graded moments. Any
average on this data is, to a first approximation, the score of those 117 people.

The tempting conclusion, that having more records makes somebody more predictable,
is not supported. The score is almost flat across the five volume groups, and the
raw correlation between volume and score is only $r = +0.154$. What actually
happens is that people with many records tend to change antenna \emph{less} often,
$r = -0.15$ between the two, so the mild upward tilt at the busy end is the
switch-rate finding wearing a different label. It also means that levelling the
number of records per person cannot be expected to redistribute difficulty, which
is exactly the prediction Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} tests head on.

\section{What the score counts, and what it deliberately does not}

Looking at people one by one also exposes something an average cannot: part of the
remaining error is not a modelling failure at all, but a deliberate property of
the measurement.

The pair at the bottom of that table is what mattered for the rest of the
internship. Those two people oscillate between near-twin antennas of the
\emph{same} mast, so plain repetition scores 50 to 67\,\% on them while the model,
which tries to choose between the two names, scores nothing at all.

The decision behind that looks like an oversight and is not one. \emph{Movement},
for every behavioural quantity in this report, is measured on the mast, which is
what makes the four kinds of user of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} meaningful. The
\emph{prediction target} remains the full antenna name, because that is the
question that was actually asked, and because coarsening it halfway through would
have made every number measured before that point incomparable with every number
after it. A small, known share of the reported error is therefore the model being
marked wrong for a difference that is not a movement.

\section{What I conclude, and where it leads}

Three statements survive this chapter. The score of a person is set by how
repetitive their movement already is, and by nothing else I measured. The model's
real contribution is concentrated in the restless minority, which owns a small
share of the graded volume, so the average can barely move even when the
contribution is real. And a known share of the remaining error is the model being
marked wrong for a distinction between two names of the same physical place.

Taken together they say that \emph{the lever is not capacity}. A bigger model, or a
longer training, would improve predictions in the segment that repetition already
solves for free, which owns most of the volume and none of the difficulty. What was
needed instead was a signal that helps specifically \emph{at the moment a person is
about to move}, and the most obvious candidate is the time of day.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-user}

I led this chapter, at the time, with $r = -0.971$, and a coefficient that is
almost arithmetic cannot carry a chapter however striking it looks. What genuinely
changed what I did next is the \emph{distribution}: one fifth of the people
predicted perfectly by doing nothing at all, one fifth plateauing at 34.4\,\%, and
an average concentrated in 117 people. The correlation was the headline; the
histogram was the finding.
