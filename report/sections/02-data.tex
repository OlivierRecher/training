\chapter{The data}
\label{ch:data}

\section{The goal}

Before anything can be predicted, one has to know what the data contains. So the
first question of this report is the modest one: \emph{what does one line of
this dataset represent, and what kind of claim can it support?}

Two answers shaped every experiment that follows. \textbf{A record is not a
movement}, and \textbf{the name of an antenna is not a place}. Both are the
reason several later results look surprising at first sight.

\section{What one line of the data looks like}

The material is fifteen daily files covering 12 to 26 March 2014 in the Karlovy
Vary study area of north-west Bohemia. They are plain text
files\footnote{\textbf{CSV}, \emph{comma-separated values}: the simplest tabular
text format, one row per line with the fields separated by a delimiter. Here the
delimiter is a semicolon.} in which \textbf{one line is one person for one day},
from midnight to midnight. Depending on the day a file holds between 80{,}217
and 99{,}504 lines; the day used for most of the work here, 12 March 2014, holds
85{,}946 people.

A line starts with eight fixed columns, then repeats a pair of values for as
many records as that person produced. The eight are an anonymous number standing
for the person, their age, their gender, an unexplained five-digit number, a
behaviour class, a ``home antenna'', an ``activity antenna'' and the number of
records that follow. Then, over and over, \textbf{the name of an antenna
followed by a time}, the time always coming just after the antenna it belongs
to, written as a number of seconds since midnight from 0 to 86{,}399. Dividing
it by 3{,}600 and dropping what remains gives the hour of the day.

Only the anonymous number, the record count and the antenna-and-time pairs are
used in this work. The rest carry known aberrations, including ages of 1078 and
negative values, and none of them is needed to predict a next antenna.

A record is written whenever a phone and a mast exchange a message, and that
includes the case where nothing happens at all: a mast asks every phone attached
to it to announce itself on a fixed cycle, typically every four hours on 2G and
every two on 3G,\footnote{\textbf{Re-signalling}, or \emph{periodic location
update}: the network asking every attached phone to announce itself on a fixed
cycle, so that it knows where to route an incoming call.} so that it knows where
to route an incoming call. Figure~\ref{fig:record-not-movement} is what that
does to three days.

\section{The rule I have to beat, and why it moves}

The \textbf{persistence baseline} is the rule ``the next antenna is the last one
seen''. No model, no training, no settings, and it is scored on exactly the same
moments as my model, in exactly the same way.

Its value is not a constant, and this is the part that is easy to miss. It is a
property of the people being scored. On a day file taken as it comes, where a
large share of people barely move, it is right 69.2\,\% of the time. Once the
data is restricted to people who actually move around, the very same rule falls
to 45.5\,\%. Nothing about the rule changed; the population did.

\section{The name of an antenna is not a place}

An antenna name such as \cid{BSOCHO1} looks like an opaque location code. It is
not, and this is the point at which the technical name for it can safely be
introduced: in the working files this string is called the
\textbf{\cid{cell\_id}}, and it is used with that name from here on.

\input{figures/fig-cellid-anatomy}

It decomposes into three parts: a letter giving the generation of radio, a
middle part naming the \emph{mast},\footnote{\textbf{Site root}: the middle
letters of a \cid{cell\_id}, naming the physical mast, \cid{KVCHE} in
\cid{UKVCHE102}. \textbf{Sector}: the trailing digits, naming which antenna of
that mast served the phone.} and trailing digits naming which antenna of that
mast was in use. The study area contains \textbf{369 distinct antennas over 113
masts}, 224 on 2G and 145 on 3G, so the same physical mast appears in the data
under three or four different names.

\input{figures/fig-transition-composition}

The consequence is measurable. Out of every hundred back-to-back records on the
day used for Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, 43.6 carry the very same antenna name,
44.0 are a genuine change of mast, and \textbf{12.4} fall in between: the phone
re-attached to a different antenna of the \emph{same} mast without the person
moving at all.

Every measurement of \emph{movement} in this report is therefore taken on the
mast. The \emph{prediction target} remains the raw antenna name, because that is
what the original question asks for, and coarsening it to the mast would be
answering an easier question than the one posed.

\section{From a raw file to training material}

\input{figures/fig-data-flow}

Two choices inside that pipeline change how later results should be read.

\paragraph{The split is by person, and it is balanced.} Training and test people
are different people: the model is graded only on strangers. The assignment is
not random but \emph{balanced} on the number of records per
person,\footnote{\textbf{Stratified split}: people are first sorted into bands by
how many records they have, and the split is made inside each band.} and the two
sides are then compared to confirm they really are consistent. Without that, a
split can silently hand all the longer histories to one side.

\paragraph{Cleaning removes people, not records.} Three filtering decisions were
made over the internship, each in response to a result rather than in advance.
The earliest runs used the day file exactly as it came, which is why the rule sat
at 69.2\,\%: a large share of people produce a handful of records from a single
antenna, so the evaluation was dominated by moments where there was nothing to
predict. Then people with ten records or fewer, and people with a hole longer
than four hours, were removed; four hours is the corpus's own convention, since a
mast re-signals at least that often, so a longer gap means the person left the
recording area rather than sat still inside it. Finally the data was restricted
to people who move and stay in the area, using the per-day selection prepared by
a colleague. That is where the rule drops to the mid-forties, and it is the data
on which most of the modelling rests.

Each step makes the \emph{task} harder and the \emph{rule} weaker, which is
exactly the point. A benchmark on which one line of code already scores 69\,\%
leaves almost nothing to measure.

\section{The six sets of people I used}

\input{tables/tab-datasets}

Six sets for one problem may look like indiscipline. It is the direct
consequence of each new idea needing data the earlier files did not carry.
Adding the time of day required regenerating the sequences with their times
kept. Testing whether more people help required a day file with more people in
it. Testing whether \emph{many} more help required stacking eleven day files end
to end.\footnote{\textbf{Stacking} day files: putting them one after another into
a single training file, which adds \emph{people} rather than adding days to the
people already there.} Each regeneration produced a new test group, and
therefore a new value for the rule.

\section{What the data cannot answer}

\paragraph{The unit is one day.} The question posed at the start of this
internship is a within-day one: predict the rest of a person's day from its
beginning. The data is built for exactly that, and every experiment here answers
it. Predicting tomorrow from the days before is a different question, and the
natural continuation of this work.

\paragraph{The world is small.} 369 antennas over 113 masts, with a typical
person visiting on the order of twenty distinct ones. Already at the smallest
training size, 98.9\,\% of the antennas appearing in the test people's days had
also appeared in the training people's, and from 5{,}000 people onward it is
100\,\%. So discovering an unseen antenna cannot be a source of improvement
beyond that point, and any remaining gain has to come from better estimates of
the moves between antennas already known.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-data}

The decision that mattered most here was not a modelling decision. It was
accepting that the raw day file could not support the question being asked.
Moving to the selection of mobile, non-absent people was the first real lever of
the internship, and it is a data-quality result rather than a modelling one: it
cost the comfortable-looking accuracies of the early runs, and that is precisely
what made every later chapter measurable. What I would sequence differently is
the order: I spent the first iterations improving the training machinery on data
that could not answer the question, and only then changed the data.
