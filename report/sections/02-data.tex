\chapter{The data, and what it allows}
\label{ch:data}

\section{The question}

Before anything can be predicted, one has to know what the corpus actually
contains. The question that opens this report is therefore not ``how well can a
model predict the next cell?'' but the more modest one that has to be answered
first: \emph{what does a record in this dataset represent, and what kind of
claim can it support?} The answer shaped every experiment that follows, and two
of its consequences, that a record is not a movement, and that a
\cid{cell\_id} is not a place, are the reason several later results look
counter-intuitive at first sight.

\section{The corpus}

The material is a set of fifteen daily CSV exports of anonymised mobile-network
signalling records, covering 12 to 26 March 2014 in the Karlovy Vary study area
of north-west Bohemia, the area referred to as \cid{cd\_142} in the working
files, and the only one of the three available zones the team used. One row is
one user for one day, from \texttt{00:00:00} to \texttt{23:59:59}. The number
of rows varies from 80{,}217 to 99{,}504 depending on the day; the day used for
most of the work reported here, 12 March 2014, holds 85{,}946 users.

A row begins with eight fixed metadata columns, an anonymous user identifier,
age, gender, an unexplained five-digit number, a behaviour class, a ``home
cell'', an ``activity cell'' and the number of records that follow, and then
alternates \cid{cell\_id} and timestamp for as many records as that user
produced that day. The timestamp is expressed in seconds since local midnight
and always \emph{follows} the \cid{cell\_id} it belongs to, so that
$\lfloor \mathit{ts}/3600 \rfloor$ gives the hour of day of that event. Only
the user identifier, the record count and the (\cid{cell\_id}, timestamp) pairs
are used in this work; the remaining metadata carries known aberrations, ages
of 1078, negative values, and is not needed for a next-cell prediction.

\subsection*{A record is not a movement}

This is the single most important property of the corpus, and it is easy to
miss. A record is produced whenever a handset and a base station communicate.
That includes the case where nothing happens at all: a base station re-signals
to the handsets attached to it on a regular cycle, typically every four hours
on 2G and every two hours on 3G. A user who spends the whole day in one place
therefore still produces records, at a rate set by the network rather than by
the person. Two consequences follow immediately:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The number of records a user has is only partly a property of that
        person. It also depends on the radio technology their handset attaches
        to and on how much they actually used the phone.
  \item A long run of identical \cid{cell\_id} values in a trajectory is the
        normal case, not an anomaly. Any predictor that simply repeats the last
        value seen will be right very often, entirely for free.
\end{itemize}

That second point is what creates the yardstick this whole report is measured
against, and it is worth stating precisely before any model appears.

\section{The yardstick: the persistence baseline}

\emph{The persistence baseline} is the rule ``predict that the next
\cid{cell\_id} is the last one seen''. It contains no model, no training and no
parameters. It is scored on exactly the same positions as the model, in exactly
the same way, the measurement is set out in Chapter~\ref{ch:first}, so the
two are directly comparable.

Its value is not a constant, and this is the second thing that is easy to
miss. It is a property of the data. On a day-file taken as it comes, where a
large share of users barely move, ``repeat the last cell'' is right 69.2\,\% of
the time. Once the corpus is restricted to users who actually move, the same
rule falls to 45.5\,\%. Nothing about the rule changed; the population did. For
this reason every result in this report is stated as a \textbf{gap}, model
minus persistence baseline, both measured on the same users and the same scored
positions, and never as a bare accuracy. Whenever a chapter introduces a new
data regime, the value of its baseline is stated with it, and the reader should
resist comparing an accuracy in one chapter with an accuracy in another.

\section{A \cid{cell\_id} is not a place}

A \cid{cell\_id} such as \cid{BSOCHO1} looks like an opaque location token. It
is not. It decomposes into three parts, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:cellid-anatomy}: a letter giving the radio technology
(\cid{B} or \cid{D} for 2G, \cid{U} or \cid{V} for 3G), a \emph{site root}
identifying the physical antenna, and trailing digits identifying the
zone or sector of that antenna. The study area contains \textbf{369 distinct
cells over 113 site roots}, 112 distinct coordinate pairs, split into 224
2G cells and 145 3G cells. In other words, the same physical mast appears in the
data under three or four different names.

\input{figures/fig-cellid-anatomy}

The practical consequence is measurable. On the day used for the behavioural
analysis of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, 43.6\,\% of all consecutive-event pairs
repeat the same \cid{cell\_id} outright, 44.0\,\% are a genuine change of
physical site, and \textbf{12.4\,\%} fall in between: the handset re-attached
to a different cell of the \emph{same} antenna without the person going
anywhere (Figure~\ref{fig:transition-composition}). Counting that middle band as
mobility would inflate every movement statistic by roughly a quarter and, worse,
would scramble the behavioural profiles of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, whose whole
purpose is to separate users who move from users who do not.

\input{figures/fig-transition-composition}

Every mobility measurement in this report is therefore taken on the
\textbf{site root}, never on the raw \cid{cell\_id}. The prediction target, on
the other hand, remains the raw \cid{cell\_id}: that is the quantity the
original problem asks for, and coarsening it to the site root would be answering
an easier question than the one that was posed.

\section{From a raw export to a training sequence}

Turning a day-file into material a language model can be trained on takes four
conceptual stages, sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:data-flow}: keep the columns that
constitute a trajectory, convert each timestamp into an hour of day, emit one
ordered sequence of events per user, and split the users into a training and a
test group.

\input{figures/fig-data-flow}

Two choices inside that pipeline deserve to be stated, because they change how
later results should be read.

\paragraph{The split is by user, and it is stratified.} Train and test users are
disjoint: the model is graded only on people it has never seen. The assignment
is stratified on the number of records per user rather than taken at random, so
that the two groups have comparable sequence-length distributions, checked
with a Mann--Whitney test on the two length distributions. Without that
precaution, a train/test split can silently hand the easier, longer users to one
side.

\paragraph{Cleaning removes users, not events.} Three successive filtering
decisions were made over the internship, each in response to a result rather
than in advance:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nothing at first.} The earliest runs used the day-file as it
        came. This turned out to be the reason the baseline sat at 69.2\,\%: a
        large share of users produce a handful of records from a single cell, so
        the evaluation was dominated by positions where there was nothing to
        predict.
  \item \textbf{More than ten records, and no four-hour hole during the
        activity period.} The four-hour threshold is the corpus's own
        convention: because a base station re-signals at least every four hours,
        a gap longer than that means the user was outside the recording area
        rather than idle inside it. Filtering those out removes users whose
        trajectory has a hole in the middle of it.
  \item \textbf{Mobile, non-absent users only}, using the per-day selection
        prepared by a colleague. This is the corpus used for most of the
        modelling work, and it is where the baseline drops to the mid-forties.
\end{itemize}

Each of these makes the \emph{task} harder and the \emph{baseline} lower, which
is exactly the point: a benchmark on which a one-line heuristic already scores
69\,\% leaves almost nothing to measure.

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists every data regime used over the eleven

is the direct consequence of the point above, that each new approach needed data
the previous files did not carry. Adding the hour of day required regenerating
the sequences with their timestamps preserved. Testing whether more users help
required a day-file with more users. Testing whether \emph{many} more users help
required concatenating eleven day-files. And because the day-files share no
users at all, each regeneration produced a new set of test users, and
therefore a new baseline.

\section{What the data does not allow}

Three limits follow from the corpus itself, and none of them can be fixed by
better modelling.

\paragraph{One user is one day.} The user identifiers are per day-file and the
day-files share no users. There is no way to observe the same person on two
days, and therefore no way to learn a personal habit. Everything a model can
learn here is a regularity of the \emph{population}: what people who look like
this tend to do next. This single fact sets the ceiling that
Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} eventually runs into.

\paragraph{The world is small.} 369 cells over 113 sites, with a median user
visiting on the order of twenty distinct cells. Test-set vocabulary coverage is
already 98.9\,\% at the smallest training tier and reaches 100\,\% from 5{,}000
users onward, so ``discovering cells it had never seen'' cannot be a source of
improvement beyond that point. Any remaining gain has to come from better
estimates of the transitions between cells that are already known.

\paragraph{The end of a day is not the next day.} Every experiment in this
report predicts the end of a user's day from its beginning. That is a legitimate
task, but it is not the task an operator would care most about, predicting
tomorrow from the days before, and the data cannot express it.

\section{What I did next, and why}

At this point the object of study was pinned down: sequences of
(\cid{cell\_id}, hour) events, one day per user, with mobility measured on the
site root, a model-free yardstick to beat, and an explicit rule that only gaps
to that yardstick may be compared. Nothing had been trained yet. The next
question was purely instrumental, and it had to be settled before any
experiment could run at all: given a free cloud GPU and a few weeks, which
fine-tuning regime, which library stack and which base model are even
feasible? That is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}.

\section{Retrospective}
\label{sec:retro-data}

Looking back, the decision that mattered most in this chapter was not a
modelling decision at all, it was accepting that the raw day-file could not
support the question being asked. Taken as it came, a day puts the evaluation in
a regime where a large share of users produce a handful of records from a single
cell: there is no history to reason over, and a one-line heuristic is right
69.2\,\% of the time. Nothing that happens downstream of that can be measured
properly.

Moving to the mobile, non-absent selection Nino extracted for me was
therefore the first real lever of the internship, and I would describe it as a
data-quality result rather than a modelling one. It gave users with a decent
number of records, present in the recording area across the day, that is,
trajectories on which \emph{analysing the past in order to predict the rest} is
a meaningful exercise at all. It cost the comfortable-looking accuracies of the
early runs, since the baseline fell from 69.2\,\% to the mid-forties, and that
is precisely what made every chapter after it measurable.

The one thing I would sequence differently is the order in which I did those two
things. I spent the first iterations improving the training harness on a corpus
that could not answer the question, and only then changed the corpus. Settling
the data regime first, and tuning the training afterwards, would have reached
the same conclusions sooner.
