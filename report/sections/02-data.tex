\chapter{The data}
\label{ch:data}

\section{The goal}

Before anything can be predicted, one has to know what the data contains. Two
answers shaped every experiment that follows: \textbf{a record is not a movement},
and \textbf{the name of an antenna is not a place}. Both are the reason several
later results look surprising at first sight.

\section{One line of the data, taken apart}

The material is fifteen daily files covering 12 to 26 March 2014 in the Karlovy
Vary study area of north-west Bohemia. They are plain text
files\footnote{\textbf{CSV}, \emph{comma-separated values}: the simplest tabular
text format, one row per line with the fields separated by a delimiter. Here the
delimiter is a semicolon.} in which \textbf{one line is one person for one day},
from midnight to midnight. Depending on the day a file holds between 80{,}217 and
99{,}504 lines; the day used for most of the work here, 12 March 2014, holds
85{,}946 people.

A line starts with eight fixed columns, then repeats a pair of values for as many
records as that person produced. The eight are an anonymous number standing for the
person, their age, their gender, an unexplained five-digit number, a behaviour
class, a ``home antenna'', an ``activity antenna'' and the number of records that
follow. Then, over and over, \textbf{the name of an antenna followed by a time},
written as a number of seconds since midnight from 0 to 86{,}399. Only the
anonymous number, the record count and the antenna-and-time pairs are used here:
the rest carry known aberrations, including ages of 1078 and negative values.

A record is written whenever a phone and a mast exchange a message, and that
includes the case where nothing happens at all: a mast asks every phone attached
to it to announce itself on a fixed cycle, typically every four hours on 2G and
every two on 3G,\footnote{\textbf{Re-signalling}, or \emph{periodic location
update}: the network asking every attached phone to announce itself on a fixed
cycle, so that it knows where to route an incoming call.} so that it knows where
to route an incoming call. So \textbf{a record is not a movement}: somebody who
never leaves their flat still produces records all day long, and how many they
produce says as much about which generation of radio their phone attached to as
about what they did. A long run of one antenna name is also what makes ``repeat
the last name you saw'' such a hard competitor.

\subsection{The rule to beat, and the reason its value moves}

The \textbf{persistence baseline} is the rule ``the next antenna is the last one
seen''. No model, no training, no settings, and it is scored on exactly the same
moments as my model, in exactly the same way.

Its value is not a constant, and this is the part that is easy to miss. It is a
property of the people being scored. On a day file taken as it comes, where a
large share of people barely move, it is right 69.2\,\% of the time. Once the data
is restricted to people who actually move around, the very same rule falls to
45.5\,\%. Nothing about the rule changed; the population did.

\section{The name of an antenna is not a place}

An antenna name such as \cid{BKVCHE1} looks like an opaque location code. It is
not, and this is the point at which the technical name for it can safely be
introduced: in the working files this string is called the
\textbf{\cid{cell\_id}}, and it is used with that name from here on.

\input{figures/fig-base-station}

Figure~\ref{fig:base-station} draws one real mast with every name it answers to,
and the count underneath it is the whole point: eight names, one place. One mast
answers to several names at once, one per generation of radio and one per
direction its antennas point in, so there are three or four times as many names as
there are masts. A phone that never leaves the same spot can be passed from one of
those names to the next, and in the raw data that looks exactly like somebody going
somewhere.

\input{figures/fig-transition-composition}

Figure~\ref{fig:transition-composition} then counts how often the data plays that
trick: on this corpus 12.4 of every 100 back-to-back records change the name of
the antenna without the person having gone anywhere. So whenever this report asks whether somebody \emph{moved}, it looks at the mast
and not at the antenna name. What the model has to \emph{predict} stays the full
antenna name, because that is the question I was given; predicting only the mast
would be an easier one.

\section{From a raw file to training material}

\input{figures/fig-data-flow}

Figure~\ref{fig:data-flow} follows one day file through the four steps that turn
it into material a model can be trained on. Two choices inside that pipeline
change how later results should be read.

\paragraph{The split is by person, and it is balanced.} Training and test people
are different people: the model is graded only on strangers. The assignment is not
random but \emph{balanced} on the number of records per
person,\footnote{\textbf{Stratified split}: people are first sorted into bands by
how many records they have, and the split is made inside each band.} because
without that a split can silently hand all the longer histories to one side.

\paragraph{Cleaning removes people, not records.} Three filtering decisions were
made over the internship, each in response to a result rather than in advance. The
earliest runs used the day file exactly as it came, which is why the rule sat at
69.2\,\%: a large share of people produce a handful of records from a single
antenna, so the evaluation was dominated by moments where there was nothing to
predict. Then people with ten records or fewer, and people with a hole longer than
four hours, were removed; four hours is the corpus's own convention, since a mast
re-signals at least that often, so a longer gap means the person left the recording
area. Finally the data was restricted to people who move and stay in the area,
using the per-day selection prepared by a colleague. Each step makes the
\emph{task} harder and the \emph{rule} weaker, which is exactly the point: a
benchmark on which one line of code already scores 69\,\% leaves almost nothing to
measure.

\subsection{The six sets of people used in this report}

\input{tables/tab-datasets}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists all six, with the score of the one-line rule on
each in the last column. Six sets for one problem is the direct consequence of
each new idea needing data the earlier files did not carry. Adding the time of day required regenerating the
sequences with their times kept. Testing whether more people help required a day
file with more people in it. Testing whether \emph{many} more help required
stacking eleven day files end to end.\footnote{\textbf{Stacking} day files:
putting them one after another into a single training file, which adds
\emph{people} rather than adding days to the people already there.} Each
regeneration produced a new test group, and therefore a new value for the rule.

\section{The questions this data cannot answer}

\paragraph{The unit is one day.} The question posed at the start of this internship
is a within-day one: predict the rest of a person's day from its beginning. Every
experiment here answers it. Predicting tomorrow from the days before is a different
question, and the natural continuation of this work.

\paragraph{The world is small.} 369 antennas over 113 masts, with a typical person
visiting on the order of twenty distinct ones. Already at the smallest training
size, 98.9\,\% of the antennas appearing in the test people's days had also
appeared in the training people's, and from 5{,}000 people onward it is 100\,\%. So
discovering an unseen antenna cannot be a source of improvement beyond that point:
any remaining gain has to come from better estimates of the moves between antennas
already known.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:retro-data}

Two properties of this material constrain every result that follows. A record is
not a movement: the network writes a line whenever it asks a phone to announce
itself, so a day of records is largely somebody sitting still being counted over
and over, and that is exactly what makes ``repeat the last antenna'' so hard to
beat. And the name of an antenna is not a place: one mast answers to three or four
names at once, so 12.4 of every 100 changes of name are changes of radio rather
than changes of location. From here on, whenever this report says that somebody
\emph{moved}, it has looked at the mast; what the model is asked to produce stays
the full antenna name, because that is the question that was put to me.

The decision that mattered most in this chapter was not a modelling one. It was
accepting that the raw day file could not support the question being asked: moving
to the selection of people who genuinely move around the area cost the
comfortable-looking accuracies of the early runs, and that is precisely what made
every later chapter measurable. With hindsight the order was wrong. I spent the
first iterations improving the training machinery on a file where one line of code
already scored 69.2\,\%, and only then looked at what was in it; a day spent
reading the data first would have shown that there was nothing to win there. The
price of changing it late is the six sets of people of Table~\ref{tab:datasets}:
the yardstick moves from one chapter to the next, so no absolute score in this
report may be compared with an absolute score in another. Only the distance to the
rule travels.
