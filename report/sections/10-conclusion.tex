\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

The question given to me at the start of this internship was whether a language
model, retrained on anonymous network records, can predict which antenna a
person's phone will be attached to next. Nine weeks and eleven iterations later
the answer is yes, by a modest and measurable margin, and the more interesting
part of the answer is the conditions under which that is true.

\section{What the work settles}

\input{figures/fig-results-card}

\paragraph{The model does beat the one-line rule, and it is clear where the gain
comes from.} A fine-tuned Qwen2.5-0.5B answers correctly \textbf{4.3 more times
out of a hundred} than ``repeat the last antenna''. That number is not large, and
it should not be: the competitor is one line of code that is right roughly half
the time on people who genuinely move, and for five of the eleven iterations
nothing I trained beat it at all. What made the difference was neither capacity
nor volume but \emph{conditioning on who the person is}, sorting people by the
hours at which they actually change location and sending each kind of user to its
own set of trained numbers. That single change took the model from 1.8 behind the
rule to 3.7 ahead of it, and the gain fell in the exact inverse order of how well
repetition already worked for each of the four kinds.

\paragraph{The amount of data needed is small, and the report says where it stops
paying.} Population size is a real lever from 400 to 1{,}900 training people, where
the gain grows from 0.6 to 4.2, and is worth nothing above it. One explanation fits
the timing: the least populated of the four kinds of user crosses roughly a hundred
members exactly between the two sizes where the score moves.

\paragraph{Difficulty lives in the person, not in the dataset.} This is the finding
I would keep if I had to keep one. The score of an individual person ranges from
zero to one hundred per cent, and what predicts it is how repetitive that person's
movement already is. An average score on this problem is a weighted mixture of
segments of wildly different difficulty, and quoting it alone hides the only part
of the result that is about the model.

\section{What I did not have time to do}

The first four items are unfinished work rather than future research. Each could be
done in under a day, and I would do them before anything else.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Re-score the earlier model on the later test people.} One grading
        pass, no training. Because the set of test people changed four times, the
        3.7 of iteration 8 and the 4.2 of iteration 9 are two separate measurements
        of the same effect rather than a progression. Re-scoring puts every row of
        the project on one axis.
  \item \textbf{Re-measure the switch-rate correlation on the final model.} Also
        one grading pass. The $r = -0.971$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:user} was measured on
        a model that was imitating the trivial rule, and a model that genuinely
        helps the restless minority has to break the lockstep between how often
        somebody moves and how well they are predicted. It is the cheapest
        independent test of this report's main claim.
  \item \textbf{Train the same configuration twice from different random starting
        points.} About half an hour. The resampling test of Chapter~\ref{ch:scale}
        covers the luck of which test people are drawn, but not the fact that
        training itself is not perfectly repeatable, so differences of a few tenths
        between sizes sit on a floor nobody measured.
  \item \textbf{Run the capacity control for the routing.} Four sets of trained
        numbers are four times the trainable capacity of one, so part of the
        project's main gain may be capacity rather than the sorting itself. A
        single set carrying as many trainable numbers as the four together, on the
        same data, separates the two.
\end{enumerate}

Two experiments were prepared and did not complete. A seven-day comparison, one
independent training per weekday, was launched and the session crashed after five
hours with none of the seven finished, so this report claims no effect of the day
of the week. And the scaling curve was measured at constant computing time, which
means that at 21{,}000 people only 36\,\% of the available training material was
ever read; giving the largest dataset the training time its size deserves is the
one experiment that would separate ``1{,}900 people are enough at this budget''
from ``more people never help''.

\section{What I would do next}

\paragraph{Make the conditioning work on a short history.} This is the limitation I
would put first if someone asked what the work is good for. The model's edge is
largest on people about whom little is known, 4.6 with only 30\,\% of a day in hand
against 4.3 with 80\,\%, and that cold start is exactly the case an operator cares
about. But the kind of user is identified correctly for 92 out of 100 people at the
reference protocol and only 61 and 44 out of 100 when half a day or a third of it
is available. So the very conditioning that produces the edge decays as the history
shortens. Building an identifier that works on a short prefix is a smaller and
better-posed problem than the one I spent the internship on, and it is where I
would start again.

\paragraph{Widen the question from one day to several.} The question given to me
was a within-day one, and that is the question this report answers. The obvious
continuation is to predict day $N+1$ from days $1$ to $N$, on data whose
identifiers are stable from one day to the next: observing the same individual's
home-to-activity move several times, and anything that could be called
personalisation. That is the only item on this list that changes what the problem
\emph{is} rather than how well it is measured.

\paragraph{And check whether any of this survives a different city.} Every result
here comes from a single Czech region over fifteen days in March 2014, with 369
antennas over 113 masts. A denser urban area with thousands of antennas could
behave differently, and the saturation result in particular partly reflects how
small this world is.

\section{In one paragraph}

On single-day cellular records, what a population has in common is cheap to learn,
it is measurable, it is worth about four correct answers per hundred over a trivial
rule, and it is exhausted at around two thousand people. What it is not yet is
usable on somebody who has just appeared, since the conditioning it rests on has to
be read from most of a day of history. Closing that gap, rather than training a
larger model on a larger corpus, is where I would start again.
