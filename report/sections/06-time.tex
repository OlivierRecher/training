\chapter{Adding the time of day}
\label{ch:time}

\section{The goal}

Up to this point the model was given a bare list of antennas. Nothing in its
input said whether an event happened at four in the morning or at seven in the
evening.

That is a strange omission for a mobility problem. The reason a person changes
antenna at all is usually that they are going somewhere, and going somewhere
happens at particular times of day. So the question of this chapter is whether
\emph{time of day} is the missing signal, and more precisely whether a model can
be pushed to expect a change of antenna at the hours where a change is likely,
instead of falling back on the one strategy that is always available to it,
namely repeating the last antenna it saw.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} All of them come from
\Dmob{} (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): 400 people used for training and \textbf{100
different people}, never seen during training, used for grading. The one-line
rule scores 45.5\,\% on the last fifth of their day and 43.1\,\% when every
position of the day is graded, and the second half of this chapter switches to
the second of those two protocols.

\section{The clue: some people collapse at the end of the day}

The pointer came from looking at people one at a time.

Scoring the same trained model with progressively more history, most people
behave exactly as Chapter~\ref{ch:user} predicts: their score is a trait, roughly
constant whatever they are given. But one group does something else entirely.
Given \emph{more} history, their score falls off a cliff, from thirty or forty
per hundred down to zero. Table~\ref{tab:context-collapse} lists eight people,
and the bottom four are that group.

\input{tables/tab-context-collapse}

That is a diagnostic rather than a curiosity, and the reason is which moments are
being graded. When 80 to 90\,\% of the day is handed over as history, the moments
left to answer are the ones at the \emph{end} of that person's day. A model that
follows somebody correctly through the morning and then scores zero on their
evening is not short of capacity. It is confidently repeating the last antenna it
saw, in several of these cases with up to 78\,\% confidence, at exactly the
moment when that person stops staying put.

So the missing ingredient is not more history. It is knowing that the end of the
day is not the middle of it.

\section{How the time of day enters, and the two bets it carries}

Time enters in the form fixed in Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}: one symbol for the hour
placed before each event, costing one symbol per event, always given to the model
and never predicted by it. Panel (a) of Figure~\ref{fig:hour-ablation} shows the
resulting map of the day.

Two distinct bets were made at once, and it is worth separating them because only
one of the two survived the chapter.

\begin{description}
  \item[Bet 1: the model can use the hour.] Given the hour of every past event
        and of the event to be named, the model can learn that some antennas are
        morning antennas and others evening ones.
  \item[Bet 2: some hours deserve more weight.] There are \emph{transition
        windows},\footnote{\textbf{Transition window}: a stretch of hours in which
        a mistake is made to count more, so that a wrong answer at 19h under a
        multiplier of four costs four times a wrong answer at 14h. No data is
        duplicated.} stretches of the day in which a person is more likely to
        move between the area they live in and the area they spend the day in. If
        mistakes there are made to count more, the training is forced to get them
        right rather than accepting them as the price of a good average.
\end{description}

Bet 2 requires knowing which hours those are, and at this stage I did not measure
them. I \emph{guessed} them, from the time boundaries the team's home-and-activity
classification already used: \textbf{04--06h} and \textbf{18--20h}. That guess is
the subject of the end of this chapter.

\section{Bet 1 holds: the model does read the hour}

Whether a model uses an input is not something to assume. It is something to test
by breaking the input and seeing whether the score notices.

Three corruptions were applied to the same trained copy of the model and the same
test people, changing \emph{only} the hour symbols and leaving the list of
antennas untouched: shuffling the hours, replacing them all by the same value,
and moving every one of them forward by twelve. Figure~\ref{fig:hour-ablation}
draws what each corruption does and what it costs.

\input{figures/fig-hour-ablation}

The argument is the difference between two of those numbers, and only that.
Replacing every hour by a constant, which removes the information completely,
costs 0.7 correct answers per hundred. Merely shuffling the hours, so that the
values are still real hours but attached to the wrong events, costs 3.0, four
times as much. If the hour symbol were simply occupying a slot in the sequence,
destroying its \emph{ordering} could not possibly cost more than deleting it
altogether. It costs four times more, so the model has learned something real
about which hour goes with which antenna.

The third corruption is the informative one. Moving every hour forward by twelve
changes the overall score by only $+0.5$, and that near-zero hides a swap.
Inside the guessed windows the score falls from 42.7 to 39.7, while outside them
it rises from 45.3 to 47.1. The two halves of the day exchanged their difficulty
along with their labels, which is only possible if the model had attached
specific behaviour to specific hours. Bet 1 is established.

\section{Bet 2 half-holds: the hours can be won, at a price}

If the mechanism works, the obvious move is to push harder on it. Three changes
were made together.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An uneven weight.} The two windows are not comparable in size:
        18--20h carries four times as many records as 04--06h and touches 58\,\%
        of people against 39\,\%. Weighting them equally asks the training to
        work as hard on the sparse, noisy window as on the well-populated one, so
        18--20h was raised to a multiplier of four while 04--06h stayed at two.
  \item \textbf{Ask the question more often.}\footnote{\textbf{Sampler}: the
        component that chooses which slice of training data to show next. Making
        it \emph{weighted} means some slices are drawn more often. Distinct from
        a weighted mistake, which changes how much a wrong answer costs rather
        than how often the question is asked.} Training slices containing at
        least one in-window move were drawn three times more often than the rest,
        which raised the share of training batches containing such a move from
        45.1\,\% to 71.2\,\%, without duplicating a single record.
  \item \textbf{Grade the whole day.} Until then only the last fifth of each test
        person's day was graded. From this point every position is graded, one at
        a time. This is a strictly harder measurement, because early positions
        have almost no history behind them, and it is why the one-line rule on
        these same people is quoted as 43.1\,\% on the full day against 45.5\,\%
        on the last fifth. Both numbers describe the same people; they answer
        different questions, and from here on the full-day figure is the one
        used.
\end{itemize}

The result, in Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling}, is the first positive result of
the project that is not an artefact of a single cut.

\input{figures/fig-targeted-sampling}

Inside 18--20h, over 604 graded moments, the model reaches 40.6\,\% against a
rule at 37.9\,\%: \textbf{2.6 more correct answers per hundred}. Over both
windows combined, 732 moments, 2.3 more. For the first time, a trained model beat
``repeat the last antenna'' on a segment defined in advance rather than
discovered afterwards. The 04--06h figure rests on only 128 moments and should be
read as ``not worse'' rather than as a win.

And then the price, in Table~\ref{tab:after-sampling}. Outside the windows, over
7{,}893 moments, more than ten times the in-window volume, the model lost 2.6.
The distance to the rule got worse at \emph{every one} of the seven cuts, and
top-3 and top-5 fell everywhere by between 2.6 and 4.8. One degraded row would be
noise; seven consistent rows are a model that has been narrowed.

\section{What the table above says: right mechanism, wrong hours}

Table~\ref{tab:after-sampling}, just above, is the cost, and
Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling} before it is the gain. The two results are not
contradictory. They are the same phenomenon seen from opposite sides, and read
together they are quite precise.

Weighting a chosen set of moments does exactly what it is designed to do: it buys
correct answers there and pays for them elsewhere. The mechanism was never in
question after the ablation. What was in question, and what I had never checked,
was whether the moments I had chosen were the hard ones.

They were not, and the measurement is unambiguous. On the full test day, the
one-line rule scores 43.6\,\% \emph{inside} the guessed windows and 43.4\,\%
\emph{outside} them. A difference of two tenths. In other words the global window
04--06h / 18--20h does not separate easy moments from hard moments at all, so
every extra unit of effort spent there was spent on moments that were no harder
than average.

Worse, broken down by kind of user, 04--06h turns out to be \emph{easier} than
average for every single kind: the rule scores between 49 and 64\,\% inside it,
against 20 to 57\,\% on average. Song et al.\ had published exactly that in 2010.
Their curve of hourly regularity is at its highest during the night and dips
around midday and in the early evening \cite{song2010limits}, so the window I
guessed is the one stretch of the day the literature already marked as easy.
Doubling the cost of a mistake there spent the training effort on moments the
rule already gets right, which is the most expensive thing one can do on a task
like this.

The honest summary of the iteration is therefore this. \textbf{The machinery
worked, and I pointed it at the wrong hours.} The 2.6 won at 18--20h was real,
because 18--20h happens to be genuinely difficult for one of the four kinds of
user; the loss on everything else was the cost of upweighting hours that were
difficult for nobody.

That conclusion has an obvious consequence, and it is what
Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} does. If the right hours differ from one person to the
next, then the remedy is not to pick better hours for everybody. It is to stop
assuming everybody shares the same hours.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-time}

The change I would flag from this iteration is the one that looks purely
technical.

Moving from grading only the last fifth of each test day to grading every
position was the right methodological choice: the early positions have almost no
history behind them, and leaving them out was quietly making the task easier than
the question deserved. But it also moved the one-line rule, on the very same
people, from 45.5\,\% to 43.1\,\%, and that has a consequence I did not state
plainly at the time. The runs before the change and the runs after it cannot be
compared on absolute score at all, only on their respective distances to the
rule.

It is stated plainly here instead, and it is why every figure and table in this
report carries the protocol it was measured under. A change of measurement is a
change of ruler, and a change of ruler invalidates every absolute comparison made
across it. If I were running the project again I would freeze the grading
protocol before the first training and treat any later change to it as opening a
new series of results rather than continuing the old one.
