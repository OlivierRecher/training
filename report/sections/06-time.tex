\chapter{Adding the time of day}
\label{ch:time}

\section{The goal}

Up to this point the model was given a bare list of antennas. Nothing in its input
said whether an event happened at four in the morning or at seven in the evening,
which is a strange omission for a mobility problem: the reason a person changes
antenna at all is usually that they are going somewhere, and going somewhere
happens at particular times of day. So the question of this chapter is whether
\emph{time of day} is the missing signal, and whether a model can be pushed to
expect a change of antenna at the hours where a change is likely, instead of
falling back on the one strategy always available to it.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} All of them from \Dmob{}
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): 400 people used for training and \textbf{100 different
people}, never seen during training, used for grading. The one-line rule scores
45.5\,\% on the last fifth of their day and 43.1\,\% when every position of the
day is graded, and the second half of this chapter switches to the second of those
two protocols.

\subsection{The clue: some people collapse at the end of the day}

The pointer came from looking at people one at a time. Scoring the same trained
model with progressively more history, most behave exactly as
Chapter~\ref{ch:user} predicts: their score is a trait, roughly constant whatever
they are given. But one group does something else entirely. Given \emph{more}
history, their score falls off a cliff, from thirty or forty per hundred down to
zero.

\section{The form the time of day takes, and the two bets it carries}

Time enters in the form fixed in Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}: one symbol for the hour
placed before each event, always given to the model and never predicted by it. Two
distinct bets were made at once, and only one of the two survived the chapter.

\begin{description}
  \item[Bet 1: the model can use the hour.] Given the hour of every past event and
        of the event to be named, the model can learn that some antennas are
        morning antennas and others evening ones.
  \item[Bet 2: some hours deserve more weight.] There are \emph{transition
        windows},\footnote{\textbf{Transition window}: a stretch of hours in which
        a mistake is made to count more, so that a wrong answer at 19h under a
        multiplier of four costs four times a wrong answer at 14h.} stretches of
        the day in which a person is more likely to move between the area they
        live in and the area they spend the day in. If mistakes there are made to
        count more, the training is forced to get them right rather than accepting
        them as the price of a good average.
\end{description}

Bet 2 requires knowing which hours those are, and at this stage I did not measure
them. I \emph{guessed} them, from the time boundaries the team's home-and-activity
classification already used: \textbf{04--06h} and \textbf{18--20h}. That guess is
the subject of the end of this chapter.

\subsection{Bet 1 holds: the model does read the hour}

Whether a model uses an input is not something to assume. It is something to test
by breaking the input and seeing whether the score notices.

\input{figures/fig-hour-ablation}

The test is the one drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:hour-ablation}: the hour symbols are
deliberately corrupted in three different ways, and the score is re-measured each
time. The argument is the difference between two of those numbers, and only
that.
Replacing every hour by a constant, which removes the information completely,
costs 0.7 correct answers per hundred. Merely shuffling the hours, so that the
values are still real hours but attached to the wrong events, costs 3.0, four
times as much. If the hour symbol were simply occupying a slot in the sequence,
destroying its \emph{ordering} could not possibly cost more than deleting it
altogether. Bet 1 is established.

\subsection{Bet 2 half-holds: the hours can be won, at a price}

Three changes were made together. \textbf{An uneven weight}: the two windows are
not comparable in size, since 18--20h carries four times as many records as
04--06h and touches 58\,\% of people against 39\,\%, so 18--20h was raised to a
multiplier of four while 04--06h stayed at two. \textbf{Ask the question more
often}: training slices containing at least one in-window move were drawn three
times more often than the rest,\footnote{\textbf{Sampler}: the component that
chooses which slice of training data to show next. Making it \emph{weighted} means
some slices are drawn more often, which changes how often a question is asked
rather than how much a wrong answer costs.} which raised the share of training
batches containing such a move from 45.1\,\% to 71.2\,\% without duplicating a
single record. And \textbf{grade the whole day}: until then only the last fifth of
each test person's day was graded, and from this point every position is, one at a
time. That is strictly harder, because early positions have almost no history
behind them, and it is why the one-line rule on these same people is quoted as
43.1\,\% on the full day against 45.5\,\% on the last fifth.

Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling} and Table~\ref{tab:after-sampling} measure what
those three changes were worth: the first group of moments by group of moments,
the second at seven different cuts of the day.

\subsection{Right mechanism, wrong hours}

As Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling} shows, the model gains 2.6 correct answers
per hundred inside the 18--20h stretch and loses ground almost everywhere else,
and the seven rows of Table~\ref{tab:after-sampling} all move the same way. The
gain and the cost are not contradictory. Weighting a chosen set of moments
does exactly what it is designed to do: it buys correct answers there and pays for
them elsewhere. The mechanism was never in question after the ablation. What was
in question, and what I had never checked, was whether the moments I had chosen
were the hard ones.

They were not, and the measurement is unambiguous. On the full test day the
one-line rule scores 43.6\,\% \emph{inside} the guessed windows and 43.4\,\%
\emph{outside} them, a difference of two tenths. The global window 04--06h /
18--20h does not separate easy moments from hard moments at all, so every extra
unit of effort spent there was spent on moments no harder than average. Worse,
broken down by kind of user, 04--06h turns out to be \emph{easier} than average
for every single kind: the rule scores between 49 and 64\,\% inside it, against 20
to 57\,\% on average. Song et al.\ had published exactly that in 2010, their curve
of hourly regularity being at its highest during the night and dipping around
midday and in the early evening \cite{song2010limits}. The window I guessed is the
one stretch of the day the literature already marked as easy.

\textbf{The machinery worked, and I pointed it at the wrong hours.} The 2.6 won at
18--20h was real, because 18--20h happens to be genuinely difficult for one of the
four kinds of user; the loss on everything else was the cost of upweighting hours
that were difficult for nobody. That has an obvious consequence, and it is what
Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} does. If the right hours differ from one person to the
next, the remedy is not to pick better hours for everybody. It is to stop assuming
everybody shares the same hours.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:retro-time}

The chapter carried two bets and settled both. The first holds: the model does
read the hour, and the proof is that scrambling the hours costs four times as much
as deleting them, which could not happen if the symbol were merely occupying a
slot. That result is kept, and every run from this point on is given the hour of
day. The second bet half-holds. Making mistakes count more inside a chosen stretch
of the day does exactly what it is designed to do, buying 2.6 correct answers per
hundred at 18--20h, but it pays for them everywhere else, at all seven cuts of the
day tested. The machinery was right and I pointed it at the wrong hours: on these
people the one-line rule scores 43.6\,\% inside the guessed windows against
43.4\,\% outside them, so the moments I paid to emphasise were no harder than
average. Song et al.\ had published the hourly curve that says so in 2010, and the
stretch I guessed is the one the literature already marks as easy.

What I take from that is a habit rather than a result. The hours were the only
component of the design I never measured before relying on it, and they are the
only one that failed; measuring them would have cost an afternoon against the
iteration it cost instead. There is also a methodological cost I would avoid a
second time. Grading every position instead of only the last fifth was the right
choice, since leaving the early positions out was quietly making the task easier
than the question deserved, but it moved the one-line rule on the very same people
from 45.5\,\% to 43.1\,\%, so runs before and after cannot be compared on absolute
score at all. A change of measurement is a change of ruler: I would freeze the
grading protocol before the first training and treat any later change as opening a
new series of results.

The failure is also what produced the rest of the report. If one pair of hours
applied to everybody marks nothing, the remedy is not a better pair of hours. It is
to stop assuming that everybody shares the same ones.
