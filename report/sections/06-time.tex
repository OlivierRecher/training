\chapter{Adding the clock}
\label{ch:time}

\section{The question}

Up to this point the model was given a bare list of cells. Nothing in its input
said whether an event happened at four in the morning or at seven in the
evening. That is a strange omission for a mobility problem: the reason a person
changes cell at all is usually that they are going somewhere, and going
somewhere happens at particular times of day. The question of this chapter is
therefore whether \emph{time of day} is the missing signal, and, more
precisely, whether a model can be pushed to \emph{reason} about a change of cell
at the hours where a change is likely, instead of defaulting to the one strategy
that is otherwise always available to it: repeat the last cell.

\section{The evidence that a temporal signal was missing}

The pointer came from the per-user diagnosis. Scoring the same trained model
with progressively more context, most users behave as Chapter~\ref{ch:user}
predicts: their accuracy is a trait, roughly constant whatever the context, and
Table~\ref{tab:context-collapse} is the pass on which the $r = 0.77$ quoted
there is measured. But one group does something else entirely, shown in the
lower rows of that table: given \emph{more} history, their accuracy falls off a
cliff, from thirty or forty per cent down to zero.

\input{tables/tab-context-collapse}

That is a diagnostic, not a curiosity. Accuracy at 80--90\,\% context is measured
on the positions at the \emph{end} of the user's day. A model that predicts
someone correctly through the morning and then scores zero on their evening is
not short of capacity, it is confidently repeating the last cell it saw at
exactly the moment when that user stops staying put. In several of those cases
the model assigned up to 78\,\% probability to the cell it had just seen. The
missing ingredient is not more history; it is knowing that the end of the day is
not the middle of it.

\section{How the clock enters, and the two hypotheses it carries}

Time enters in the form fixed in Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}: one dedicated hour
token before each event, costing one token per event, always given and never
predicted. Panel~(a) of Figure~\ref{fig:hour-ablation} shows the resulting
weight map.

Two distinct hypotheses were encoded at once, and it is worth separating them
because only one of them survived the chapter.

\begin{description}
  \item[H1, the model can use the hour.] Given the hour of each past event
        and of the event to predict, the model can learn that some cells are
        morning cells and others evening cells.
  \item[H2, some hours deserve more weight.] There are
        \emph{transition windows}:\footnote{\textbf{Transition window}: a range of
hours where the training loss is multiplied, so that a mistake at 19h under a
$\times4$ weight counts four times as much as the same mistake at 14h. No data
is duplicated.} hours at which a user has a higher chance of
        moving between their home area and their activity area. If the loss at
        those positions is multiplied, the optimiser is forced to get them right
        rather than to accept them as the price of a good average.
\end{description}

H2 requires knowing which hours those are, and at this stage I did not measure
them, I guessed them, from the time boundaries the team's home-cell and
activity-cell classification already used: \textbf{04--06h} and
\textbf{18--20h}. That guess is the subject of the end of this chapter.

\section{H1 holds: the hour is genuinely read}

Whether a model uses an input is not something to assume; it is something to
test by breaking the input. Three corruptions, an
\emph{ablation},\footnote{\textbf{Ablation}: an experiment that removes or corrupts one component and
measures what the score loses, which is how a useful input is told from a
decorative one. Used three times here: on the hour tokens, and twice on the
group token in Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}.} were applied to the same trained
checkpoint and the same test users, changing \emph{only} the hour tokens and
leaving the cell sequence untouched. Panel~(b) of
Figure~\ref{fig:hour-ablation} gives the result.

\input{figures/fig-hour-ablation}

Replacing every hour by the same constant costs $0.7\pt$. Shuffling the hours,
so that the values are still real hours but in the wrong order, costs
$3.0\pt$, four times as much. The difference between those two numbers is the
whole argument: if the token were merely occupying a slot, destroying its
\emph{ordering} would cost no more than removing it. It costs four times more,
so the model has learned something about which hour goes with which cell.

The third corruption is the informative one. Shifting every hour by twelve moves
the aggregate by only $+0.5\pt$, and that near-zero hides a swap. Accuracy
\emph{inside} the transition windows falls from 42.7\,\% to 39.7\,\%, while
accuracy \emph{outside} them rises from 45.3\,\% to 47.1\,\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:hour-ablation}(c)). The two halves of the day exchanged their
difficulty along with their labels, which means the model was not merely reading
the hour: it had learned behaviour attached to the windows specifically. H1 is
established.

\section{H2 half-holds: the windows can be won, at a price}

If the mechanism works, the obvious move is to push harder on it. Three changes
were made together:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An asymmetric weight.} The two windows are not comparable in
        size: $18$--$20$h carries four times as many records as $04$--$06$h and
        touches 58\,\% of users against 39\,\%. Weighting them equally asks the
        optimiser to work as hard on the sparse, noisy window as on the
        well-populated one, so
        $18$--$20$h was raised to $\times4$ while $04$--$06$h stayed at
        $\times2$.
  \item \textbf{A weighted sampler.}\footnote{\textbf{Sampler}: the component that chooses which slice of training data
to show next; making it \emph{weighted} means some slices are drawn more
often. Distinct from a weighted loss, which changes how much a mistake counts
rather than how often the question is asked.} Training windows containing at least
        one in-window transition were drawn three times more often than the
        rest,
        which raised the share of training batches containing such a transition
        from 45.1\,\% to 71.2\,\%, without duplicating a single record.
  \item \textbf{Full-sequence evaluation.} Until then only the last 20\,\% of
        each test sequence was scored. From this point every position is scored,
        one at a time. This is a strictly harder measurement, early positions
        have almost no history behind them, and it is why the persistence
        baseline on this corpus is quoted as 43.1\,\% on the full sequence
        against 45.5\,\% on the last fifth. Both numbers describe the same users;
        they answer different questions, and from here on the full-sequence
        figure is the one used.
\end{itemize}

The result, in Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling}, is the first positive result
of the project that is not an artefact of a single context split.

\input{figures/fig-targeted-sampling}

Inside $18$--$20$h, 604 predictions, the model reaches 40.6\,\% against a
baseline of 37.9\,\%: $+2.6\pt$. Over both windows combined, 732 predictions,
$+2.3\pt$. For the first time, a trained model beat ``repeat the last cell'' on
a segment defined in advance rather than discovered afterwards. The $04$--$06$h
figure rests on 128 predictions and should be read as ``not worse'' rather than
as a win.

And then the price, in Table~\ref{tab:after-sampling}. Outside the windows,
7{,}893 predictions, more than ten times the in-window volume, the model lost
$2.6\pt$. The gap to the baseline widened at \emph{every one} of the seven
context fractions, and top-3 and top-5 fell by between $2.6$ and $4.8\pt$
everywhere. One degraded row would be noise; seven consistent rows are a model
that has been narrowed.

\section{What the numbers said: the mechanism was right, the target was wrong}

The two results above are not contradictory, and read together they are quite
precise. Weighting a chosen set of positions does exactly what it is supposed to
do: it buys accuracy there and spends it elsewhere. The mechanism was never in
question after the ablation. What was in question, and what I had never
checked, was whether the positions I had chosen were the hard ones.

They were not. Measured on the full test sequences, the persistence baseline
scores 43.6\,\% \emph{inside} the guessed windows and 43.4\,\% \emph{outside}
them: a difference of $0.2$ percentage points. In other words the global window
$04$--$06$h / $18$--$20$h does not separate easy positions from hard positions at
all. Worse, broken down by behavioural profile, $04$--$06$h turns out to be
\emph{easier} than average for every single profile, persistence between 49
and 64\,\% inside it, against 20 to 57\,\% on average. Song et al.\ had
published that much in 2010: their hourly regularity curve is at its highest
during the night and dips around midday and in the early evening
\cite{song2010limits}, so the window I guessed was the one hour band the
literature already marked as easy. Doubling the loss there
spent the training effort on positions the baseline already gets right, which
is the most expensive thing one can do on a task like this.

The honest summary of the iteration is therefore: the machinery worked, and I
pointed it at the wrong hours. The $+2.6\pt$ at $18$--$20$h was real because
$18$--$20$h happens to be genuinely difficult for one of the four profiles; the
loss on everything else was the cost of upweighting hours that were difficult
for nobody.

\section{Retrospective}
\label{sec:retro-time}

The change I would flag from this iteration is the one that looks purely
technical. Moving from scoring only the last fifth of each test sequence to
scoring every position was the right methodological choice: the early positions
have almost no history behind them, and leaving them out was quietly making the
task easier than the question deserved. But it also moved the persistence
baseline on the very same users from 45.5\,\% to 43.1\,\%, and that has a
consequence I did not state plainly at the time, the runs before the change
and the runs after it cannot be compared on absolute accuracy at all, only on
their respective gaps.

It is stated plainly here instead, and it is why every figure and table in this
report carries the protocol it was measured under. A change of measurement is a
change of baseline, and a change of baseline invalidates every absolute
comparison made across it. If I were running the project again I would freeze
the evaluation protocol before the first training and treat any later change to
it as opening a new series of results rather than continuing the old one.
