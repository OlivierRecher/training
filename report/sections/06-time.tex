\chapter{Temporal Feature Integration and Hourly Loss Weighting}
\label{ch:time}

\section{Hypothesis Formulation and Temporal Encoding Objectives}

Prior experimental configurations provided models strictly with ordered cell identifier sequences, excluding explicit temporal timestamps. Because human mobility exhibits strong temporal periodicity (e.g., morning and evening commutes), excluding temporal context omits a key predictive feature.

The central research objective evaluated in this chapter is to investigate whether \emph{explicit temporal encoding (hour of day)} improves transition prediction accuracy, and whether loss weighting across specific transition hours forces the model to anticipate non-stationary mobility events.

\paragraph{Data Provenance.} Experiments evaluate active mobility dataset \Dmob{} (\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:datasets}}), comprising 400 training subscribers and \textbf{100 held-out evaluation subscribers}. Corresponding persistence baseline accuracy is 45.5\% on 80/20 evaluation splits and 43.1\% across whole-trajectory evaluation.

\subsection{Identification of Temporal Performance Degradation Patterns}

The starting point of this chapter is not a new experiment but a closer reading of the previous training. The model of Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}, trained on \Dmob{} without any hour token and read at seven context fractions in \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:context-sweep}}, was scored again subscriber by subscriber instead of on the whole population. This individual error analysis revealed specific trajectories where model accuracy degraded sharply as context length increased, and it is this observation that leads to the finding below.

\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:context-collapse}} scores eight representative subscribers twice with the same trained model, once under a short history prefix (30\%--60\% of the day) and once under a long one (80\%--90\%); the \textbf{Change} column is the second score minus the first. More history should help or at worst do nothing, which is what rows 1--3 show, and across the whole population the two scores correlate strongly ($r = 0.77$), so predictability is an intrinsic subscriber trait rather than an artifact of the context split. The bottom four entries (rows 5--8), however, collapse to near 0\%. With an 80\%--90\% prefix, the moments left to predict fall in the late afternoon and evening; without explicit temporal tokens, the model keeps repeating the morning cell, with up to 78\% output probability, at the exact time these subscribers begin their evening commute. Sequence context alone, without temporal encoding, fails to distinguish late-day transition probability shifts from mid-day stationary patterns.

\section{Temporal Feature Representation and Experimental Hypotheses}

Temporal feature integration prepends dedicated hourly tokens (\cid{<H00>} through \cid{<H23>}) to cell event tokens within the sequence. Two distinct hypotheses were evaluated:

\begin{description}
  \item[Hypothesis 1 (Temporal Feature Sensitivity).] Providing explicit hour tokens enables the model to condition transition probabilities on diurnal temporal states (distinguishing morning vs. evening transition likelihoods).
  \item[Hypothesis 2 (Targeted Loss Weighting).] Assigning higher cross-entropy loss weights to specific \emph{transition windows}\footnote{\textbf{Transition window}: predefined temporal intervals where loss values are multiplied (e.g., $4\times$ multiplier for 18h--20h) to penalize transition prediction errors.} focuses gradient updates on high-mobility periods.
\end{description}

Initial loss weighting experiments assigned heuristic transition windows based on preliminary domain assumptions: \textbf{04:00--06:00} ($2\times$ loss weight) and \textbf{18:00--20:00} ($4\times$ loss weight).

\subsection{Ablation Analysis of Temporal Tokens}

To verify whether the model actively utilizes temporal tokens rather than ignoring them as sequence noise, an ablation experiment was performed.

The experiment starts from the input format drawn in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:hour-weights}}: each event enters the sequence as an hour token followed by a cell token, and errors count twice at 04h--05h and four times at 18h--19h.

\input{figures/fig-hour-weights}

The trained model is then left untouched and only its inputs are altered. \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:hour-corruptions}} shows the three corruptions: the same model is re-evaluated on the same subscribers with the hour tokens altered and the cell tokens left untouched.

\input{figures/fig-hour-corruptions}

Each corruption yields one bar of \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:hour-cost}}, which compares the resulting top-1 accuracies (\Dmob{}, 80\% context, 1,747 evaluated moments):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Uncorrupted reference} (44.7\%): correct hour tokens at every position.
  \item \textbf{Constant dummy tokens} (44.0\%, $-0.7\%$): every hour token replaced by \cid{<H12>}, which deletes the temporal information.
  \item \textbf{Shuffled temporal tokens} (41.7\%, $-3.0\%$): hour tokens shuffled across positions, so real hours are attached to the wrong events.
  \item \textbf{+12-hour label shift} (45.2\%, $+0.5\%$): every hour moved forward by 12 (\cid{<H07>} $\rightarrow$ \cid{<H19>}), which preserves the ordering but swaps day and night labels.
\end{enumerate}

\input{figures/fig-hour-cost}

Shuffling costs about $4\times$ as much as deleting the hours ($-3.0\%$ vs.\ $-0.7\%$ in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:hour-cost}}). If the model ignored the hour tokens, corrupting their ordering could not cost more than removing them altogether.

The small overall effect of the +12h shift is not indifference either, as \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:hour-shift}} shows by separating the upweighted hours from the rest: inside the upweighted windows, top-1 accuracy falls from 42.7\% to 39.7\%, while outside them it rises from 45.3\% to 47.1\%, so the two halves of the day exchanged their difficulty along with their labels. The model actively conditions its transition probabilities on the hour tokens, which validates Hypothesis 1.

\subsection{Evaluation of Weighted Loss and Targeted Sampling}

To test Hypothesis 2, three joint modifications were implemented:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unequal loss weighting}: 18:00--20:00 transitions received a $4\times$ loss multiplier and 04:00--06:00 transitions $2\times$.
  \item \textbf{Targeted sequence sampling}: training sequences containing a transition-window event were sampled $3\times$ more often, raising their share of the batches from 45.1\% to 71.2\%.
  \item \textbf{Full-trajectory evaluation protocol}: evaluation moved from scoring only the final 20\% of each trajectory to scoring every event step, which lowers the persistence baseline from 45.5\% to 43.1\%.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling}} and \textbf{Table~\ref{tab:after-sampling}} evaluate the effect of these adjustments on the 100 held-out subscribers.

\subsection{Analysis of Empirical Window Misalignment}

Each bar pair of \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling}} is one group of evaluated moments, with its size printed under the label; grey-blue is the persistence baseline and dark blue the model, on exactly the same moments. Within the 18:00--20:00 window (604 moments), the model gains +2.6\,\% (40.6\% vs.\ 37.9\%), and +2.3\,\% across both windows combined (732 moments; 45.9\% vs.\ 43.6\%): the first time in the project that a trained model beat the baseline on a segment chosen in advance. The 04:00--06:00 window rests on only 128 moments and its +0.8\,\% (71.1\% vs.\ 70.3\%) reads as ``not worse'' rather than as a win. The fourth pair, everything outside the windows, carries 7,893 moments, more than ten times the volume of the gain, and there the model loses $-2.6$\,\% (40.8\% vs.\ 43.4\%).

\textbf{Table~\ref{tab:after-sampling}} shows that this loss outside the windows is not an artifact of one cut. Each row is a context fraction (``30\%'' means the first 30\% of each day is given as history), and the distances before and after are model minus baseline on the non-target moments. At all seven cuts the model now trails the baseline, by $-1.1\%$ to $-2.8\%$, and top-3 and top-5 accuracy fall by 2.6\,\% to 4.8\,\%: the correct cell has not slipped from first to second place, it has left the model's top candidates entirely.

Loss weighting therefore reallocates the model's optimization focus toward the targeted intervals, a trade worth making only if those intervals are the hard ones. They were not: on full test trajectories, the persistence baseline scores 43.6\% \emph{inside} the guessed windows versus 43.4\% \emph{outside} them (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling}}). Early morning hours (04:00--06:00) are in fact highly regular across all user cohorts (baseline accuracy 49\%--64\%). As documented by Song et al.\ \textbf{\cite{song2010limits}}, nocturnal hours exhibit maximum trajectory regularity, whereas transition difficulty dips around midday and early evening. Globally fixed hours, applied uniformly across all subscribers, fail to account for individual temporal mobility variations. This motivates the behavioral stratification approach presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:retro-time}

This chapter validated the integration of explicit temporal tokens (\cid{<H00>}--\cid{<H23>}). Ablation analysis demonstrated that shuffling temporal tokens causes a 3.0\,\% accuracy drop (\textbf{Figures~\ref{fig:hour-cost} and~\ref{fig:hour-shift}}), confirming that model predictions depend on temporal sequence ordering.

Conversely, applying globally fixed hourly loss weighting (04:00--06:00 and 18:00--20:00) demonstrated that while loss weighting successfully forces local optimization (+2.6\,\% at 18:00--20:00 in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:targeted-sampling}}), applying fixed hours across all subscribers penalizes non-targeted intervals. Because temporal mobility patterns differ significantly across subscribers, effective transition loss weighting requires individual user cohort stratification, as detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}.

In hindsight, the two experiments of this chapter answer two different questions. The ablation shows that the model reads the hour, since corrupting its order costs more than deleting it. The sampling experiment shows that reading the hour is not enough when the emphasised hours are the wrong ones: the model beat the baseline on a segment chosen in advance (+2.6\,\% at 18:00--20:00), but trailed it at every one of the seven cuts outside the windows ($-1.1\%$ to $-2.8\%$), and the guessed windows were no harder than the rest of the day (43.6\% against 43.4\% for the baseline). The mechanism was right; the hours it was pointed at were not.
