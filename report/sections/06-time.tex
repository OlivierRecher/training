\chapter{Adding the time of day}
\label{ch:time}

\section{The goal}

Up to this point the model was given a bare list of antennas. Nothing in its
input said whether an event happened at four in the morning or at seven in the
evening, which is a strange omission for a mobility problem: the reason a person
changes antenna at all is usually that they are going somewhere, and going
somewhere happens at particular times of day.

So the question of this chapter is whether \emph{time of day} is the missing
signal, and more precisely whether a model can be pushed to expect a change of
antenna at the hours where a change is likely, instead of falling back on the one
strategy always available to it, repeating the last antenna it saw.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} All of them from
\Dmob{} (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): 400 people used for training and \textbf{100
different people}, never seen during training, used for grading. The one-line
rule scores 45.5\,\% on the last fifth of their day and 43.1\,\% when every
position of the day is graded, and the second half of this chapter switches to
the second of those two protocols.

\section{The clue: some people collapse at the end of the day}

The pointer came from looking at people one at a time. Scoring the same trained
model with progressively more history, most behave exactly as
Chapter~\ref{ch:user} predicts: their score is a trait, roughly constant whatever
they are given. But one group does something else entirely. Given \emph{more}
history, their score falls off a cliff, from thirty or forty per hundred down to
zero.

So the missing ingredient is not more history. It is knowing that the end of the
day is not the middle of it.

\section{How the time of day enters, and the two bets it carries}

Time enters in the form fixed in Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}: one symbol for the hour
placed before each event, always given to the model and never predicted by it.
Two distinct bets were made at once, and only one of the two survived the
chapter.

\begin{description}
  \item[Bet 1: the model can use the hour.] Given the hour of every past event
        and of the event to be named, the model can learn that some antennas are
        morning antennas and others evening ones.
  \item[Bet 2: some hours deserve more weight.] There are \emph{transition
        windows},\footnote{\textbf{Transition window}: a stretch of hours in which
        a mistake is made to count more, so that a wrong answer at 19h under a
        multiplier of four costs four times a wrong answer at 14h. No data is
        duplicated.} stretches of the day in which a person is more likely to move
        between the area they live in and the area they spend the day in. If
        mistakes there are made to count more, the training is forced to get them
        right rather than accepting them as the price of a good average.
\end{description}

Bet 2 requires knowing which hours those are, and at this stage I did not measure
them. I \emph{guessed} them, from the time boundaries the team's
home-and-activity classification already used: \textbf{04--06h} and
\textbf{18--20h}. That guess is the subject of the end of this chapter.

\section{Bet 1 holds: the model does read the hour}

Whether a model uses an input is not something to assume. It is something to test
by breaking the input and seeing whether the score notices.

\input{figures/fig-hour-ablation}

The argument is the difference between two of those numbers, and only that.
Replacing every hour by a constant, which removes the information completely,
costs 0.7 correct answers per hundred. Merely shuffling the hours, so that the
values are still real hours but attached to the wrong events, costs 3.0, four
times as much. If the hour symbol were simply occupying a slot in the sequence,
destroying its \emph{ordering} could not possibly cost more than deleting it
altogether. Bet 1 is established.

\section{Bet 2 half-holds: the hours can be won, at a price}

If the mechanism works, the obvious move is to push harder on it. Three changes
were made together.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An uneven weight.} The two windows are not comparable in size:
        18--20h carries four times as many records as 04--06h and touches 58\,\%
        of people against 39\,\%. Weighting them equally asks the training to
        work as hard on the sparse, noisy window as on the well-populated one, so
        18--20h was raised to a multiplier of four while 04--06h stayed at two.
  \item \textbf{Ask the question more often.}\footnote{\textbf{Sampler}: the
        component that chooses which slice of training data to show next. Making
        it \emph{weighted} means some slices are drawn more often. Distinct from a
        weighted mistake, which changes how much a wrong answer costs rather than
        how often the question is asked.} Training slices containing at least one
        in-window move were drawn three times more often than the rest, which
        raised the share of training batches containing such a move from 45.1\,\%
        to 71.2\,\%, without duplicating a single record.
  \item \textbf{Grade the whole day.} Until then only the last fifth of each test
        person's day was graded. From this point every position is graded, one at
        a time. This is strictly harder, because early positions have almost no
        history behind them, and it is why the one-line rule on these same people
        is quoted as 43.1\,\% on the full day against 45.5\,\% on the last fifth.
\end{itemize}

\section{What the table above says: right mechanism, wrong hours}

The gain and the cost are not contradictory. They are the same phenomenon seen
from opposite sides, and read together they are quite precise.

Weighting a chosen set of moments does exactly what it is designed to do: it buys
correct answers there and pays for them elsewhere. The mechanism was never in
question after the ablation. What was in question, and what I had never checked,
was whether the moments I had chosen were the hard ones.

They were not, and the measurement is unambiguous. On the full test day the
one-line rule scores 43.6\,\% \emph{inside} the guessed windows and 43.4\,\%
\emph{outside} them. A difference of two tenths. The global window 04--06h /
18--20h does not separate easy moments from hard moments at all, so every extra
unit of effort spent there was spent on moments no harder than average.

Worse, broken down by kind of user, 04--06h turns out to be \emph{easier} than
average for every single kind: the rule scores between 49 and 64\,\% inside it,
against 20 to 57\,\% on average. Song et al.\ had published exactly that in 2010,
their curve of hourly regularity being at its highest during the night and
dipping around midday and in the early evening \cite{song2010limits}. The window I
guessed is the one stretch of the day the literature already marked as easy, and
doubling the cost of a mistake there spent the training effort on moments the
rule already gets right.

The honest summary is therefore this. \textbf{The machinery worked, and I pointed
it at the wrong hours.} The 2.6 won at 18--20h was real, because 18--20h happens
to be genuinely difficult for one of the four kinds of user; the loss on
everything else was the cost of upweighting hours that were difficult for nobody.

That has an obvious consequence, and it is what Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} does. If
the right hours differ from one person to the next, the remedy is not to pick
better hours for everybody. It is to stop assuming everybody shares the same
hours.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-time}

The change I would flag is the one that looks purely technical. Moving from
grading only the last fifth of each test day to grading every position was the
right methodological choice, since the early positions have almost no history
behind them and leaving them out was quietly making the task easier than the
question deserved. But it also moved the one-line rule, on the very same people,
from 45.5\,\% to 43.1\,\%, which means the runs before the change and the runs
after it cannot be compared on absolute score at all, only on their respective
distances to the rule. A change of measurement is a change of ruler, and a change
of ruler invalidates every absolute comparison made across it. If I were running
the project again I would freeze the grading protocol before the first training
and treat any later change to it as opening a new series of results rather than
continuing the old one.
