\chapter{State of the art}
\label{ch:sota}

\section{The one published result this report leans on}
\label{sec:sota-ceiling}

Predicting where a person goes next is not a new question, and the literature on
it is large. What follows is deliberately not a survey of it, for two reasons.
The internship had eleven weeks and no access to a comparable corpus, so no
published number here could have been reproduced or contradicted; and the
argument the following chapters make does not need a survey. It needs one idea, stated once.

The idea is the one established by Song et al.\ \cite{song2010limits}: how well a
person's next location can be predicted is mostly a property of \emph{that
person}, not of the predictor. A user whose day is spent in one or two cells is
almost perfectly predictable; a user who moves between many is not, and no model
removes that difference. Predictability, in other words, is bounded per
individual by how spread out that individual's own history is.

Everything else this chapter says is measured on the corpus of
Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, not taken from anywhere.

\section{Why that makes the trivial rule the real competitor}

If predictability comes from repetition, then the rule \emph{``predict that the
next cell is the last one seen''}, one line of code, no parameters, no
training, is already right most of the time. On a raw day of this corpus it is
right \textbf{69.2\,\%} of the time (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}). That rule is the
\textbf{persistence baseline}, and it is the only yardstick used in this report.

Two conventions follow from it, and they are used everywhere after this page.

\paragraph{Every result is a gap, never an absolute.} The same trivial rule
scores 69.2\,\% on one of the files used here and 43.1\,\% on another, because
the users in them differ (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). An absolute accuracy
therefore describes the test set more than it describes the model. Only the
distance to the baseline measured on the same positions survives that, so that
is what every number in this report is.

\paragraph{The interesting quantity is per segment, not on average.} If
predictability is a property of the user, a population average hides the thing
worth reporting. Chapters~\ref{ch:user} and~\ref{ch:groups} measure the spread
directly: 22 points between behavioural profiles, against three tenths of a
point across an entire training-size ladder.
That decomposition is this report's main claim.

\section{What is left open, and this report asks}

Four things separate the result above from the task actually taken on here, and
they are the questions the remaining chapters answer.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A cell, not a place.} Published mobility work predicts the next
        \emph{place}: a home, a workplace, a clustered stay point. The target
        here is the next \cid{cell\_id}, which is noisier: 12.4\,\% of
        consecutive records change cell without anyone moving, because the
        handset re-attached to another cell of the same antenna
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}).
  \item \textbf{A population, not an individual.} A per-individual bound is
        estimated from a long history per person, whereas the question here is a
        within-day one on a corpus of single days, so what can be learned is a
        \emph{population} prior. How far that goes is measured in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:user}.
  \item \textbf{A language model rather than a purpose-built one.} Whether a
        general next-symbol predictor, fine-tuned, is worth its cost on this
        task is the question of Chapter~\ref{ch:tools} onward. Two colleagues
        were testing the other branch on the same corpus by prompting a frozen
        model (Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}); this report takes the fine-tuning one.
  \item \textbf{Where the difficulty lives.} Not the aggregate score but its
        decomposition across users, hours and behavioural profiles, in
        Chapters~\ref{ch:user} to~\ref{ch:groups}.
\end{enumerate}
