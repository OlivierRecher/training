\chapter{State of the art}
\label{ch:sota}

Eight published works matter to this report. Two of them changed the way I work
and are worth the first section on their own; three place the instrument, two are
the ancestry of my only substantial gain, and the last one marks the boundary
between the published task and the one I actually took on.

\section{How much room a predictor has}
\label{sec:sota-ceiling}

The most useful part of this literature is not the part that proposes predictors.
It is the part that measures how much room a predictor has before it even starts.
Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling-ladder} puts that measurement, and my own numbers, on one
scale.

\input{figures/fig-ceiling-ladder}

Song et al.\ took three months of mobile-phone records for 45{,}000 people,
treated each person's sequence of visited antennas as a stream of symbols, and
asked how much of the next position is already implied by everything that came
before \cite{song2010limits}. From that they derived a \emph{ceiling}, the best
score any predictor could ever reach, however clever: 93\,\%.

Ikanovic and Mollgaard then showed that most of the 93 is not knowledge about
anybody \cite{ikanovic2017alternative}. The quantity Song et al.\ bound answers
\emph{``where will this person be during the next time slot''}, and in most time
slots the answer is ``exactly where they are now''. Re-running the same machinery
on the question a predictor is actually useful for, \emph{``where will this person
go next''}, with the repetitions removed, the ceiling fell to 71\,\%. Roughly a
quarter of what looked like predictability was the passage of time rather than the
behaviour of a person. That is what names my competitor: if most of a high score
is people staying where they are, any honest claim has to be a distance to
``answer the last antenna seen'' rather than an absolute score.

Two further things in Song et al.\ bear directly on later chapters.

\textbf{The ceiling is the same for everybody, but the score is not.} The authors
expected heavy travellers to be harder to predict and were wrong: past a radius of
ten kilometres, the ceiling stops depending on distance travelled at all. Yet what
can be predicted from a person's visit frequencies alone is spread very widely
across the population and peaks near 30\,\%. A population average therefore hides
the thing worth reporting, which is why Chapters~\ref{ch:user}
and~\ref{ch:groups} measure the spread directly.

\textbf{And a curve published almost in passing, which cost me an iteration.}
Splitting the week into 168 hourly slots, the authors measure how often a person
is found in their most-visited place for that hour. The answer is close to 90\,\%
during the night, with clear low points around midday and between six and seven in
the evening. The hours at which people are hard to predict were published in 2010,
and they are not the small hours of the morning. Chapter~\ref{ch:time} spends its
whole length rediscovering that the expensive way.

\section{What the other six gave me}

Three of them place the instrument. Luca et al.\ survey the deep-learning branch
of human mobility \cite{luca2021survey}, and two of their observations are used
without modification here: giving a model the time of day is standard practice, so
the mechanism of Chapter~\ref{ch:time} is not an idea of mine, and scoring a
prediction at three depths, top-1, top-3 and top-5, is the convention of the field
rather than a choice of mine. The language-model work closest to this one reports
the same three depths \cite{qin2025mobllm}. The convention exists for a practical
reason: an operator that can prepare three antennas instead of one is served by a
correct answer in third place. The same survey notes that the better step-by-step
networks had to be extended with a mechanism for reaching back to a matching
moment in an earlier day. That reaching back is the native operation of the
architecture behind current language models,\footnote{\textbf{Transformer}: the
architecture behind current language models, in which every position of a sequence
can look directly at every earlier one.} which is one honest reason to reach for an
existing language model here rather than build a predictor from scratch.

Since 2023 the field has split into the same two branches the team I joined was
split into. Wang et al.\ leave the model's numbers alone and write a better
instruction \cite{wang2023llmmob}: that is the branch I did not take, and in
substance what Tiphain and Benjamin were doing on this corpus. Qin et al.\ change
the numbers instead, with the same cheap method used here, over six mobility
datasets \cite{qin2025mobllm}; of the eight, that is the work mine is closest to.

The last two are the ancestry of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}, and I found them only
after the fact. Pappalardo et al.\ establish that a population separates into
recognisable kinds of person rather than spreading smoothly along one scale of
mobility \cite{pappalardo2015returners}. Jeong, Leconte and Proutière supply the
mechanism: one person's history is too short to estimate that person's habits
from, so group people who resemble each other and predict one person's next
location partly from the others' \cite{jeong2016cluster}. My four kinds of user
are the same idea with the axis changed, from how far a person travels to at which
hours they change location.

\section{Predicting an antenna, not a place}

Everything above predicts a \emph{place}: a home, a workplace, a stopping point
obtained by grouping nearby records. My target is the next antenna, which is a
different object. The work that predicts antennas sits in the networking community
and asks the question for another purpose: Chen, Mériaux and Valentin predict
which antenna a phone will be handed over to,\footnote{\textbf{Handover}: the
moment the network moves an ongoing connection from one antenna to another,
normally because the new one offers a better signal.} from radio measurements, so
the network can prepare it \cite{chen2013nextcell}.

Predicting an antenna from a trace of antennas, with no radio measurements,
inherits a drawback from each side. In this corpus 12.4\,\% of back-to-back
records change antenna without the person having gone anywhere, because the phone
re-attached to another antenna of the same mast (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}), so part of
what my model is asked to predict is a decision of the network rather than of a
human being. And the human part of it is graded on antennas rather than on places,
which is a finer target than the published ceilings were ever estimated for.
