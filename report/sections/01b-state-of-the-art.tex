\chapter{State of the art}
\label{ch:sota}

Eight published works matter to this report, and this chapter says what each one
gives it. Two set the frame every later result is read in, three place the
instrument, two are the closest relatives of its one substantial gain, and one
marks the boundary between the published task and the task actually taken on
here.

\section{How predictable a person is, and why that is the first question}
\label{sec:sota-ceiling}

The literature on predicting where someone goes next is large, and the part of
it that matters most here is not the part that proposes predictors. It is the
part that measures how much room a predictor has before it starts.

Song et al.\ measured exactly that \cite{song2010limits}. From three months of
mobile-phone records for 45{,}000 people, they treated each person's sequence of
visited towers as a source of symbols and asked how much of the next position is
already implied by the order of everything that came before. From that quantity,
an entropy,\footnote{\textbf{Entropy}: a measure of how spread out and how
disordered a set of possibilities is. A user seen in two cells in a fixed
alternation has low entropy; one seen in forty in no particular order has high
entropy.} they derived a ceiling on the accuracy that \emph{any} predictor could
ever reach on that person, whatever its sophistication, and found that ceiling
narrowly peaked at 93\,\%.

Three things in that paper matter to this report, and the 93\,\% is the least of
them.

The first is that the ceiling turned out to be uniform. The authors state their
own expectation plainly, and it was wrong: they expected heavy travellers to be
harder to predict than people who never leave their neighbourhood, and found
instead that past a radius of ten kilometres the ceiling stops depending on
distance travelled at all. Someone covering hundreds of kilometres a week is
bounded no lower than someone whose whole life fits in a ten-kilometre circle.
Whatever separates an easy user from a hard one, it is not how much ground that
user covers.

The second is that a uniform ceiling does not imply a uniform score. The same
paper also computes what is predictable from a user's visit frequencies alone,
ignoring the order in which places were visited, and reports that this quantity
is spread very widely across the population and peaks near 30\,\%. The room is
therefore the same for everyone while what a predictor extracts from it is not,
and the variability lives in how much of the ceiling a given signal can reach.
That is the quantity Chapters~\ref{ch:user} and~\ref{ch:groups} measure on this
corpus, where it runs from 0 to 100\,\% depending on the user.

The third is a curve the paper publishes almost in passing, and not having read
it sooner cost me an iteration. Splitting the week into 168 hourly slots, they
measure how often a user is found in their most-visited location for that hour
of the week. The answer is about 70\,\% across the population, but close to
90\,\% during the night, when people are reliably at home, with clear minima
around midday and between six and seven in the evening. The hours at which
people are hard to predict were published in 2010, they are not the small hours,
and Chapter~\ref{ch:time} spends its length rediscovering that the expensive
way.

\section{The ceiling is mostly standing still}

A ceiling of 93\,\% sounds like an invitation, and the most useful thing I read
during the internship was the argument that it is not one.

Ikanovic and Mollgaard make that argument precisely \cite{ikanovic2017alternative}.
The quantity Song et al.\ bound answers the question \emph{``where will this
person be during the next time slot''}. In most time slots the answer is
``exactly where they are now'', because people do not move most of the time, so
most of the apparent 93\,\% is not knowledge about anybody's habits. It is the
fact that staying put is the normal case. The authors therefore re-ran the same
machinery on the question a predictor is actually useful for, \emph{``where will
this person go next''}, by removing the repetitions before measuring, and on GPS
traces from 604 people the ceiling fell from above 90\,\% to \textbf{71\,\%}.
Roughly a quarter of what looked like predictability was the passage of time
rather than the behaviour of a person.

This names my competitor, and it is why the whole report is written against one.
If most of a high score is people staying where they are, then the rule
\emph{``predict that the next cell is the last one seen''}, one line of code, no
parameters, no training, already collects most of it. On a raw day of this corpus
that rule is right \textbf{69.2\,\%} of the time (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}), which
is roughly where Song et al.'s hourly regularity sits and well below the 93\,\%
ceiling, and the distance between those two numbers is the entire working space
of this report. The rule is the \textbf{persistence baseline}, and it is the only
yardstick used here.

Two conventions follow, and they are used everywhere after this page.

\paragraph{Every result is a gap, never an absolute.} The same trivial rule
scores 69.2\,\% on one of the files used here and 43.1\,\% on another, because
the users in them differ (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). An absolute accuracy
therefore describes the test set more than it describes the model. Only the
distance to the baseline measured on the same positions survives that, so that
is what every number in this report is.

\paragraph{The interesting quantity is per segment, not on average.} If the
ceiling is uniform but the score is not, a population average hides the thing
worth reporting. Chapters~\ref{ch:user} and~\ref{ch:groups} measure the spread
directly: 22.4 points between behavioural profiles, against four tenths of a
point across an entire training-size ladder.
That decomposition is this report's main claim.

\section{What has been used to predict}

The predictors themselves I take from one place. Luca et al.\ survey the whole
deep-learning branch of human mobility, from recurrent
networks\footnote{\textbf{Recurrent network}: a neural network that reads a
sequence one element at a time while carrying a summary of what it has already
read, so a prediction can depend on more than the last item.} that read a
trajectory step by step to attention mechanisms that let a prediction reach back
to a matching moment in an earlier day \cite{luca2021survey}. Two points in it
bear on the chapters below.

Giving a model the time of day alongside the location has been standard practice
in that branch for years, so the mechanism of Chapter~\ref{ch:time} is not an
idea of mine; what Chapters~\ref{ch:time} and~\ref{ch:groups} add is the finding
that \emph{which} hours matter is a property of the user rather than of the
corpus. And the
attention that the better recurrent models had to be extended with is the native
operation of the architecture behind current language
models,\footnote{\textbf{Transformer}: the architecture behind current language
models, in which every position of a sequence can attend directly to every
earlier one.} which is one honest reason to reach for an existing language model
here instead of building a predictor from scratch.

\section{Prompting or fine-tuning}

Since 2023 the field has been reaching for those models, and it split into the
same two branches the team I joined was split into (Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}).

The first branch leaves the model's weights alone and writes a better
instruction. Wang et al.\ turn a user's recent stays into a text prompt,
separating the long-term history from the immediate context, and ask GPT-3.5 for
the next location \cite{wang2023llmmob}. They get results competitive with
purpose-built deep-learning predictors without training anything, and report
that the design of the prompt, not the choice of model, is what moves the score.
That is in substance the branch Tiphain and Benjamin were taking on this corpus.

The second branch changes the weights, and it is mine. Qin et al.\ fine-tune a
small open-source model with the same parameter-efficient method used here,
across six mobility datasets, and argue that a model adapted this way transfers
to a new corpus better than prompting a large commercial one does, at a fraction
of the running cost \cite{qin2025mobllm}. It is the closest published analogue
of what this report attempts.

\section{A population, split into kinds of user}

The step that produced this report's only substantial gain, grouping users by
\emph{when} they move and letting each group inform its own members
(Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}), has two ancestors I found only after the fact.

Pappalardo et al.\ establish the observation \cite{pappalardo2015returners}.
Looking at both mobile-phone and GPS data, they find that a population does not
spread smoothly along a single axis of mobility but separates into two
recognisable kinds of person: returners, who keep cycling between a small set of
habitual locations however far they travel, and explorers, whose set of visited
places keeps growing. The two behave differently enough that a phenomenon
spreading through the population moves at a different speed depending on which
kind carries it. The four behavioural profiles of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} are a
version of the same finding with the axis changed, since what separates mine is
not how far a user travels but at which hours they change site.

Jeong, Leconte and Prouti\`ere supply the mechanism \cite{jeong2016cluster}. Their
argument is that any single user's history is too short to estimate that user's
own transition behaviour from, so they cluster users and predict one user's next
location partly from the trajectories of those who resemble them. The property
that makes it more than a heuristic is that their method falls back to
per-user prediction when the similarities are not really present in the training
data, so clustering cannot make things worse. Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} does the
same thing in a different place: the group is a single token prepended to the
sequence, so one model serves every group and the grouping is a hint rather than
a partition, and it has to be recoverable from the user's past alone, which is
the constraint that chapter spends most of its length on.

\section{Predicting cells rather than places}

Everything above predicts a \emph{place}: a home, a workplace, a stay point
obtained by clustering nearby records together. The target here is the next
\cid{cell\_id}, which is a different object, and the work that predicts cells
rather than places sits in the networking community and asks the question for a
different purpose. Chen, M\'eriaux and Valentin predict which cell a handset will
hand over\footnote{\textbf{Handover}: the moment the network moves an ongoing
connection from one cell to another, normally because the new one now offers a
better signal.} to, from radio measurements, so that the network can prepare the
handover before it happens \cite{chen2013nextcell}. They have signal strengths
this corpus does not carry, and they do not need to know where the person is
going, only which antenna will serve them next.

Predicting a cell from a trace of cells, with no radio measurements, sits
between the two and inherits a drawback from each. 12.4\,\% of consecutive
records in this corpus change cell without the site the user is attached to
having really changed, because the handset re-attached to another cell of the
same antenna (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}), so part of what a model here is asked to
predict is a network decision rather than a human one. And the human part of it
is graded on cells rather than on places, which is a finer target than the
published ceilings were ever estimated for.
