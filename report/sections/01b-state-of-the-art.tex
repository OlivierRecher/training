\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{ch:sota}

Eight published studies form the theoretical and empirical baseline for this investigation, all of which are cited in the bibliography. Two foundational papers established the theoretical bounds of trajectory predictability and guided the methodology of this study. Among the remaining six works, three establish standard benchmarking protocols and evaluation metrics for mobility predictors, two provide the theoretical basis for user stratification, and one defines the conceptual boundary between spatial location prediction and cellular antenna handovers.

\section{Predictability Ceilings and Baseline Boundaries}
\label{sec:sota-ceiling}

The mobility literature provides critical bounds on theoretical predictability limits prior to model deployment. \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling-ladder}} places these bounds and the scores measured in this work on a single scale, the percentage of moments predicted correctly. The two upper grey bars are theoretical predictability ceilings published in the literature, the best score any predictor could reach; the two lower bars are measured on the evaluation datasets of this report, in blue for the persistence baseline and in green for the gain of the fine-tuned language model.

\input{figures/fig-ceiling-ladder}

Song et al.\ analyzed three months of mobile phone location records for 45,000 subscribers, modeling visited antenna sequences as discrete stochastic processes to quantify theoretical entropy bounds \textbf{\cite{song2010limits}}. From information-theoretic entropy measurements, they derived an upper bound of 93\% for overall location predictability, the first bar of \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling-ladder}}.

Subsequently, Ikanovic and Mollgaard demonstrated that a substantial portion of this 93\% ceiling stems from temporal stationary states rather than true mobility trajectory predictability \textbf{\cite{ikanovic2017alternative}}. The entropy formulation of Song et al.\ addresses the query \emph{``where will the user be during the next time interval''}, which defaults to the user's current position during stationary periods. By reformulating the target to non-stationary transition events (\emph{``where will the user move next''}, with stationary repetitions removed), the theoretical ceiling drops to 71\%; the dashed section of the second bar is the share lost to standing still. Approximately one quarter of apparent trajectory predictability is therefore attributable to stationary temporal residence.

The two lower bars show what this correction means in practice. On a raw trajectory file, the persistence baseline alone achieves 69.2\%, leaving virtually no margin below the 71\% ceiling. On active mobile subscribers who genuinely move, the same rule drops to 50.2\% without any change to its own definition (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}), and the fine-tuned model reaches 54.5\%. A raw day file and a filtered cohort of active subscribers therefore cannot be compared on absolute accuracy: rigorous model evaluation requires measuring performance gains relative to the persistence baseline rather than reporting uncalibrated absolute accuracy figures.

Two additional insights from Song et al.\ directly inform subsequent sections of this report \textbf{\cite{song2010limits}}:

\textbf{Constancy of Predictability Ceilings Across Travel Radii.} The predictability ceiling remains largely invariant to user travel distances beyond a radius of ten kilometers. However, localized predictability derived from individual visit frequencies varies widely across subscriber cohorts, peaking near 30\%. Consequently, aggregate population averages mask underlying subgroup variations, necessitating the individual user profiling detailed in Chapters~\ref{ch:user} and \ref{ch:groups}.

\textbf{Diurnal Regularity Patterns.} By partitioning weekly trajectories into 168 hourly slots, Song et al.\ observed that individual regularity reaches approximately 90\% during night hours, while exhibiting pronounced dips around midday and early evening (18h--20h) \textbf{\cite{song2010limits}}. This empirical distribution of temporal difficulty motivates the hourly feature integration evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:time}.

\section{Categorization of Literature Contributions}

The secondary literature informs the evaluation framework and model architecture utilized in this research. Luca et al.\ present a comprehensive survey of deep learning approaches for human mobility modeling \textbf{\cite{luca2021survey}}. Two standard conventions from their survey are adopted here: integrating explicit temporal representations (hour of day) into the model input, and evaluating prediction accuracy across multiple rank thresholds (top-1, top-3, and top-5). Parallel work by Qin et al.\ adopts identical multi-depth evaluation metrics \textbf{\cite{qin2025mobllm}}. Top-$k$ evaluation reflects practical deployment requirements, where network operators benefit from pre-allocating handover capacity across candidate top-ranked cell targets. Furthermore, mobility surveys highlight the importance of recurrent memory mechanisms to capture historical trajectory patterns, a capability inherently supported by Transformer-based attention mechanisms.

Recent developments in LLM-based mobility prediction diverge into two principal paradigms. Wang et al.\ utilize zero-shot and few-shot natural language prompting without modifying underlying model parameters \textbf{\cite{wang2023llmmob}}, an approach evaluated in parallel by team members Tiphain and Benjamin. Conversely, Qin et al.\ update model parameters directly using parameter-efficient fine-tuning across mobility datasets \textbf{\cite{qin2025mobllm}}, establishing the methodological foundation adopted in this report.

The theoretical framework for user cohort stratification in Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} originates from demographic mobility studies. Pappalardo et al.\ demonstrate that human populations partition into distinct mobility behavioral classes rather than conforming to a single continuous spectrum \textbf{\cite{pappalardo2015returners}}. Jeong et al.\ provide the algorithmic motivation: because individual historical traces are frequently sparse, grouping subscribers with similar mobility patterns enables collective parameter sharing and collaborative prediction \textbf{\cite{jeong2016cluster}}. The four user profiles established in Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} extend this concept by stratifying users according to temporal cell transition distributions.

\section{Comparison Between Antenna-Level and Location-Level Prediction}

The majority of existing literature focuses on predicting semantic \emph{locations} (e.g., home, workplace, or clustered points of interest). In contrast, the objective of this study is the prediction of specific cellular \emph{antennas}, presenting distinct domain-specific properties. Cellular antenna prediction is primarily addressed within mobile network engineering; for instance, Chen et al.\ predict physical antenna handovers using radio signal measurement logs to optimize network handover management \textbf{\cite{chen2013nextcell}}.

Predicting cellular antennas strictly from sequence traces without physical signal strength telemetry introduces specific challenges. In the evaluation dataset, 12.4\% of consecutive records exhibit cell identifier changes caused by inter-antenna handovers on the same base station mast without physical subscriber displacement (Chapter~\ref{ch:data}). Consequently, model evaluation involves predicting network handover decisions alongside physical human mobility. Furthermore, grading accuracy at the granular cell level represents a finer-grained target than spatial region clustering evaluated in theoretical predictability bounds.
