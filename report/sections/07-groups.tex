\chapter{Sorting people into four kinds of user}
\label{ch:groups}

\section{The goal}

Chapter~\ref{ch:time} ended on a specific failure: one pair of stretches of the
day, guessed in advance and applied to everybody, marks moments that are no harder
than average. The remedy is not ``pick better hours'' but ``stop assuming everybody
has the same hours''. Some people leave home at seven in the morning and are
settled by six in the evening; others do the opposite; a third kind barely moves at
all.

That turns into two questions. \emph{Can people be sorted into kinds of user
according to when they move, using only information that will still be available at
the moment a prediction is asked for?} That is a measurement problem, and the
answer is yes. \emph{And does telling the model which kind of user it is looking at
make it predict that person better?} That is a modelling problem, and the answer
was no until the kind of user stopped being a piece of text.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} From \Dmob{}
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): the four kinds of user are fitted on the \textbf{400
training people}, and every score is measured on the \textbf{100 held-out people},
who are different people and never trained on. Every position of their day is
graded, and on that protocol the one-line rule scores 43.1\,\%.

\section{A kind of user is a shape, and it must be readable from the past}

A kind of user here is not a label anybody assigned. It is a \emph{shape}. For each
person I compute how often their \emph{physical location} changes, separately over
four stretches of the day: night from 22h to 07h, morning from 07h to 12h, day from
12h to 18h, and evening from 18h to 22h. Those four numbers are the person's
profile. Four stretches rather than eight because each one has to hold enough
events to be estimated at all.

Two properties of this definition matter more than the sorting itself.
\textbf{Movement is measured on the mast, never on the antenna name}, because 12.4
out of every 100 back-to-back records change antenna name with no change of place
(Chapter~\ref{ch:data}); measuring on the raw name was tried first and separated
behaviour less well. And \textbf{the kind of user must be recoverable from the past
alone}, since at the moment a prediction is asked for only the person's history
exists. The profile is therefore computed on the history only, never on the whole
day, both during training and at grading time. Without that the idea would be
circular.

\section{The four kinds of user, and the hours each one finds hard}

The sorting is done by an automatic procedure\footnote{\textbf{Clustering}: an
automatic procedure that sorts items into groups by resemblance, with nobody
labelling anything. Each group is summarised by its average profile, and each item
is assigned to the nearest one.} handed each person's four numbers and asked for
four average profiles. \textbf{The number four was chosen rather than discovered},
and it is the one arbitrary parameter of the whole mechanism: I picked it because
four stretches of the day give four numbers per person, and because fewer than four
profiles merged the two mirror-image kinds into one.

\input{figures/fig-group-profiles}

Figure~\ref{fig:group-profiles} draws the four average profiles hour by hour, and
the four shapes are easy to tell apart by eye.

\begin{description}
  \item[G0, the stay-at-home user, 28\,\% of people.] Flat and low all day, about
        30 changes of location per 100 back-to-back records at every hour alike.
  \item[G1, the steady user, 35\,\%.] Also flat, but higher: about 45 changes per
        100 records from morning to evening. What characterises this person is the
        flatness, not the level.
  \item[G2, the evening-moving user, 14\,\%.] A calm morning, then a climb all
        afternoon to 84 changes per 100 records at seven in the evening. Somebody
        who goes home late, or goes out after work.
  \item[G3, the morning-moving user, 23\,\%.] The mirror image: 79 before noon,
        settling to 29 by evening. Somebody who commutes early and then stays put.
\end{description}

G2 and G3 are the reason a single global stretch of the day could never have
worked. They are near mirror images in time, they are 37\,\% of the population
between them, and any hour that is hard for one is easy for the other.

\input{figures/fig-group-difficulty}

Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty} carries the two results that make the sorting
usable at all: its panel (a) shows that the label predicts \emph{difficulty}, and
its panel (b) that the label can be read from a person's past rather than from
their whole day. Take the first. Knowing nothing whatever about a person except
which of the four kinds they fall into accounts for 69\,\% of the spread in how predictable people
are on the training side, and 72\,\% on the held-out side, with the profiles fitted
on the training side only. That scale runs from 0\,\%, where the label would tell
you no more than always guessing the population average, to 100\,\%, where it would
tell you each person's score exactly.

One rival explanation has to be acknowledged: the sheer length of somebody's day
also predicts how well they are scored, and length alone explains 48\,\% of that
same spread.\footnote{$\log n$: the logarithm of a person's number of records.
Using it rather than $n$ says that what matters is the \emph{order of magnitude} of
a history.} But the label still explains 38\,\% of what remains after length has
been removed, and the part of the profile that has no scale in it, how lopsided it
is between morning and evening, is barely related to length at all.

\subsection{Hours derived from the data, not guessed}

With profiles in hand, the stretches of the day to emphasise can be defined the way
they should have been from the start: as the run of consecutive hours where a given
kind of user's \emph{own} rule score falls furthest below that same kind's
\emph{own} average. In plain terms, the hours at which ``repeat the last antenna''
stops working \emph{for that kind of person}.

\input{tables/tab-group-windows}

Table~\ref{tab:group-windows} gives the stretches that procedure returns for each
kind of user, and its lower block is the measurement that justifies the whole
detour.
Applied to the full test day, the guessed global window separates the rule by two
tenths: 43.6\,\% inside it against 43.4\,\% outside. The per-kind hours separate it
by \textbf{27.4}: 19.0\,\% inside against 46.4\,\% outside. The first marks nothing
at all; the second marks moments that are twenty-seven correct answers per hundred
harder than average.

\section{Passing the kind of user to the model}

\subsection{The first attempt failed, and the check that located the failure}

Given all that, the first attempt was expected to work and did not. The kind of
user was written into the input as a dedicated symbol placed at the head of the
sequence, one shared symbol \cid{<G0>} to \cid{<G3>}, so that the model could look
back at it from every later position.\footnote{\textbf{Attention}: the mechanism by
which each position of the model looks at the others and decides how much weight to
give each. It may only look backwards, never at the future.} The run scored
41.3\,\% against a rule of 43.1\,\%, so 1.8 behind, no better than anything before
it.

The useful part is the check that followed, because it does not merely say ``it did
not work'' but says \emph{where}. Switching the symbol off entirely gives 41.1\,\%.
Feeding a deliberately \emph{wrong} kind of user gives 41.2\,\%. The correct one
gives 41.3\,\%. Three conditions within two tenths of each other means one thing
only: \textbf{the model was not reading the symbol at all}.

So the failure was not in the sorting, which had just been shown to separate the
rule by twenty-seven, and not in the hours, which an earlier test had shown the
model does use. It was in the \emph{channel}: one symbol at the head of a sequence
of up to five hundred is one symbol among many, and the model is free to dilute it
to nothing, because ignoring it cost almost nothing during training.

\input{figures/fig-adapter-routing}

Figure~\ref{fig:adapter-routing} draws the difference between the two ways of
telling the model which kind of user it is looking at: on the left the kind of
user as a symbol in the input, which the model may ignore, and on the right as a
switch that decides which trained numbers run at all.

\subsection{The fix, and the one part of it that mattered}

Four changes were made. Three are bookkeeping, and one changes the nature of the
mechanism.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Narrow the evening-moving user's stretch} from 16--21h to 18--21h,
        since most of a five-hour block carries no more movement than the rest of
        the day.
  \item \textbf{Work out the peak hours person by person, then average}, instead of
        from one histogram over everybody: because a handful of people own a large
        share of the records, the global histogram was really \emph{their} day
        rather than the population's.
  \item \textbf{Give each kind of user its own set of trained numbers.} The base
        model stays frozen and four independent sets of added numbers are trained,
        one per kind of user. The kind of user is no longer text the model may or
        may not attend to: it physically decides which numbers run. Each set is
        trained on every person of its kind and on nobody else, which turns four
        hundred people into four cross-trained
        populations\footnote{\textbf{Cross-training}: training one set of numbers
        on several people at once, so that people who behave alike inform each
        other instead of being learnt as independent sequences.} rather than four
        hundred independent fits. Somebody with sixty events now benefits from
        everybody whose day has the same shape.
  \item \textbf{Keep the derived hours.} The one component already measured to be
        correct was left untouched.
\end{enumerate}

Only the third could plausibly account for a large move, and that is what the
result suggests happened.

\subsection{The routing is the part that worked}

The run after those four changes reached \textbf{46.8\,\% against the same 43.1\,\%
rule, so 3.7 more correct answers per hundred}. It is the first run of the whole
project to clear ``repeat the last antenna'' on a complete test set rather than on
a hand-picked segment. The rule itself did not move, which is the point: same
people, same protocol, same architecture, and the only difference is which mistakes
the training was told to care about and which numbers were allowed to answer.

\input{figures/fig-group-gain}

Figure~\ref{fig:group-gain} shows where that 3.7 lands, one bar per kind of user.
\textbf{The gain ranks in exactly the reverse order of the rule, with no exception
among the four.} Read against Chapter~\ref{ch:user}, this is that chapter's finding
arriving from the other direction: there, difficulty was shown to live in the
person; here, the model is shown to earn its cost precisely where the trivial rule
fails, and to earn nothing where it already works.

This number is not the report's headline. It is what the configuration is worth on
four hundred training people, and it raises the next question: four hundred people
split across four kinds leaves the smallest kind with a few dozen members, which is
barely a profile at all. The configuration itself, hour symbols plus one set of
numbers per kind of user, is carried unchanged into Chapter~\ref{ch:scale}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:retro-groups}

This chapter answered both questions it opened with. People can be sorted into
kinds of user from when they change location, using only the part of the day a
predictor will actually have in hand, and the sorting is not decoration: knowing
nothing about somebody except which of the four kinds they fall into accounts for
72\,\% of the spread in how predictable they are, and the stretches of the day
derived per kind separate the one-line rule by 27.4 correct answers per hundred
where the guessed global stretch separated it by two tenths. And telling the model
which kind of user it is looking at does improve the prediction, but only once the
kind of user stopped being a symbol in the input and became a switch deciding
which trained numbers run. The result is the first run of the project to clear the
one-line rule on a complete test set: 46.8\,\% against 43.1\,\%, so 3.7 more
correct answers per hundred.

This is the chapter I would defend rather than qualify. Cross-training is the
largest success of the internship: instead of treating four hundred people as four
hundred independent sequences, it lets people who behave alike inform one another,
and it is the only change whose effect is clearly larger than the noise around it.
What makes it convincing is not the headline but the diagnosis that preceded it.
The first attempt failed, and the three-way check, correct symbol, wrong symbol
and no symbol at all, landed within two tenths of each other and said exactly
where: not in the sorting, not in the hours, but in the channel. Without that
check I would have concluded that the sorting was worthless and thrown away the
only idea in the report that worked.

Two things temper the headline rather than undo it. The 3.7 is the plain average
over four profiles counted once each, while an operator, who meets the four kinds
in their real proportions, would experience 3.3; given the choice again I would
lead with 3.3. And four sets of trained numbers are four times the trainable
capacity of one, so part of the gain may be capacity rather than the sorting. A
single set carrying as many trainable numbers as the four together would separate
the two in one run of half an hour, and that control was never run.
