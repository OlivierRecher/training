\chapter{Sorting people into four kinds of user}
\label{ch:groups}

\section{The goal}

Chapter~\ref{ch:time} ended on a specific failure. One pair of stretches of the
day, guessed in advance and applied to everybody, marks moments that are no
harder than average.

The remedy that failure implies is not ``pick better hours''. It is ``stop
assuming everybody has the same hours''. Some people leave home at seven in the
morning and are settled by six in the evening; others do the opposite; and a
third kind barely moves at all. A single stretch of the day cannot be the hard
one for all of them at once.

That turns into two questions, which this chapter answers together.

\emph{Can people be sorted into kinds of user according to when they move, using
only information that will still be available at the moment a prediction is
asked for?} That is a measurement problem, and the answer is yes.

\emph{And does telling the model which kind of user it is looking at make it
predict that person better?} That is a modelling problem, and the answer was no
until the kind of user stopped being a piece of text.

\paragraph{Where the numbers in this chapter come from.} From \Dmob{}
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): the four kinds of user are fitted on the \textbf{400
training people}, and every score is measured on the \textbf{100 held-out
people}, who are different people and are never trained on. Every position of
their day is graded, and on that protocol the one-line rule scores 43.1\,\%.

\section{What a kind of user is, and the constraint that makes it usable}

A kind of user here is not a label anybody assigned. It is a \emph{shape}.

For each person, I compute how often their \emph{physical location} changes,
separately over four stretches of the day: night from 22h to 07h, morning from
07h to 12h, day from 12h to 18h, and evening from 18h to 22h. That gives four
numbers per person, and those four numbers are the person's profile.

Four stretches were used rather than five, six or eight because each one has to
hold enough events to be estimated at all. With finer stretches, somebody with
three night-time events gets a night-time rate of 0 or 100\,\%, which is noise
dressed up as a profile. For the same reason each stretch is pulled towards that
person's own all-day rate in proportion to how little data it holds: a stretch
containing three events ends up most of the way towards the all-day rate, while
one containing two hundred is left almost untouched. A thin stretch cannot
dominate a profile that way.

Two properties of this definition matter more than the sorting itself.

\paragraph{Movement is measured on the mast, never on the antenna name.} As
established in Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, 12.4 out of every 100 back-to-back records
change antenna name with no change of place. Computing these profiles on the raw
antenna name would fold that 12.4 into ``mobility'' and blur exactly the
distinction the whole exercise exists to draw. Measuring on the raw name was in
fact tried first, and produced kinds of user that separated behaviour less well.

\paragraph{The kind of user must be recoverable from the past alone.} This is the
constraint that makes the idea usable rather than circular. At the moment a
prediction is asked for, only the person's history exists; their future, which is
the very thing being predicted, does not. So the profile is computed on the
history \emph{only}, never on the whole day, both during training and at grading
time, and the same computation is used on both sides. A variant in which the
profile is read from the whole day exists in the code, but only as a diagnostic
upper bound, and it is never used to produce a reported score.

Whether that constraint is affordable is an empirical question, and
Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty}(b) answers it. At the reference protocol, where
80\,\% of the day is given as history, the pipeline assigns the correct kind of
user to 92 of the 100 held-out people, unaided. Below roughly 60\,\% of the day
it collapses, to 61\,\% agreement at half a day and 44\,\% at 30\,\%, because
there is simply not enough history to tell a morning mover from an evening mover.

That is a real operational limit and it is stated here rather than averaged away:
the mechanism is worth using on somebody with most of a day behind them, and
worth nothing on somebody who has just appeared and offers an hour of history.

\section{The four kinds of user}

The sorting itself is done by an automatic procedure\footnote{\textbf{Clustering}:
an automatic procedure that sorts items into groups by resemblance, with nobody
labelling anything. Each group is summarised by its average profile, and each
item is assigned to the nearest one.} which is handed each person's four numbers
and asked for four average profiles. \textbf{The number four was chosen rather
than discovered}, and it is the one arbitrary parameter of the whole mechanism: I
picked it because four stretches of the day give four numbers per person, and
because fewer than four profiles merged the two mirror-image kinds described
below into one.

Fitted on the 400 training people, the four profiles are the ones shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:group-profiles}, and they are shapes in time rather than levels.

\input{figures/fig-group-profiles}

\begin{description}
  \item[G0, the stay-at-home user, 28\,\% of people.] Flat and low all day. This
        person changes location on roughly 30 out of every 100 back-to-back
        records, at every hour alike, peaking at only 35 at five in the
        afternoon. There is no moment of their day at which they are noticeably
        more likely to go somewhere.
  \item[G1, the steady user, 35\,\%.] Moves at a constant, \emph{moderate} rate
        from seven in the morning to eight in the evening. Moderate here means
        about 45 changes of location per 100 records, all day long: clearly above
        the 30 of the stay-at-home user, and far below the 80 that the next two
        kinds reach at their peak. What characterises this person is not the
        level but the flatness: no single hour stands out, except one late rise
        to 58.
  \item[G2, the evening-moving user, 14\,\%.] A calm morning at around 40 to 55,
        then a climb all afternoon to 84 changes per 100 records at seven in the
        evening. This is the shape of somebody who goes home late, or goes out
        after work.
  \item[G3, the morning-moving user, 23\,\%.] The mirror image: 79 changes per
        100 records before noon, then a settling down through the evening to 29.
        This is the shape of somebody who commutes early and then stays put.
\end{description}

G2 and G3 are the reason a single global stretch of the day could never have
worked. They are near mirror images of each other in time, they are 37\,\% of the
population between them, and any hour that is hard for one is easy for the other.
A rule that emphasises 18--20h for everybody helps the evening-moving user and
actively misleads the morning-moving user, for whom the evening is the moment
they have just settled down.

The sorting is also useful in a stronger sense than description: the label
predicts \emph{difficulty}. Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty}(a) shows that the
stay-at-home user changes location about a third of the time and ``repeat the
last antenna'' is right 56.3\,\% of the time for them, while the morning-moving
user changes location half again as often and the same rule is right only
17.8\,\% of the time. The ranking of the rule is exactly the inverse of the
ranking of mobility, which is what one would hope for and is worth verifying
rather than assuming.

\input{figures/fig-group-difficulty}

Quantitatively, knowing nothing whatever about a person except which of the four
kinds they fall into accounts for 69\,\% of the spread in how predictable people
are on the training side, and 72\,\% on the held-out side, with the profiles
fitted on the training side only. That scale runs from 0\,\%, where the label
would tell you no more than always guessing the population average, to 100\,\%,
where it would tell you each person's score exactly.

One rival explanation has to be acknowledged. The sheer length of somebody's day
also predicts how well they are scored, and length alone explains 48\,\% of that
same spread.\footnote{$\log n$: the logarithm of a person's number of records.
Using it rather than $n$ says that what matters is the \emph{order of magnitude}
of a history, so 10 to 100 records counts the same as 100 to 1{,}000.} But the
label still explains 38\,\% of what remains after length has been removed, and
the part of the profile that has no scale in it, namely how lopsided it is
between morning and evening, is barely related to length at all. The four kinds
of user are therefore not merely a proxy for ``long histories versus short
ones''.

\section{Hours derived from the data, not guessed}

With profiles in hand, the stretches of the day to emphasise can be defined the
way they should have been from the start: as the run of consecutive hours where a
given kind of user's \emph{own} rule score falls furthest below that same kind's
\emph{own} average. In plain terms, the hours at which ``repeat the last
antenna'' stops working \emph{for that kind of person}. The result is
Table~\ref{tab:group-windows}.

\input{tables/tab-group-windows}

Two features of that table are worth pausing on.

First, the stay-at-home user receives \emph{no} emphasised hours at all. A person
whose day is flat has no hours worth emphasising, and leaving every hour at its
ordinary cost is the correct answer for 28\,\% of the population. That is an
outcome a hand-chosen global window structurally cannot produce.

Second, the derived hours for the evening-moving user and the morning-moving user
are almost disjoint in time, which is the same mirror-image fact stated in the
language of the training procedure.

The lower block of the table is the measurement that justifies the whole detour.
Applied to the full test day, the guessed global window separates the rule by two
tenths: 43.6\,\% inside it against 43.4\,\% outside. The per-kind hours separate
it by \textbf{27.4}: 19.0\,\% inside against 46.4\,\% outside. The first marks
nothing at all; the second marks moments that are twenty-seven correct answers
per hundred harder than average. Whatever the model then does with that
emphasis, it is at least being aimed at the right places.

\section{The first attempt failed, and why}

Given all that, the first attempt was expected to work and did not.

The kind of user was written into the input as a dedicated symbol placed at the
head of the sequence, one shared symbol \cid{<G0>} to \cid{<G3>}, so that the
model could look back at it from every later position.\footnote{\textbf{Attention}:
the mechanism by which each position of the model looks at the others and decides
how much weight to give each. It may only look backwards, never at the future,
which is why a symbol placed first is visible to everything that follows it.} The
run scored 41.3\,\% against a rule of 43.1\,\%, so 1.8 behind, no better than
anything before it.

The useful part is the check that followed, because it does not merely say ``it
did not work'' but says \emph{where} it did not work. Switching the symbol off
entirely gives 41.1\,\%. Feeding a deliberately \emph{wrong} kind of user gives
41.2\,\%. The correct one gives 41.3\,\%. Three conditions within two tenths of
each other means one thing only: \textbf{the model was not reading the symbol at
all}. The same test on the hour symbols gave 41.3\,\% with real hours against
41.8\,\% with no hours whatsoever, so if anything slightly better without them.

So the failure was not in the sorting, which had just been shown to separate the
rule by twenty-seven. It was not in the hours, which an earlier test had shown
the model does use. It was in the \emph{channel}. One symbol at the head of a
sequence of up to five hundred is one symbol among many, and the model is free to
dilute it to nothing. On this task it did exactly that, because ignoring it cost
almost nothing during training.

\section{The fix, and which part of it mattered}

Four changes were made. Three are bookkeeping, and one changes the nature of the
mechanism.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Narrow the evening-moving user's stretch} from 16--21h to
        18--21h. A five-hour block is wide enough that most of it carries no more
        movement than the rest of the day, which spreads the emphasis over hours
        that did not deserve it.
  \item \textbf{Work out the peak hours person by person, then average}, instead
        of from one histogram over everybody. Because a handful of people own a
        large share of the records, as Chapter~\ref{ch:user} measured, the global
        histogram was really \emph{their} day rather than the population's.
  \item \textbf{Give each kind of user its own set of trained numbers.} The base
        model stays frozen and four independent sets of added numbers are
        trained, one per kind of user. The kind of user is no longer text the
        model may or may not attend to: it physically decides which numbers run.
        Each set is trained on every person of its kind and on nobody else, which
        is what turns four hundred people into four cross-trained
        populations\footnote{\textbf{Cross-training}: training one set of numbers
        on several people at once, so that people who behave alike inform each
        other instead of being learnt as independent sequences.} rather than four
        hundred independent fits. People who move at the same hours now shape the
        same numbers, and somebody with sixty events benefits from everybody
        whose day has the same shape. This is the structural change, and it is
        drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:adapter-routing}(b).
  \item \textbf{Keep the derived hours.} The one component already measured to be
        correct was left untouched.
\end{enumerate}

Only the third could plausibly account for a large move, and that is what the
result suggests happened.

\section{What I conclude: the routing is what worked}

The run after those four changes reached \textbf{46.8\,\% against the same
43.1\,\% rule, so 3.7 more correct answers per hundred}. It is the first run of
the whole project to clear ``repeat the last antenna'' on a complete test set
rather than on a hand-picked segment. The rule itself did not move, which is the
point: same people, same protocol, same architecture, and the only difference is
which mistakes the training was told to care about and which numbers were allowed
to answer.

Figure~\ref{fig:group-gain} shows where those correct answers came from, and the
pattern is the strongest single result in this report.

\input{figures/fig-group-gain}

\textbf{The gain ranks in exactly the reverse order of the rule, with no
exception among the four.} The stay-at-home user, whom repetition already
predicts 56.0\,\% of the time, gains 0.8. The morning-moving user, whom
repetition predicts 17.8\,\% of the time, gains 6.4. In between, the steady user
gains 2.3 and the evening-moving user 5.4.

I state that as an ordering on purpose. A correlation coefficient over four
points would land within a whisker of $-1$ for any inverse ranking whatsoever, so
quoting one here would dress the same observation up as a stronger measurement
than it is. The ordering itself is the claim, and it is exact.

Read against Chapter~\ref{ch:user}, this is that chapter's finding arriving from
the other direction. There, difficulty was shown to live in the person; here, the
model is shown to earn its cost precisely where the trivial rule fails, and to
earn nothing where the trivial rule already works. The two results are the same
statement about this problem, measured two different ways.

Two honest qualifications belong with the headline. The 3.7 is the plain average
over the four kinds of user, each counted once. Weighted by how many people each
kind actually contains, the gain is \textbf{3.3}, because 28\,\% of the
population sits in the kind that needed no model at all, and 3.3 is the figure an
operator would experience. And the whole mechanism depends on the kind of user
being detectable, which Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty}(b) showed collapses
below roughly 60\,\% of a day of history.

\textbf{Where this leaves the project.} This number is not the report's headline.
It is what the configuration is worth on four hundred training people, and it
raises the obvious next question: four hundred people split across four kinds
leaves the smallest kind with a few dozen members, which is barely a profile at
all. If the mechanism depends on the kinds being well populated, then population
size is the next lever to pull. The configuration itself, hour symbols plus one
set of numbers per kind of user, is carried unchanged into
Chapter~\ref{ch:scale}, where the same architecture on larger populations reaches
4.2 and 4.3 against their own rules. The mechanism is established here; its
ceiling is measured there.

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-groups}

This is the chapter I would defend rather than qualify. The move to
cross-training is the largest success of the internship, and the reason is worth
stating in one sentence: instead of treating four hundred people as four hundred
independent sequences, each with a handful of events to learn from, it lets
people who behave alike inform one another. That is what turned a model that had
spent five iterations imitating a one-line rule into one that beats it, and it is
the only change in the whole project whose effect is larger than the noise around
it.

Two qualifications I would attach to the headline number, both of which temper it
rather than undo it. The first is that 3.7 is the plain average over four
profiles counted once each, while the figure an operator would actually
experience is 3.3. Given the choice again I would lead with 3.3 and mention 3.7
as the per-profile average, not the other way round. Both are numbers about this
iteration and this set of four hundred people, which is why neither is the figure
quoted in the abstract.

The second is a control I did not run. Four sets of trained numbers is four times
the trainable capacity of one, so part of the gain may be capacity rather than
the sorting itself. Training a single set carrying as many trainable numbers as
the four together, on the same data, would have separated the two, and I would
run that comparison first if I picked the work up again. My expectation is that
most of the effect is the sorting, because the checks on the failed run showed
the model extracting nothing at all from the kind of user when it arrived as a
symbol rather than as a routing decision. But an expectation is not a
measurement.
