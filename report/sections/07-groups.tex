\chapter{Grouping users by when they move}
\label{ch:groups}

\section{The question}

Chapter~\ref{ch:time} ended on a specific failure: a single pair of transition
windows, guessed a priori and applied to everybody, marks positions that are no
harder than average. The remedy it implies is not ``pick better hours'' but
``stop assuming everybody shares the same hours''. That turns into two questions
that this chapter answers together.

\emph{Can users be grouped by when they move, using only information that will
still be available at prediction time?} And if so, \emph{does telling the model
which group a user belongs to make it predict them better?} The first question
is a measurement problem and the answer is yes. The second is a modelling
problem, and the answer was no until the group stopped being a piece of text.

\section{What a group is, and the constraint that makes it usable}

A behavioural group here is not a label anyone assigned. It is a shape: for each
user, the share of consecutive record pairs at which the \emph{physical site}
changes, computed separately over four bands of the day, night (22h--07h),
morning (07h--12h), day (12h--18h) and evening (18h--22h). Four broad bands were
used rather than five, six or eight because each band must keep enough events
per user to be estimated at all; with finer bands a user with three night-time
events gets a night-time rate of 0\,\% or 100\,\%, which is noise dressed as a
profile. For the same reason each band is drawn towards the user's global
rate: a band holding three events barely constrains anything, so it ends up
most of the way towards that user's all-day rate, while a band holding two
hundred is left almost untouched. A sparse band
cannot dominate a profile that way.

Two properties of this definition matter more than the clustering itself.

\paragraph{Movement is measured on the site root.} As established in
Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, 12.4\,\% of consecutive-event pairs are a change of
\cid{cell\_id} with no change of place. Computing these profiles on the raw
\cid{cell\_id} would fold that 12.4\,\% into ``mobility'' and blur exactly the
distinction the groups exist to draw. Measuring them on the raw
\cid{cell\_id} was in fact tried first, and produced groups that separated
behaviour less well.

\paragraph{A group must be recoverable from the past alone.} This is the
constraint that makes the whole idea usable rather than circular. At prediction
time only a user's history exists; their future, the very thing being
predicted, does not. So the group is computed on the \emph{context prefix}
only, never on the full sequence, both during training and at test time, and the
same computation is used on both sides. The ``oracle'' variant, where the group
is read from the whole sequence, exists only as a diagnostic upper bound and is
never used to produce a reported score.

Whether that constraint is affordable is an empirical question, answered in
Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty}(b): at the reference 80\,\% context the
pipeline assigns the correct group to 92 of the 100 held-out users, unaided, and
at 90\,\% context the figure is the same. Below roughly 60\,\% of the day it
collapses, 61\,\% agreement at half a day, 44\,\% at 30\,\%, because there is
simply not enough history to tell a morning mover from an evening mover. That is
a real operational limit, and it is stated here rather than averaged away: the
group mechanism is worth using on users with most of a day behind them, and is
worth nothing on a cold start, that is, on someone who has just appeared and
offers an hour of history instead of a day.

\section{The four profiles}

The grouping itself is done by clustering,\footnote{\textbf{Clustering}: an
automatic procedure that sorts items into groups by resemblance, with nobody
labelling anything. Each group is summarised by its average profile, and each
item is assigned to the nearest one.} which is handed each user's four-band
profile and asked for four average profiles. The number four was chosen rather than
discovered, and it is the one arbitrary parameter of the whole mechanism.
Fitted on the 400 training users of \smob, the groups are the ones shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:group-profiles}, and they are shapes in time rather than
levels.

\input{figures/fig-group-profiles}

\begin{description}
  \item[G0, the homebody (28\,\% of users).] Flat and low all day, peaking at
        35\,\% at 17h. Barely moves.
  \item[G1, the steady user (35\,\%).] Moves at a constant, moderate rate from
        07h to 20h, with a single late peak at 58\,\%.
  \item[G2, the evening mover (14\,\%).] A calm morning, then a climb all
        afternoon to 84\,\% at 19h.
  \item[G3, the morning mover (23\,\%).] The mirror image: 79\,\% before noon,
        then settling for the evening down to 29\,\%.
\end{description}

G2 and G3 are the reason a single global window could never have worked. They
are near mirror images of each other in time, they represent 37\,\% of the
population between them, and any hour that is hard for one is easy for the
other. A window that upweights 18h--20h for everybody helps G2 and actively
misleads G3, for whom the evening is the moment they have just settled down.

The grouping is also useful in a stronger sense than description:
Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty}(a) shows that the group label predicts
\emph{difficulty}. G0 changes site about a third of the time and ``repeat the
last cell'' is right 56.3\,\% of the time for them; G3 changes site half again as
often and the same rule is right only 17.8\,\% of the time. The ranking of
persistence is exactly the inverse of the ranking of mobility, which is what one
would hope for and is worth verifying rather than assuming.

\input{figures/fig-group-difficulty}

Quantitatively, knowing nothing whatever about a user except which of the four
profiles they fall into accounts for 69\,\% of the spread in per-user
predictability on the training users and 72\,\% on the held-out ones, with the
profiles fitted on the training side only. The scale runs from 0\,\%, where the
label would tell you no more than always guessing the population average, to
100\,\%, where it would tell you each user's score exactly. One rival
explanation has to be acknowledged: sequence length also predicts
predictability, and $\log
n$\footnote{$\log n$: the logarithm of a user's number of records. Using it rather than
$n$ says that what matters is the \emph{order of magnitude} of a history: 10
to 100 records counts the same as 100 to 1{,}000. Sequence lengths here span
more than a decade.} alone
explains 48\,\% of that spread. But the label still explains 38\,\% of what
remains after removing $\log n$, and the scale-free part of the profile, the
morning-minus-evening asymmetry of its shape, correlates with $\log n$ at only
$-0.28$. The groups are therefore not merely a proxy for ``long users versus
short users''.

\section{Windows derived, not guessed}

With profiles in hand, the transition windows can be defined the way they should
have been from the start: as the contiguous hours where a group's \emph{own}
persistence falls furthest below that group's \emph{own} average, that is,
where ``repeat the last cell'' stops working for that kind of user. The result
is Table~\ref{tab:group-windows}.

\input{tables/tab-group-windows}

Two features of that table are worth pausing on. First, G0 receives \emph{no}
window at all. A group whose day is flat has no hours worth upweighting, and a
uniform weight of 1.0 is the correct answer for 28\,\% of the population, an
outcome a hand-chosen global window structurally cannot produce. Second, the
derived windows for G2 and G3 are almost disjoint in time, which is the same
mirror-image fact stated in the language of the loss function.

The lower block of the table is the measurement that justifies the whole
detour. Applied to the full test sequences, the guessed global window separates
persistence by $+0.2$ percentage points, inside it the baseline scores
43.6\,\%, outside it 43.4\,\%. The per-group windows separate it by
$\mathbf{-27.4}$ percentage points: 19.0\,\% inside against 46.4\,\% outside. The
first marks nothing; the second marks positions that are twenty-seven points
harder than average. Whatever the model then does with that weighting, it is at
least being aimed at the right places.

\section{The first attempt failed, and the ablation says why}

Given all that, the first cross-training\footnote{\textbf{Cross-training}:
training one set of weights on several users at once, so that users who behave
alike inform each other instead of being learnt as independent
sequences.} run was expected to work and did not.
The group was encoded as a dedicated token prepended to the sequence, one
shared \cid{<Gk>} symbol at the head, so that causal
attention\footnote{\textbf{Attention}: the mechanism by which each position of
the model looks at the others and decides how much weight to give each.
\textbf{Causal} means it may look only backwards, never at the future.} lets it
condition every subsequent prediction, a token placed first being visible to
every position that follows it, and the run scored 41.3\,\% against a baseline of 43.1\,\%:
$-1.8\pt$, no better than anything before it.

The useful part is the ablation that followed, because it does not merely say
``it did not work'' but \emph{where} it did not work. Switching the group token
off entirely gives 41.1\,\%. Feeding a deliberately \emph{wrong} group gives
41.2\,\%. The correct group gives 41.3\,\%. Three conditions within
$0.2\pt$ of each other means one thing only: the model was not reading the group
at all. The same test on the hour tokens gave 41.3\,\% with real hours against
41.8\,\% with no hours whatsoever, if anything, slightly better without them.

So the failure was not in the groups, which had just been shown to separate
persistence by twenty-seven points, nor in the hours, which an earlier ablation
had shown the model does use. It was in the \emph{channel}. A single token at
the head of a sequence of up to five hundred tokens is one symbol among many;
attention is free to dilute it to nothing, and on this task it did, because
ignoring it costs almost no training loss.

replaced it.

\section{The fix, and which part of it was structural}

Four changes were made. Three are bookkeeping, and one changes the nature of the
mechanism.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Narrow G2's evening window} from 16--21h to 18--21h. A five-hour
        block is wide enough that most of it carries no more change than the rest
        of the day, which dilutes the upweighting across hours that did not
        deserve it.
  \item \textbf{Derive the peak hours per user, then average}, instead of from
        one global histogram. Because a handful of users own a large share of the
        records, the concentration measured in Chapter~\ref{ch:user}, the
        global histogram was really \emph{their} day rather than the
        population's.
  \item \textbf{Route each group to its own adapter.} The base model stays
        frozen and four independent LoRA adapters are trained, one per group. The
        group is no longer text that attention may or may not attend to: it
        physically decides which weights run. Each adapter is trained on every
        user of its group and on no one else, which is what turns four hundred
        users into four cross-trained populations rather than four hundred
        independent fits: people who move at the same hours now shape the same
        weights, and a user with sixty events benefits from everyone whose day
        has the same shape. This is the structural change, and it is drawn in
        Figure~\ref{fig:adapter-routing}(b).
  \item \textbf{Keep the per-group windows.} The one component already measured
        to be correct was left untouched.
\end{enumerate}

Only the third could plausibly account for a large move, and that is what the
result suggests happened.

\section{What the numbers said}

The run after those four changes reached \textbf{46.8\,\% against the same
43.1\,\% baseline: $+3.7\pt$}, the first run of the whole project to clear
``repeat the last cell'' on a full test set rather than on a hand-picked segment.
The baseline itself did not move, which is the point: same users, same protocol,
same architecture, and the only difference is what the loss was told to care
about and which weights were allowed to answer.

Figure~\ref{fig:group-gain} shows where those points came from, and the pattern
is the strongest single result in this report.

\input{figures/fig-group-gain}

The gain ranks in exactly the reverse order of the baseline, with no exception
among the four. G0, whom repetition already predicts 56.0\,\% of the time, gains
$+0.8\pt$; G3, whom repetition predicts 17.8\,\% of the time, gains $+6.4\pt$.
The ordering is the result, and I state it as an ordering on purpose: a
correlation coefficient over four points would land within a whisker of $-1$ for
any inverse ranking whatsoever, so quoting one here would dress the same
observation up as a stronger measurement than it is. Read against
Chapter~\ref{ch:user}, this is that chapter's finding arriving from the other
direction: difficulty lives in the user, and a model earns its cost precisely
where the trivial rule fails.

Two honest qualifications belong with that number. The $+3.7\pt$ is the
unweighted mean over the four groups, each counted once. Weighted by how many
users each group actually contains, the gain is $+3.3\pt$, because 28\,\% of the
population sits in the group that needed no model. And the whole mechanism
depends on the group being detectable, which Figure~\ref{fig:group-difficulty}(b)
showed collapses below roughly 60\,\% of a day of history.

One thing this number is not is the report's headline. It is what the
configuration is worth on four hundred training users, and the configuration
itself, hour tokens plus one adapter per behavioural group, is carried unchanged
into Chapter~\ref{ch:scale}, where the same architecture on larger populations
measures $+4.2$ and $+4.3\pt$ against their own baselines. The mechanism is
established here; its ceiling is measured there.

\section{Retrospective}
\label{sec:retro-groups}

This is the chapter I would defend rather than qualify. The move to
cross-training is the largest success of the internship, and the reason is worth
stating in one sentence: instead of treating four hundred users as four hundred
independent sequences, each with a handful of events to learn from, it lets
users who behave alike inform one another. That is what turned a model that had
spent five iterations imitating a one-line rule into one that beats it, and it
is the only intervention in the whole project whose effect is larger than the
noise around it.

Two qualifications I would attach to the headline number, both of which
temper it rather than undo it. The first is that $+3.7\pt$ is the unweighted
mean over the four profiles, each counted once. Weighted by how many users each
profile actually contains it is $+3.3\pt$, and that is the figure an operator
would experience, because 28\,\% of the population sits in the group that needed
no model at all. Given the choice again I would lead with $+3.3$ and mention
$+3.7$ as the per-profile mean, not the other way round. Both are numbers about
this iteration and this corpus of four hundred users, which is why neither is
the figure quoted in the abstract.

The second is a control I did not run. Four adapters is four times the trainable
capacity of one, so part of the gain may be capacity rather than the group
information itself. Training a single adapter carrying as many trainable
numbers as the four together, on the same data, would have separated the two,
and I would run that comparison
first if I picked the work up again. My expectation is that most of the effect
is the grouping, because the ablations of the failed run showed the model
extracting nothing at all from the group when it arrived as a token rather than
as a routing decision; but an expectation is not a measurement.
