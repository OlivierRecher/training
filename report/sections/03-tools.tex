\chapter{The tools}
\label{ch:tools}

\section{The goal}

The data was fixed; the instrument was not. Before any experiment could run, one
purely practical question had to be settled: \emph{given one free graphics card
and a few weeks, which model, which training method and which software are
actually feasible?}

This chapter answers it, and states the verdict on each choice with the benefit
of eleven iterations of hindsight. It is worth saying at the outset that this
toolchain was decided in the first week and never changed afterwards. What
changed over the internship was the data and the training signal, never the
instrument, and that is what makes the later comparisons between runs
meaningful.

Two things in that picture drive the rest of the chapter. The first is the

report is actually about, making some mistakes count more than others and

and the stack was chosen in the first week for that one property.

\section{How much of the model to change}
\label{sec:tools-peft}

A language model is a stack of very large tables of
numbers.\footnote{\textbf{Weights}, or \textbf{parameters}: the numbers inside
the model that turn an input into an output. ``Frozen'' means left exactly as
its original authors trained them.} Adapting one that somebody else has already
trained\footnote{\textbf{Pre-trained}: already trained, by someone else, on a
very large body of general text. Fine-tuning starts from those numbers rather
than from nothing, which is what makes it affordable at all.} can be done at
three very different costs, and what separates them is not accuracy. It is
memory. Figure~\ref{fig:lora} draws all three.

\input{figures/fig-lora}

\begin{description}
  \item[Full fine-tuning] modifies every number in the model. It has the highest
        ceiling in principle, and while it runs each number has to be held in the
        card's memory together with the bookkeeping needed to adjust it, about
        four numbers for every one, several times the size of the model itself.
  \item[LoRA] \cite{hu2022lora} freezes the original numbers entirely and adds a
        small set of new ones beside them. Only the new ones are ever modified,
        so that bookkeeping collapses. In the configuration used here, about 2
        numbers out of every 100 were ever allowed to change.
  \item[QLoRA] \cite{dettmers2023qlora} does the same and additionally stores the
        frozen numbers in a coarser form, four bits each instead of
        sixteen,\footnote{\textbf{4-bit precision}, or \emph{quantisation}:
        storing each number with sixteen possible values instead of the usual
        precision. These numbers are frozen and never updated, so it costs a
        little accuracy rather than correctness.} which roughly quarters the
        memory the frozen part occupies.
\end{description}

I chose QLoRA, and I chose it on hardware grounds rather than on expected
quality: it was the only one of the three under which my first candidate model
could be loaded at all on a free graphics card.

That choice held for the rest of the internship, and it is what made the volume
of experiments possible. Freezing the bulk of the model and training a small part
on a compressed copy of the rest is what turns a training run into something
that takes half an hour instead of half a day, and it is what made the per-group
routing of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} cheap enough to try at all.

\section{Which model, and why the small one won}
\label{sec:tools-model}

The first real run used \textbf{Mistral-7B} \cite{jiang2023mistral}, a general
purpose model with seven billion numbers in it.\footnote{``7B'' means seven
billion numbers. Qwen2.5-0.5B has five hundred million, fourteen times fewer.
The count is the model's capacity, and also, directly, how much memory it
occupies.} A deliberately small first experiment, about thirty minutes over
1.2\,\% of the training data, produced 16.87\,\% correct next-antenna answers.
As a signal that the pipeline worked end to end this was encouraging. A Llama
model tried at the same size did not even return well-formed antenna names.

Scaled up to a full run on 400 training people, however, Mistral-7B spent roughly
five and a half hours on the free card to reach a best score of 41.1\,\% and then
stopped improving. The same data given to \textbf{Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct}
\cite{qwen2025}, fourteen times smaller, ran forty-nine passes over the data in
about seventy-two minutes, and twenty-nine minutes once the fixes described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:first} were in. Mistral-7B was abandoned.

The reasoning behind that, stated at the time and still my view, is that the task
is narrow. Naming the next antenna out of 369 requires no knowledge of the world,
no reasoning in English and no ability to follow instructions. It requires
learning how one small geographical area is moved through. A seven-billion-number
model brings capacity the task cannot use, and charges for it in waiting time, by
a factor of roughly twenty-three per run, which over nine weeks is the difference
between thirty experiments and two.

\section{How a day is written down for the model}

The model cannot read a day; it reads symbols. Figure~\ref{fig:training-example}
shows exactly what one person's day looks like on disk, and then what the model
actually sees once it has been cut into symbols. Three choices are visible in
that picture, and each one is a single sentence with a large consequence.

\input{figures/fig-training-example}

\paragraph{One antenna, one symbol.} All 369 antenna names were added to the
model's vocabulary\footnote{\textbf{Vocabulary}: the fixed set of symbols a
language model can read and produce. \textbf{Tokenizer}: the component that cuts
an input into those symbols.} as symbols of their own. Left alone, the tokenizer
would have split \cid{BSOCHO1} into four meaningless fragments, and the model
would have had to spell an antenna correctly to be right, which is precisely the
failure the Llama attempt showed. With the 369 names added, naming the next
antenna becomes a choice among 369 known symbols instead of the spelling of a
string that may not even be a valid name.

\paragraph{One hour, one symbol.} From the sixth iteration each event is preceded
by a symbol for its hour of day, \cid{<H00>} to \cid{<H23>}. Writing the time out
in full would have cost four or five symbols per event instead of one, and since
the model may only look at 512 symbols at a time,\footnote{\textbf{Context
window}: the maximum number of symbols the model may look at in one go. Not the
same thing as the \emph{context fraction} of Chapter~\ref{ch:first}, which is a
property of the measurement.} that would have cut the visible history by about
two thirds. Hours rather than minutes or seconds, for the same reason:
twenty-four learnable symbols instead of 86{,}400 unlearnable ones. The hour is
always \emph{given} to the model and never predicted by it, which is legitimate
both here and in production, since the current time is always known when a
prediction is requested.

\paragraph{Only the antennas are graded.} Everything else in the line, the
opening boilerplate and the hour symbols, is excluded from the
score.\footnote{\textbf{Masking} a position means excluding it from the score:
the model still \emph{reads} the symbol but is neither rewarded nor penalised for
producing it.} The model is trained, and graded, only on the positions where an
antenna name has to be produced.

\section{How one training run works}

Figure~\ref{fig:training-loop} is the training procedure itself, and it is worth
following once before any result is read, because two of the report's conclusions
are about this loop rather than about the model.

\input{figures/fig-training-loop}

Two further mechanisms were added to this loop from the sixth iteration onward,
and later chapters depend on them.

\paragraph{Some mistakes are made to count more.} At step 3 the surprise measured
at a given position can be multiplied, according to the hour that position falls
in. A mistake at seven in the evening under a multiplier of four costs four times
as much as the same mistake at two in the afternoon. No data is duplicated; the
optimiser is simply told which mistakes matter.

\paragraph{Some slices are shown more often.} At step 1, slices containing an
interesting moment can be drawn three times more often than the rest. This is a
different lever from the previous one: it changes how often the question is
asked, not how much a wrong answer costs.

Both turned out to help only where the targeted positions were genuinely the hard
ones, which is the subject of Chapters~\ref{ch:time} and~\ref{ch:groups}.

\section{The machine: one free graphics card}

Every run reported in this document was trained on \textbf{Google
Colab}\footnote{\textbf{Google Colab}: a service that runs a Python notebook on a
graphics card rented in the browser, free for a few hours a day and liable to
disconnect. \textbf{T4} is the entry-level NVIDIA card it hands out.} on an
NVIDIA \textbf{T4}. That card carries 16\,GB of memory, and that single figure is
the constraint behind every choice in this chapter. The daily free quota is the
reason the experiment schedule is organised as one training per iteration rather
than as broad sweeps of settings.

On the analysis side, three standard libraries do work that never touches the
model.\footnote{\textbf{pandas}, \textbf{scikit-learn} and \textbf{scipy}: the
three standard Python libraries for, respectively, manipulating tables of data,
machine-learning utilities, and statistical tests.} They produce the balanced
split between training and test people, check that the two halves really do have
comparable histories, and run the resampling test
\cite{efron1993bootstrap}\footnote{\textbf{Bootstrap}: error bars obtained by
re-scoring the models on many random redraws of the test people, with no
assumption about the shape of the underlying distribution.} that eventually
decides the central question of Chapter~\ref{ch:scale}.

\section{What I reused from my colleagues}

Three pieces came from the team and are attributed as such.

The daily extracts and the selection of people who move and stay in the area came
out of Nino's own analysis tooling. He ran the extraction for me whenever I
needed a new regime, and those files are the data on which most of this report's
modelling rests. That help was substantial, and none of it was his own
assignment.

The move-or-stay framing, separating the moments where a person really goes
somewhere from the moments where nothing happens, is Tiphain and Benjamin's, and
it is the idea behind the filtering of Chapter~\ref{ch:data} and behind reporting
in-window and out-of-window scores separately. The technique of levelling every
person's record count, tested in Chapter~\ref{ch:scale}, is Tiphain's.

Their prompting approach, obtaining predictions from an untouched model through
better instructions, is the complementary branch of the same problem, and I did

\section{Looking back}
\label{sec:retro-tools}

With hindsight, one of these choices was solved by another. I adopted QLoRA
because storing the frozen numbers in four bits was the only way to load a
seven-billion-number model on a free card, and a week later I abandoned that
model for one fourteen times smaller, which would have fitted without the
compression. Keeping QLoRA afterwards was harmless, but it is a fair illustration
of engineering around a constraint instead of removing it: the cheaper answer was
to make the model smaller, not the numbers coarser.

The one setting I never revisited is how much of the model I allowed to move.
About 2 numbers in every 100 changed in every run of the internship, from four
hundred training people to twenty-one thousand, and that fraction was never
scaled with the data. Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} is where the omission bites.
