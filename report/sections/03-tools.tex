\chapter{Tools and techniques}
\label{ch:tools}

\section{The question}

The corpus was fixed; the instrument was not. Before any experiment could run,
one purely practical question had to be settled: \emph{given a free cloud
GPU\footnote{\textbf{GPU}: \emph{graphics processing unit}. The chip that
does the arithmetic of training. What limits a training run is almost never its
speed but its \emph{memory}: everything the run needs must fit on the card at
once, which is the whole subject of Section~\ref{sec:tools-peft}.} and a few
weeks, which fine-tuning regime, which software stack and which base model are
actually feasible?} This chapter answers it, and states the verdict on
each choice with the benefit of eleven iterations of hindsight. It is worth
saying at the outset that this toolchain was decided in the first week and did
not change afterwards. What changed over the internship was the data and the
training signal, never the instrument, which is what makes the later
comparisons between runs meaningful.

\section{State of the art in practice: how much of the model to move}
\label{sec:tools-peft}

This section is where the fine-tuning regime is chosen, and why. Adapting a
pre-trained
language model\footnote{\textbf{Pre-trained}: already trained, by someone
else, on a very large body of general text. Fine-tuning starts from those
weights rather than from nothing, which is what makes it affordable at all.} to
a new task can be done at three very different costs, and the difference between
them is not accuracy but memory. Figure~\ref{fig:lora} draws all three.

\input{figures/fig-lora}

\begin{description}
  \item[Full fine-tuning] updates all of the model's
        weights.\footnote{\textbf{Weights}: the numbers inside the model, several hundred million
here, that turn an input into an output; training is the act of adjusting
them. \textbf{Gradient}: for each weight, how much changing it would reduce
the error, recomputed every step. \textbf{Optimiser state}: two further
running statistics per weight. Full fine-tuning therefore holds about four
numbers per weight in memory, not one.} It has the highest ceiling
        in principle, and it requires holding the weights, their gradients and
        the optimiser state in GPU memory at once, several times the size of the
        model itself.
  \item[LoRA] (Low-Rank Adaptation, \cite{hu2022lora}) freezes the original
        weights entirely and inserts small trainable matrices alongside them.
        Only those matrices are updated, so the memory needed for gradients and
        optimiser state collapses by orders of magnitude. In the configuration
        used here roughly 2\,\% of the model was ever allowed to change.
  \item[QLoRA] (\cite{dettmers2023qlora}) additionally stores the frozen base
        weights in 4-bit precision,\footnote{\textbf{4-bit precision}, or \emph{quantisation}: storing each weight with
sixteen possible values instead of the usual five significant digits. Coarse,
but these weights are frozen and never updated, so it costs a little accuracy
rather than correctness; the adapters stay at full precision.} which roughly quarters the memory the \emph{frozen} part
        occupies, and trains LoRA adapters on top of that.
\end{description}

The decision was QLoRA, and it was made on hardware grounds rather than on
expected quality: it was the only regime under which the first candidate model
could be loaded at all on the free tier of a cloud GPU. Two practitioner
sources were used to fix the vocabulary and the trade-offs
\cite{heavybit2025,tewari2025}.

That choice held for the rest of the internship, and it is what made the volume
of experiments possible: freezing the base and training a small adapter on a
quantised copy of it is what turns a training run into something that fits in
half an hour rather than half a day, and it is what made the per-group adapter
routing of Chapter~\ref{ch:groups} cheap enough to try at all.

\section{Which software stack}

The stack is the Hugging Face ecosystem,\footnote{\textbf{Hugging Face}: the
company and open-source project that hosts most publicly available language
models and publishes the standard Python libraries for using them. ``The Hugging
Face ecosystem'' means those libraries, listed next, plus the online repository
the base model is downloaded from.} four libraries with four jobs:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Transformers} \cite{wolf2020transformers}: loads the base
        model and runs the training loop.
  \item \textbf{PEFT}\footnote{\textbf{PEFT}: \emph{Parameter-Efficient
        Fine-Tuning}. The library implementing LoRA and QLoRA: it inserts the
        small trainable matrices of Figure~\ref{fig:lora} into a frozen model and
        keeps track of which parameters are allowed to move.} \cite{peft2022}:
        the adapters, and the training of the embeddings of the tokens added to
        the vocabulary.
  \item \textbf{TRL}\footnote{\textbf{TRL}: the name expands to \emph{Transformer Reinforcement
Learning}, which is misleading here: no reinforcement learning is used. Only
the library's ordinary supervised path is, and what was taken from it is a
\emph{file format}, see Figure~\ref{fig:training-example}(a).} \cite{trl2020}: the
        conversational file format of the data files.
  \item \textbf{bitsandbytes}: the 4-bit storage of the frozen weights.
\end{itemize}

\input{figures/fig-training-example}

The stack was chosen in the first week for one property above all others:
complete freedom over the training configuration, including the loss
function\footnote{\textbf{Loss function}: the number training tries to make small. At each
scored position it measures how surprised the model was by the correct answer.
\emph{Weighting} the loss multiplies that number at chosen positions, so
mistakes there count for more.} itself.

That property is what made the second half of the internship possible. From the
sixth iteration onward the work depends on \emph{modifying the loss},
weighting individual target positions according to the hour of day they fall in
(Chapter~\ref{ch:time}), and later routing whole sequences to different adapters
according to the behavioural group of the user (Chapter~\ref{ch:groups}).
Neither is expressible by configuration; both require reaching into the training
step. The one piece of TRL that survived to the end is therefore its data
format, each user-day stored as a short conversation, with a system message
describing the task and an assistant message carrying the trajectory, exactly
the record printed in Figure~\ref{fig:training-example}(a), while the training
loop itself became a customised one.

\section{Which base model}
\label{sec:tools-model}

The first real run used \textbf{Mistral-7B} \cite{jiang2023mistral}, a
seven-billion-parameter\footnote{\textbf{Parameter}: a synonym for
\emph{weight}, used when counting them. ``7B'' means seven billion of them;
Qwen2.5-0.5B has five hundred million, fourteen times fewer. The count is the
model's capacity, and also, directly, its memory footprint.} general-purpose
model, loaded in 4-bit. A first, deliberately small experiment, one hundred
optimiser steps,\footnote{\textbf{Optimiser step}: one adjustment of the
weights, made after reading a small batch of examples. An \textbf{epoch} is one
full pass over the whole training set, and therefore contains many steps.} about
thirty minutes, covering 1.2\,\% of the training data, produced 16.87\,\%
next-cell accuracy, with the training loss falling from 0.902 to 0.583. As a
signal that the pipeline worked end to end this was encouraging. A Llama model
tried at the same scale did not even return well-formed \cid{cell\_id} values.

Scaled up to a full run on 500 users, however, Mistral-7B spent roughly five and
a half hours on a Colab T4 to reach a best validation accuracy of 41.1\,\% and
then stopped improving. The same corpus given to \textbf{Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct}
\cite{qwen2025}, fourteen times smaller, ran forty-nine epochs in about
seventy-two minutes and, after the harness fixes described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:first}, twenty-nine minutes. Mistral-7B was abandoned.

The reasoning behind that, stated at the time and still my view, is that the
task is narrow. Predicting the next cell in a sequence drawn from a 369-symbol
vocabulary requires no world knowledge, no reasoning in natural language and no
instruction following; it requires learning the transition structure of one
small geographical area. A seven-billion-parameter model brings capacity that
the task cannot use, and charges for it in wall-clock time, roughly a factor
of twenty-three per run, which over eleven weeks is the difference between
thirty experiments and two.

\section{How the trajectory is represented}

Three representation choices did most of the work, and each is a one-line
statement with a large consequence.

\paragraph{One cell, one token.} All 369 \cid{cell\_id} values were added to the
tokenizer\footnote{\textbf{Tokenizer}: the component that cuts an input into the symbols the
model reads. Left alone it would split \cid{BSOCHO1} into four meaningless
fragments, and the model would have to spell a cell correctly to be right;
adding the 369 identifiers as symbols of their own removes that.} as dedicated tokens, so that a cell is a single symbol
rather than a handful of sub-word fragments. Predicting the next cell then becomes a choice
among 369 known symbols rather than the generation of a string that may or may
not be a valid identifier, which is precisely the failure the Llama attempt
exhibited. The embeddings\footnote{\textbf{Embedding}: the vector of numbers the model associates with a
token, its internal representation of that symbol. The 369 cell tokens start
random and must learn theirs, which is why they are trained alongside the
adapters.} of these new tokens are trained alongside the
adapters.

\paragraph{One hour, one token.} From the sixth iteration each event is preceded
by a dedicated token for its hour of day, \cid{<H00>} to \cid{<H23>}. The
alternative, writing the timestamp out in text, would have cost several
tokens per event and eaten the context budget\footnote{\textbf{Context window}: the maximum number of tokens the model may look at
in one go, 512 here. Each event already costs two, one hour and one cell, so
writing a timestamp out at four or five tokens per event would have cut the
visible history by roughly two thirds. Not the \emph{context fraction} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:first}, which is a property of the measurement.} that the
trajectory itself needs.
Hour granularity, rather than minute or second, was chosen for that reason:
twenty-four learnable symbols against an unlearnable vocabulary of 86{,}400.
The hour is always \emph{given}, never predicted, which is legitimate both
offline and in production: the current time is always known when a prediction is
requested.

\paragraph{The loss looks only at the cells.} The prefix, the system message and
the hour tokens are masked out of the loss.\footnote{\textbf{Masking} a position
means excluding it from the score: the model still \emph{reads} the token but is
neither rewarded nor penalised for predicting it.
Figure~\ref{fig:training-example}(b) shows which positions survive the mask.} The model is scored, and trained,
only on the positions where a \cid{cell\_id} has to be produced.

\section{Training discipline}

Two mechanisms deserve to be named because later chapters depend on them.

\paragraph{Best-checkpoint selection.} Validation
accuracy\footnote{\textbf{Validation set}: training users held back and used \emph{during}
training to decide when to stop, as distinct from the \textbf{test set}, read
once at the end. \textbf{Checkpoint}: a saved copy of the weights at the
end of one epoch; a twenty-epoch run produces twenty of them, and which one is
read is a decision.} on this task peaks early,
typically between epoch four and epoch nine, and then declines for tens of
epochs while the training loss keeps falling. Keeping the checkpoint
with the best validation score, instead of the one at the end of training, was
worth $+15.7\pt$ of test top-1 on its own and cut GPU time by 60\,\%
(Chapter~\ref{ch:first}). It is the single largest one-step improvement of the
whole internship, and it is not a modelling idea.

\paragraph{Weighted loss and weighted sampling.} From the sixth iteration
onward, individual target positions could be given a loss multiplier according
to the hour they fall in, and training windows containing such a position could
be drawn three times more often than the rest. Both are ways of telling the
optimiser \emph{which} mistakes matter, without duplicating any data. They are
the mechanism behind Chapters~\ref{ch:time} and~\ref{ch:groups}, and both turned
out to help only where the targeted positions were genuinely the hard ones,
which is the subject of those chapters.

\section{Compute, and analysis}

All reported runs were trained on Google Colab,\footnote{\textbf{Google Colab}: a service that runs a Python notebook on a rented
GPU in the browser, free for a few GPU-hours a day and liable to disconnect.
\textbf{T4} and \textbf{L4} are the entry-level NVIDIA cards it hands out,
with 16 and 24\,GB of memory: that figure is the constraint behind every
choice in this chapter.} on a T4 or L4 GPU, under the constraint of
a daily free quota, which is why the experiment schedule is organised as ``one
training per iteration'' rather than as sweeps.

On the analysis side, pandas\footnote{\textbf{pandas}, \textbf{scikit-learn} and
\textbf{scipy}: the three standard Python libraries for, respectively,
manipulating tables of data, machine-learning utilities, and statistical tests.
None of them does any modelling here; they prepare and check the data.} and
scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} produce the stratified user split, scipy
checks that the two halves have comparable length distributions, and a
\emph{paired} bootstrap\footnote{\textbf{Bootstrap}: error bars without any distributional assumption. Draw
100 users at random \emph{with replacement} from the 100 test users, score
both models on that draw, note the difference; repeat two thousand times; the
middle 95\,\% of those differences is the confidence interval. \emph{Paired}
means both models always see the same draw, so a lucky sample of easy users
cancels out. An interval containing zero means the two are indistinguishable.}
over the test users \cite{efron1993bootstrap} is what eventually decided the
central question of Chapter~\ref{ch:scale}: resampling the same users for two
models, so that the luck of the test draw cancels and only the difference
between the models remains.

\section{What I reused from my colleagues}

Three pieces came from the team and are attributed as such. The per-day exports
and the mobile, non-absent user selection came out of Nino's own analysis
tooling: he ran the extraction for me whenever I needed a new regime, and those
files are the data on which most of this report's modelling rests. That help was
substantial, and it is worth saying that none of it was his own task. The move-or-stay
framing, separating the steps where a user really goes somewhere from the
steps where nothing happens, is Tiphain and Benjamin's, and it is the idea
behind the filtering of Chapter~\ref{ch:data} and behind reporting in-window and
out-of-window accuracy separately. The record-levelling technique tested in
Chapter~\ref{ch:scale} is Tiphain's. Her prompting approach, obtaining
predictions from a frozen model through better instructions, is the
complementary branch of the same problem, and I did not take it: this internship
is the fine-tuning branch, and the two answer different questions rather than
competing.

\section{What I did next, and why}

With the instrument fixed, QLoRA adapters on a small base model, a
Hugging Face training loop I could modify, dedicated tokens for cells and hours,
and best-checkpoint selection, the first real experiments could run. The
question they had to answer was the crudest possible version of the project's
question: does a fine-tuned model beat ``repeat the last cell'' at all? The
answer, for four iterations, was no.

\section{Retrospective}
\label{sec:retro-tools}

The change I would highlight from this chapter is not a modelling idea at all.
Evaluating the \emph{best} checkpoint instead of the last one was one of the
first things I put in place, and it was worth $+15.7\pt$ of test top-1 on its
own, more than anything that came after it. Doing it early was the right
call, and not only for the points it bought: from that moment on, every number
in the project is the best score the training actually reached, rather than an
artefact of wherever the run happened to stop. Two runs are always compared at
their respective peaks, which is what turned the training harness into a
measuring instrument rather than a source of noise. It also removed forty
wasted epochs per run, which on a daily GPU quota is the difference between one
experiment a week and several.

The general lesson I take from it is about the order in which to look. On a task
where the honest yardstick is a one-line heuristic, the first place to search
for points is the measurement and the harness, not the architecture. Verifying
that a run is being read correctly cost me a day; the modelling ideas that
followed cost weeks each and moved the score by less.
